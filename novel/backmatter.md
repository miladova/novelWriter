% AUTHOR'S NOTE

This is a novel, and almost everyone in it is invented. The city is not.

The Rome of this book is the Rome of Trajan: a city of roughly a million people, of whom perhaps a third were slaves or freed people; a city of insulae six and seven storeys high, of narrow streets and open sewers and the constant noise of carts, of a grain supply that could starve it in a fortnight if the ships from Egypt were late. It was a city with a Senate that had lost most of its power and all of its illusions, and a bureaucracy of imperial freedmen who had never held office and ran everything. It was a city where a woman like Cassia — a freedwoman with money, a household, and no husband — could own property, make contracts, lend at interest, and be received by the great, and could still be reminded at any moment, by any man, exactly what she had been.

The procedures in the novel are as close as I could make them. A charge of parricide was tried before the Senate, sitting as a court, with a consul presiding; the prosecutor and the defence spoke; witnesses were examined; the verdict was delivered by the house and the penalty by the emperor's discretion. The *poena cullei* — the sack, the serpent, the water — was a real punishment for the murder of a parent, and it had been on the statute book for centuries, and men as sophisticated as Pliny still discussed it seriously. The *delatores*, the informers who brought prosecutions for a share of the condemned man's property, were the plague of the age, and every senator knew that a rival's ambition could arrive at his door in the shape of a clerk with a tablet.

The law of status is the quiet engine of the book. A slave had no name of his own that the law recognised, no property, no marriage, no children who belonged to him. A freedman took the name of the man who had freed him and wore it for the rest of his life, and his children after him. A child born to a slave woman belonged to her master, and could be sold, given away, or exposed at the door, and none of this was murder or kidnapping or anything at all. What happens to Cassia at Tibur happened to women constantly, and the men who did it were not villains by the standards of their world. That is the horror of it, and I have tried not to soften it.

A few liberties, declared honestly. The villa at Tibur, the Sulpicii, the banker Sittius, the informer Pactus, the clerk Primus, Psyche, the potter and his son, and Cassia herself are all invented. The events of 88 are invented. The emperor's household in the novel is drawn from what is known of Trajan's court and Plotina's influence, and no more than that; the emperor's secretary is a fiction of a real type. Nolus's grave is invented; the ground outside the Porta Esquilina where the poor were buried is not. The names of the months, the days of the festival, and the counting of the Kalends have been checked as carefully as I could manage, and if a date is wrong it is my error and not the calendar's.

The book's central question is not who killed Marcus Sulpicius Corvinus. It is what a woman does with the twenty-four years she is given after everything has been taken from her. Cassia's answer — the house, the accounts, the clients, the garden, the three Graces in the alcove, the door with the green paint — is not a heroic answer. It is the answer most people give, in one form or another, and I have come to think it is the better for that.

I owe thanks to the staff of several libraries, to the translators of Pliny's letters and Juvenal's satires, to the epigraphers whose work on the *columbaria* of the great households made the household in this book possible, and to the friends who read the manuscript in pieces and told me, kindly and repeatedly, where it sagged.

Any errors are mine. The pigeons are Nolus's.

% A GLOSSARY OF TERMS

**Amphora** — a tall two-handled clay jar for wine, oil, or fish sauce. Made in millions, stacked in warehouses, and broken up for rubble when empty.

**Annona** — the grain supply of the city, and the department that ran it. A prefect of the annona was a senior equestrian official; a delay in the Egyptian fleet could empty the bakeries in a week.

**A rationibus** — literally "for accounts": the imperial freedman in charge of the emperor's finances. One of the most powerful men in Rome, and, by law, a nobody.

**Atrium** — the main hall of a Roman house, opening off the street, where clients were received and the masks of the ancestors were displayed.

**Augur** — a member of the college of augurs, who took the auspices. An augurate was a great honour and, for a man like Corvinus, the whole of his public identity.

**Caldarium** — the hot room of a bath complex.

**Client** — a man who attended a patron in the morning, accompanied him in the forum, and voted as he was told, in return for legal help, loans, and a dinner. The morning *salutatio* was a performance of rank, and the size of a man's crowd was the measure of his standing.

**Cognomen** — the third name of a Roman citizen, often a family nickname: Corvinus, "the raven."

**Consular** — a man who had held the consulship. The senior rank in the Senate, and the one that gave its holder the right to speak first.

**Delator** — an informer who brought a prosecution in the hope of a share of the condemned man's estate. Loathed, feared, and employed.

**Denarius** — a silver coin, roughly a day's wage for a skilled workman. A sestertius was a quarter of a denarius; a hundred thousand sesterces was a comfortable fortune.

**Eques** — a knight, the second rank of Roman society, below the Senate and above everyone else. Knights ran the treasury, the fleets, and the banks.

**Freedman, freedwoman** — a slave who had been manumitted. Free before the law, a citizen if the manumission was formal, and never quite free of the stain. A freedman took his patron's name; his children were born free and unmarked.

**Insula** — a block of flats, let floor by floor, built high and badly, and burned or fell down with depressing regularity.

**Kalends, Nones, Ides** — the three anchor days of the Roman month. The Kalends was the first; the Nones fell on the fifth or seventh; the Ides on the thirteenth or fifteenth. Days were counted backwards from the next anchor, inclusively, so that the sixteenth day before the Kalends of January is the seventeenth of December.

**Lictor** — a magistrate's attendant, carrying the fasces, who cleared the way and carried out arrests.

**Manumission** — the formal freeing of a slave, by the rod (*vindicta*), by will, or by census. The master's hold on his slaves was called *dominica potestas*; over his children it was *patria potestas*, and the two were spoken of in the same breath.

**Patron** — the senior partner in the relationship described under *client*. Also the term a freedman used for the man who had freed him, for life.

**Peculium** — the small fund a master allowed a slave to keep and trade with. Legally the master's; in practice the seed of a slave's purchase price, and of his freedom.

**Poena cullei** — "the punishment of the sack": the murderer of a parent was sewn into a leather sack with a dog, a cock, a snake, and a monkey, and thrown into deep water. An ancient and savage penalty, still on the books under the emperors, and debated by jurists with perfect calm.

**Praetor** — a senior magistrate, chiefly concerned with the courts. A praetor's widow is a woman of standing and no money.

**Praefectus annonae** — the prefect of the grain supply, who commanded the harbour police and the river barges and answered for the city's bread.

**Proconsul** — the governor of a province, usually an ex-consul, usually for a year. The Asian command was one of the two or three richest postings in the empire.

**Sestertius** — the standard unit of reckoning: a small brass coin. Fortunes were counted in hundreds of thousands of them; a legionary's annual pay was twelve hundred.

**Sigillum** — a small figurine, usually of clay, given at the Saturnalia. The festival of the sigilla was the *Sigillaria*, the last two days of the holiday.

**Tablinum** — the study or record room of a Roman house, opening off the atrium. Where business was done, letters written, and money counted.

**Triclinium** — the dining room, named for its three couches.

**Vigiles** — the night watch: seven cohorts of freedmen, stationed across the city, whose duties were firefighting, night patrol, and, when required, rough justice.

% A NOTE ON THE SEQUENCE

The novel opens in December of the year 111, and the trial at its centre takes place in the following summer, in the year in which Gaius Vibidius Nestor turns twenty-four.

The dates in the text are given the Roman way, as the characters would have given them: by the consuls of the year, by the day counted back from the Kalends. For the reader who wants the plain arithmetic: the events at Tibur took place in September and October of 88, in the last years of Domitian; the novel takes place twenty-four years later, in the last years of Trajan; and the epilogue carries the story to the Saturnalia of 112 and beyond it, briefly, to the end.
