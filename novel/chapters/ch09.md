The dinner was Cassia's idea, and it was a dangerous one, and Tertulla told her so twice before the guests arrived.

"You are inviting a man into your house," she said, "who is trying to destroy the father of your child, who has threatened your household, and who has already been paid by someone we do not know. And you are giving him the best fish."

"I am giving him the best fish because the best fish costs money, and a man who is given the best fish understands that he is being given something." Cassia checked the arrangement of the couches for the third time. "A man like Felix has spent his whole life being let in through the service door. Tonight he comes through the front. He will be so grateful that he will tell me things he should not, and then he will go home and be unable to sleep, and in the morning he will write me a letter that begins 'Domina' and ends with an apology."

"And if he does not?"

"Then he is stupider than he looks, and I will have to do this the other way." Cassia straightened a cushion. "Which is more expensive and less pleasant for everyone."

The guest list was chosen with the care of a general disposing his troops. Proculus, because Proculus was a senator and a gossip and would tell the whole city that Felix had dined at the House of the Graces, which would make Felix feel elevated and make his patron nervous. Comptus, because Comptus owed her a favour and because his presence would suggest that the evening was about business, which would make Felix feel important. Tertulla, to leaven the company and to watch. Ione, because Ione was leaving in the spring and had asked to be present at one last dinner, and because a beautiful woman at the end of the table makes men talk. And, at the last moment, Cassia had sent a boy to the Palatine with a note for Publius Sulpicius Corvinus, asking him to dine.

The young man arrived early, nervous, in his second-best tunic, and stood in the atrium looking at the painted walls as though he had never seen a house before.

"Domina," he said. "My father does not know I am here."

"Your father is not on the guest list."

"He would not approve."

"Your father does not approve of most things. It is what makes him so restful." Cassia took his arm. "Come. You are seated at the end, next to the lady Tertulla, who will be rude to you and will make you laugh. When the man named Felix speaks to you, you will be polite and you will tell him nothing. When he asks about your family, you will say that your grandmother is unwell and your sister is to be married and your father is busy with the Asian command. You will say it as though you were bored."

"I am not bored."

"Then you will pretend. It is a skill, like swimming." She looked at him. "And Publius. Whatever happens tonight, you will not lose your temper. Not for any reason. If he insults your father, you will smile. If he insults me, you will smile. If he says something about your mother, you will smile and you will change the subject to the games. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Say it."

"I will smile and change the subject to the games."

"Good boy," Cassia said, and saw his face change at the words, and understood that no one had called him a good boy in a very long time.

The dinner was a success. It was always a success; Cassia had built her reputation on the fact that a dinner in her house was never dull and never quite safe. The gustatio came in on silver: eggs, olives, mushrooms in garum, oysters from Baiae, bread from the bakery next door. The wine was Caecuban, and Proculus, who had been raised to believe that Caecuban was the only wine worth drinking, drank a great deal of it and became jovial. Comptus talked about the grain fleet and was listened to politely. Felix, seated at Cassia's right hand, ate slowly and watched everything.

Proculus, by the second cup, had begun to tell the story of the time he had been insulted by a man in the theatre, which was a story he told at every dinner and which had improved with the telling to the point where it now involved an emperor, two Vestals and a performing bear. Comptus listened with the fixed courtesy of a man who needs something. Tertulla listened with the expression of a woman who has heard the story four times and intends to say so before the evening is out. Publius looked at Dama.

"Tell me," Cassia said to Felix, while Proculus was drawing breath, "how does a man get into your trade?"

Felix looked at her over his cup. "You are asking about my family."

"I am asking about your life. There is a difference."

"My father was a schoolmaster in the Subura. He taught letters to the sons of freedmen for a copper a day and he died of a cough when I was twelve. My mother took in washing. I had a good memory and no money and no name, and I discovered at seventeen that a man with a good memory can make a living out of other people's mistakes." He ate an oyster. "There is no mystery to it, domina. It is the same trade as yours. You sell what people want to hear about themselves. I sell what they want to hear about other people."

"That is a very good answer."

"It is a true one. They are not always the same."

"You are quiet," Cassia said, when the fish came.

"I am listening."

"To what?"

"To the room." Felix cut a piece of fish. "You have arranged this evening very carefully, domina. The senator on your left, to show that you are respectable. The grain man across the table, to show that you are useful. The old lady at the end, to show that you have friends who are not men. And the boy." He ate the fish. "The boy is the interesting one."

"He is a guest."

"He is a son. There are three kinds of men in Rome, domina: men who have sons and are proud of them, men who have sons and are disappointed in them, and men who have no sons and would give a great deal for one. I have been watching you all evening and I cannot tell which kind you are."

"I am a woman. There is a fourth kind."

Felix laughed. "I am corrected."

At the second course Dama sang, and the room went quiet, because Dama's voice was a thing that made even Proculus stop talking. She sang a song about a girl who is turned into a laurel tree to escape a god, and the room listened, and Publius, at the end of the table beside Tertulla, did not look at his wine or at the dancers, but at Dama, with his mouth slightly open, the way the young look at beautiful things.

Ione leaned across the table to Felix and said something that made him laugh — a real laugh, surprised out of him — and Cassia watched the two of them together and thought that Ione would do very well in whatever life she chose, because she had learned the one thing that could not be taught, which was how to make a man feel that he had been clever in front of other people.

"Your Ione is wasted on you," Felix said, when Ione had turned away.

"Everyone is wasted on me. It is the tragedy of my house."

"She is leaving."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because she asked, and because I said yes." Cassia turned her cup. "You are surprised."

"I am surprised that you would let her go. A woman like that is worth a great deal."

"A woman like that is worth more than a great deal. She is worth what she decides she is worth." Cassia looked at him. "That is the difference between us, Felix. You see people as property. I see them as investments. An investment that is allowed to grow is worth more than a thing that is kept in a box."

Afterward, when the plates had been cleared and the fruit brought in and the lamps trimmed, Cassia leaned back and said, in a voice pitched to carry exactly as far as she wanted it to, "Felix. Tell us about your work."

The room went still. Tertulla put down her cup. Comptus looked at the table. Proculus, who was too drunk to be discreet, said, "Yes, do. I have always wanted to know how it is done."

"It is not complicated," Felix said. He was smiling, but his eyes had gone careful. "A man has a secret. A woman has a secret. A whole family has a secret. I find out what it is and I tell a magistrate, and the magistrate is grateful, and the state is grateful, and sometimes there is a reward. It is a public service."

"It is a business," Cassia said.

"It is a public service that pays."

"And who pays you?"

"The state, when the case succeeds."

"And when it does not succeed?"

"Then I do not eat." Felix turned his cup. "It is a hard trade, domina. Not everyone can do it. It requires patience and a good memory and a certain indifference to being disliked."

"I have all three," Cassia said, "and I have never once had to inform on anyone to pay my cook. That is because I sell pleasure, and you sell fear, and the market for pleasure is larger." She smiled at the table. "But I have always thought that a man in your trade must have a great many enemies, and that a man with enemies ought to have a great many friends."

The room was silent. Proculus looked from one to the other with the fat, happy confusion of a man who suspects a fight and is looking forward to it.

"I have friends," Felix said.

"Do you? Name one."

He opened his mouth, and then closed it. The silence went on a beat too long.

"I thought so," Cassia said. "You have clients and payers and men who use you and men who despise you. You do not have friends. I am offering you the chance to have one, and I am doing it in front of witnesses, which means that if you accept, everyone in this room will know that you were received at my table, and the next time a magistrate looks at you he will see a man with a friend in the Esquiline, and he will think twice."

"Why would you do that?"

"Because I want something."

"Of course you do." Felix set down his cup. "What?"

"Pactus," Cassia said. "And the man behind Pactus. And the petition that is going to be laid before the consul, and the names of the witnesses, and the date."

The room had gone very quiet. Publius was staring at her. Proculus had stopped chewing. Comptus was looking at his plate as though it might save him.

"That is a great deal," Felix said.

"It is. And I am offering a great deal in exchange. Not just money. Money is the least of it. I am offering you a place at this table, and the protection of this house, and the kind of introduction that no amount of money can buy." Cassia leaned forward. "You are a clever man, Felix. You have spent your life climbing a wall that has no footholds, and you have climbed it with your fingernails, and you are still on the wrong side. I am telling you that there is a gate, and that I am standing beside it, and that the price of the gate is one name."

Felix looked at her. In the lamplight his face was not handsome; it was tired and sharp and quite intelligent, and there was something in it that Cassia recognized, because she had seen it in her own mirror: the look of a person who has been alone for a very long time and has learned to call it independence.

"Think about what, exactly?" Cassia said. "About whether you would rather be a man who is feared or a man who is received? About whether the money you are being paid is worth more than the protection I am offering? About what will happen to you when Pactus has what he wants and no longer needs a delator?" She set her cup down. "Because I will tell you what will happen. He will pay you, and then he will be a consular with an Asian command, and he will look at you one day across a room and he will think: that man knows what I did. And then he will find a magistrate who owes him a favour and he will have you charged with extortion, and you will lose everything, and the man who paid you will be the man who signs the order."

Felix's face did not change. But his hand did, and Cassia saw it, and she knew she had found the place where he was soft.

"You do not know that," he said.

"I know men. Your Pactus is a new man who has spent his whole life climbing, and a new man who has climbed is more afraid of falling than any old family in Rome. He will not tolerate a witness to his own beginning. He will pay you and then he will bury you, and he will do it politely, in the Senate, with a speech about the dignity of the equestrian order." She leaned forward. "I will not bury you, Felix. I have no reason to. You are useful to me, and useful men are worth keeping. That is not friendship. It is arithmetic. But arithmetic is more reliable than friendship, and I have found that it lasts longer."

"I will think about it," he said.

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only one you are getting at the table." He rose. "You have been very generous, domina. The fish was excellent. I will send my answer tomorrow."

He went out. Bathyllus showed him to the door, and the room let out its breath, and Proculus said, "Well," and reached for the wine.

The garden was cold and the fountain had been turned off for the winter, and Cassia stood by the dry basin with her arms wrapped around herself and watched the last of the guests go.

Tertulla came out with a cloak and put it around her shoulders. "He will not take it."

"He might."

"He will not. He is a man who has been bought by other men all his life. He does not know how to be bought by a woman." Tertulla stood beside her. "What did you see in his face?"

"Fear. And something else." Cassia pulled the cloak closer. "He is proud. That is the thing about men like Felix. They are not poor and they are not stupid and they are not without courage. What they are is proud, and the world has spent their whole lives telling them that they are nothing, and they have built a career out of proving the world wrong in the worst possible way." She looked up at the sky. "If I can persuade him that there is another way to be a man than the one he has chosen, he will come to me. If I cannot, he will go to Pactus and tell him everything I said tonight, and we will have a war."

"You should have let me be in the room."

"You were in the room."

"I mean with a knife."

Cassia laughed. It was a tired laugh, and it hurt somewhere in her chest, and she stopped.

"I am too old for this," she said.

"You are forty-two."

"That is exactly what I mean." She turned. "When I was twenty-five I thought that by the time I was forty I would be safe. I thought I would have a house and a fortune and a wall around me and no one would be able to touch me. And here I am, at forty-two, standing in a cold garden bargaining with a delator for the life of a man I have spent twenty-four years trying to forget."

"You are not bargaining for his life."

"I am bargaining for his life, and for the truth about my son, and for the safety of a household that depends on me, and for the future of a girl who came to me in a wine shop because she wanted to know what her family was." Cassia shook her head. "They are all the same bargain. That is what no one tells you about power. You think it is a thing you hold. It is a thing that holds you."

Tertulla took her hand. "Come inside. It is cold, and there is wine, and there is a boy in the dining room who is pretending to be a man and doing it badly, and he needs to be sent home before his father finds out he was here."

They went in. Publius was standing by the table with a cup in his hand, looking at the three Graces in the alcove. He turned when they came in, and his face was flushed with wine and with something else.

"Domina," he said. "I want to ask you something."

"Ask."

"Felix said something tonight. About a potter on the Via Ostiensis. A boy of twenty-four." He looked at her. "I have heard my grandmother say something like it. She said it to my mother, when she was ill. She said, 'He is not to be found.' And my mother said, 'Who?' and my grandmother said, 'The potter's boy.'"

Cassia's face did not change. She had had years of practice.

"Your grandmother is dying," she said. "Dying people say a great many things."

"She is not mad."

"No. She is not mad." Cassia sat down on the couch. "Sit, Publius. This is going to be a long conversation, and I would rather have it sitting down."

He sat. He was twenty-one years old, and he had his father's eyes and his grandmother's jaw and his mother's stubbornness, and he was frightened, and he was trying not to show it.

"There is a story," Cassia said, "that belongs to your father and to me and to your grandmother, and it is not a story I am going to tell you tonight. But I will tell you this much, because you have earned it: there may be a man in this city who is your brother. Not your mother's son. Not your father's lawful son. A child born before you were born, to a woman who was told he had died." She looked at him. "If he is alive, he does not know who he is. If he is alive, he has been raised by people who may not know the truth either. And if he is alive, then everything you have been told about your family is a lie, and you will have to decide what to do with that."

Publius stared at her. His face had gone the colour of the plaster.

"Is he alive?" he said.

"I do not know. I am going to find out."

"Does my father know?"

"Your father knows as much as I do. Perhaps less." Cassia leaned forward. "And Publius. This is the most important thing I will ever say to you. You will not tell your grandmother. You will not tell your sister. You will not tell your mother until your father and I have decided how to tell her. If you tell anyone — anyone at all, even a friend, even a slave — you will put the boy in danger. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Say it."

"I understand."

"Good." Cassia stood. "Go home. And Publius—"

"Yes?"

"Your father loves you. He has a bad way of showing it, and he has spent your whole life being afraid that you would turn out like him, and it has made him cold. But he loves you." She said it, and knew it was true, and knew also that she had spent twenty-four years believing the opposite. "It is a poor sort of love that cannot say its own name. But it is not nothing."

He went out. Cassia stood in the dining room with the three Graces looking down at her and the lamps burning low, and Tertulla came and stood beside her and said nothing at all.

She did not tell Corvinus about the box. She told herself it was because a message sent to the Palatine would be read by a dozen people before it reached him, and because his mother's household was full of eyes, and because the accusation was still hanging over him and any letter from her was a risk. All of that was true. It was not the reason.

The reason was that she wanted to open it alone.

Bathyllus came back on the third day, at dawn, with the box.

Cassia was in the tablinum when he came in. She had not slept; she had spent the night going over the accounts and the black book and the household lists, because there is a kind of comfort in columns of figures, and because she had found that a woman who is waiting for news can either wait well or wait badly, and waiting well consists of finding something to do with the hours.

He was dirty and tired and had a cut over one eye, and he set the box on the desk and stood back.

"Domina."

"You are hurt."

"A stone. On the road. It is nothing." He wiped his face with his sleeve. "The men from the city were at the inn at Tibur the night before we came. Two of them. They had been to the house with the fig tree and the granddaughter had told them nothing, and they had gone away, and they had come back with a magistrate's man and a piece of paper that said they had the right to search the premises."

"And?"

"And the magistrate's man was a boy of nineteen who did not know what he was doing, and I gave him a cup of wine and a little money and told him that the old woman was my mother's aunt and that she had a chest of my uncle's things that I had come to collect, and he looked in the chest and found linen and a broken lamp and a doll, and he went away." Bathyllus's mouth moved. "The men from the city were not pleased. They are still at the inn, waiting."

"The box?"

"The old woman gave it to me herself. She would not let the granddaughter carry it. She held it on her lap all the way to the gate and then she put it in my hands and said, 'Tell her I kept it. Tell her I kept it every day, and I am not afraid of her.'" He paused. "She would not come. She said she has lived in that house for fifty years and she will die in it, and that if the lady wants to hear the truth she can come and hear it in the place where it happened."

Cassia looked at the box. It was a small chest of dark wood, no bigger than two hands, with a bronze lock and a strip of leather around it, and it had been polished so many times that the grain had worn smooth.

"And the granddaughter?"

"Outside. In the kitchen. She is frightened, domina, and she has walked a long way, and she says she will not go back without the box."

"She will not have to." Cassia reached out and put her hand on the lid. It was cold. She thought about the night in the cell, and the cry, and the cart, and the fig tree. "What is her name?"

"Marcella."

"Bring her in."

Bathyllus went out. Cassia sat with her hand on the box and did not open it. She had waited twenty-four years. She could wait a quarter of an hour more.

Marcella was perhaps nineteen, with her grandmother's quick eyes and a countrywoman's hands and the look of a person who has been told all her life that there is a thing in the house that must not be spoken of. She stood in the doorway and looked at Cassia and then at the box and then at Cassia again.

"Domina," she said. "My grandmother says to tell you that the little master lived."

The room was very quiet. Outside, a cart went past on the stones, and a bird sang in the garden, and the house went on with its morning, and Cassia sat behind her desk with her hand on a wooden box and her heart going like a bird in a fist.

"Say it again," she said.

"The little master lived," Marcella said. "She gave him to a man. She has the paper. It is in the box."

Cassia's hand closed on the lock. She did not open it.

"Does she know where he is?"

"She knows the name of the man. She does not know if the boy is alive. She says the man was a potter, and that he lived in Rome, outside the gate, and that she has prayed for him every day for twenty-four years." Marcella's chin trembled. "She says she is sorry. She says she has been sorry every day, and that she could not tell you before because the old lady would have had her killed, and because she was afraid, and because she did not know whether you were alive."

Cassia sat very still. Then she said, "Take her to the kitchen and give her something to eat. Thais will find her a bed. She is to stay until I say otherwise."

Marcella went out. Cassia sat alone in the tablinum with the box in front of her, and the morning light coming in through the window, and the whole house going on around her, and she did not open it.

She did not open it because she was afraid, and because she had been afraid for twenty-four years, and because a woman who has spent her life building walls does not know what to do when a door opens.

Thais came in without knocking, which she had earned the right to do, and stood looking at the box on the desk.

"Is it what she says it is?"

"I do not know. I have not opened it."

"You should open it."

"I know."

"Then why do you not?"

Cassia looked at the box. "Because when I open it, I will know. And as long as I do not open it, he is alive and he is dead at the same time, and I have had twenty-four years of living with the dead, and I know how to do it. I do not know how to do the other thing." She put her hand flat on the lid. "There is a woman in a village at Tibur who has been keeping this box for twenty-four years. She kept it because she was afraid of an old woman in a chair. And I have been afraid of a wooden box on a desk."

"That is not the same."

"No," Cassia said. "It is worse."

She sat there until the light changed, and then she locked the box in the strongroom, and went out, and told Hermeros to have the travelling carriage prepared, and the horses fed, and a room taken at the inn at Tibur for the day after tomorrow.

And then she went into the garden, where the roses were beginning to put out their first green shoots, and she knelt down in the wet earth at the foot of the fig tree that she had planted there herself, six years ago, for no reason she had ever been able to explain to anyone, and she stayed there for a long time.
