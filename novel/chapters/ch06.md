Tertulla's house on the Caelian was smaller than Cassia's and older than the Republic, and it had two doors: one on the street, where the clients came, and one in the garden wall at the end of an alley where the rubbish was put out. Cassia came by the garden door. She came alone, with her veil over her face and a lamp under her cloak, and Bathyllus walked behind her at a distance of twenty paces with a knife in his sleeve and an expression of profound disapproval.

"An hour," he said at the gate. "If you are not out in an hour, I am coming in."

"Two."

"An hour and a half, and I will knock loudly and embarrass you."

"That is the most insubordinate thing anyone has said to me this year," Cassia said, "and it is January."

The garden was small and bare and smelled of wet earth and rosemary. Tertulla was waiting at the door of the little dining room with a lamp in her hand and a face that was doing its best to be neutral and failing.

"He is here," she said.

"I know."

"He came by the garden door and he stood in my atrium and looked at the ceiling for a long time and then he said, 'This is a very old house,' and I said, 'Yes,' and then neither of us said anything for a quarter of an hour." Tertulla's mouth twisted. "I do not like him."

"You do not have to like him."

"I have to watch him look at you. That is different." She put her hand on Cassia's arm. "I will be in the kitchen. If you need me, scream. If you do not need me, scream anyway, and I will come and be rude to him."

She went out. Cassia stood in the corridor for a moment, with the lamp and the cold and the smell of rosemary, and then she opened the door.

He was standing by the window with his back to her, in a dark cloak, his hands behind his back. It was the posture of his father; she had seen it in a tablinum at Tibur twenty-four years before, and the sight of it went through her like a blade.

"You came," she said.

"I said I would."

"Men say a great many things."

He turned. In the lamplight he looked older than he had in the forum; the lines around his mouth were deeper, and there was a greyness under his skin that had not been there at the Kalends. He had not slept. She recognized the signs because she had not slept either.

"Thank you for seeing me," he said.

"Sit down, Lucius. You are making the room smaller."

He sat. She sat opposite him, with the table between them, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The lamp burned. Somewhere in the house a door closed, and there was the sound of a woman laughing, very far away.

"Tell me what you know," Cassia said.

He told her. It took a quarter of an hour, and it was worse than she had feared.

Felix had come to him in December, before the Saturnalia, with a demand for three hundred thousand sesterces and a story. The story was this: a man named Faustus, who had been the assistant to the physician who had attended his father in his last illness, was now living in a room above a wine shop on the Aventine, and he had been talking. He had been talking to a woman named Psyche, a midwife, who had been at Tibur in the summer of eighty-eight. He had been talking to the old steward, who was retired and lived in a cottage near Ficulea and had a bad chest and a daughter who was not married. He had been talking, in fact, to anyone who would listen, and he had been paid to talk by a man who was not Felix, and Felix knew who the man was and would not say.

"And the accusation?"

"Parricide. That I procured the death of my father." Corvinus's voice was flat. "It is absurd. It is legally absurd. A man cannot be accused of parricide on the word of a physician's assistant and a midwife and a steward who is dying. But it does not have to be true to be laid. It only has to be laid before the consul, and then there is a trial, and a trial is a year, and in a year the Asian command is gone, the augurate is gone, the house is gone, and I am a man who was accused of killing his father and was acquitted, which in this city is worse than being convicted."

"Who is behind it?"

"Pactus. It must be Pactus. He has wanted the Asian command since he was praetor, and he has never had the name for it, and he knows that if I am out of the way the lot will fall to him."

"Pactus is a new man. He cannot afford a scandal. If it comes out that he hired a delator to destroy a consular, the emperor will eat him alive." Cassia shook her head. "No. If Pactus is involved, he is a partner, not the principal. There is someone behind him who wants the Sulpicii damaged and has the money to pay Felix and the patience to wait."

"Who?"

"I do not know yet. I will find out." She looked at him. "Now tell me the rest."

"What rest?"

"The part you have not told me. The part you have been carrying for twenty-four years. Tell me what happened that night. Tell me what you knew, and when you knew it, and what you did."

He was silent for a long time. The lamp guttered, and he reached out and turned the wick up, and the light steadied, and his face was closed and careful and entirely without defence.

"I knew my father had found out," he said. "He told me on the twenty-first of September. He called me into the tablinum and he told me that he knew, and that I was a fool, and that the girl would be sold and the child disposed of, and that I would be on a ship to Syria before the month was out. I argued with him. I told him I would marry you. He laughed at me." Corvinus's hands were flat on the table. "I told him I would go to the governor of the province and ask for a posting and take you with me. He said that if I did, he would have you sold to a house in Puteoli and he would make sure I never found you. He said he had done it before, with a girl in Rome, and that I would forget you in a year, and that in ten years I would thank him."

"And you believed him?"

"I believed he could do it. I did not believe I would forget you." He looked up. "I went to my mother. I thought — I do not know what I thought. I thought she might help. She was always the one who could manage him. She listened to me and she said that she would speak to him, and that I was to do nothing, and that she would send word to you. And then the next morning she came to me and told me that you had gone into labour in the night, and that the child was dead, and that you were dead."

Cassia sat very still. The room was cold. She could hear her own breathing, and the lamp, and the rain that had started outside, a thin, steady rain on the roof tiles.

"She told me you were dead," Corvinus said. "She told me you had a fever and the child was stillborn and you died before dawn. She had the steward bring me the physician's certificate. I did not see the body. I did not ask to see the body. I was twenty years old and I believed my mother, and I got on a ship to Syria three days later, and I did not come back to Rome for two years, and when I came back the villa at Tibur had been sold and the household had been broken up and there was no one left to ask."

"She sold me," Cassia said.

"I know."

"She sold me to a woman named Lusca, who kept a brothel in Puteoli, and I was there for four years, and I was beaten and I was used and I was made to do things that I will not tell you and that you will not ask about, and I survived because I am the kind of woman who survives, and I bought my freedom with money I earned on my back and with the help of a man who was kind to me and died of a dropsy, and I have spent twenty-four years building a life out of the wreck of that night." Her voice did not rise. "And you got on a ship."

He did not look away. "Yes."

"And you did not come back."

"I came back. Two years later. The house was empty. The fig tree was still there. I stood in the garden and I could not remember your face. I could remember your voice and the way you held your head and the way you read Catullus out loud in the kitchen, and I could not remember your face, and I thought that was the worst thing that had ever happened to me." He laughed, once, without any humour at all. "I was a fool. I was a boy. I have been a boy for twenty-four years, and I am tired of it."

Cassia looked at him across the table. The rain came down. The lamp burned. Somewhere in the house Tertulla was moving about in the kitchen, and the sound of her was small and ordinary and comforting.

"There is something you should know," she said.

"Tell me."

"The midwife is alive. Psyche. She is old and she is ill and she lives at Tibur, and she has sent me a message. She says she has something to give me." Cassia watched his face. "She was there that night. She delivered the child. She told me he was stillborn, and I heard him cry, and I have spent twenty-four years telling myself that I dreamed it."

Corvinus's face changed. It was not a large change. It was the change of a man who has been holding a door shut for a very long time and has just felt the wood begin to give.

"He cried," he said.

"He cried. And then your mother took him out of the room, and the midwife gave me something to make me sleep, and when I woke the room was clean and the child was buried under the fig tree and I was on a cart to Puteoli." Cassia leaned forward. "If she has something to give me, Lucius, it is not a ring. It is not a memory. It is either a grave with a body in it, or it is a name."

"A name," he said.

"A name and an age and a place. There is a boy somewhere in this world who is twenty-four years old and who does not know who his mother is. I am going to Tibur, and I am going to find out, and I am going to bring him home if he is alive." She said it without emphasis, as though she were discussing a shipment of wine. "And you are going to help me."

He was silent. She watched him and let the silence work, because silence was one of the tools of her trade and she had learned to use it as a carpenter uses a plane.

"Who else knows?" she said. "About the witnesses. About the accusation."

"Felix. Pactus, if he is behind it. My mother, though she will not admit it. The old steward at Ficulea, who is frightened and dying and has a daughter who is not married." He rubbed his face. "And a man in the prefect's office, I think. Felix said something about a manifest. I did not understand it at the time."

Cassia's head came up. "The grain manifest."

"What?"

"Nothing. Something else. Something that may be useful." She was already thinking — Comptus, the manifest, the clerk with the inky fingers who had been talking to someone. A chain of small men, each holding one link. "Go on."

"That is all. That is everything I know."

"Then we have something to work with. A frightened steward can be reassured. A clerk can be bought. A delator can be broken. Your mother is the only one who cannot be dealt with, because she does not want money and she does not want safety. She wants the past to stay where it is." Cassia's mouth tightened. "And the past is not going to stay where it is, because the midwife is alive and I am going to Tibur."

He was silent.

"You owe it," she said.

"I know."

"Do you? Because this is not a favour, Lucius. This is not a debt I am calling in to make you feel better. This is a child. If he is alive, he has been raised by whoever your mother sold him to, and he has been told whatever they told him, and he has no idea that there is a woman in Rome who has been listening for him for twenty-four years." Her voice was steady. "And if he is dead, then I want to stand at the grave and I want you standing beside me, because I did it alone the first time and I will not do it alone again."

He looked at her. His eyes were wet, and he did not wipe them, and she did not look away, and in the long silence that followed, the rain stopped as suddenly as it had begun, and the room was very quiet.

"Yes," he said. "I will help you."

"Then we have a bargain."

"We have a bargain."

She stood up. She did not know what she had intended to do — to leave, to call Bathyllus, to go into the kitchen and let Tertulla be rude to him. What she did instead was walk around the table and stand in front of him, and he stood up, and they were close enough that she could smell the cedar and the wool and the rain on his cloak.

"You are still wearing the same oil," she said.

"My mother's housekeeper buys it. I have never thought to change it."

"I hated it. For twenty-four years, I hated the smell of it. I would smell it in a crowd and I would have to stop walking."

"I am sorry."

"Stop saying that."

"I am sorry," he said, and then he laughed, a wrecked sort of laugh, and she put her hand flat on his chest, to stop him, or to steady him, or to feel whether he was real.

The kiss was not like the kisses in the vineyard. It was not young and it was not easy and it was not a promise of anything. It was a collision. He made a sound against her mouth, and his hands came up and held her face, and she gripped the front of his tunic and pulled him down and kissed him back as though she were trying to take something out of him by force.

They broke apart. Both of them were breathing hard.

"That is not part of the bargain," she said.

"No."

"It is a very bad idea."

"Yes."

She kissed him again.

***

Afterward they lay in Tertulla's bed, in the room with the blue ceiling, and the rain had started again and was ticking on the roof. The lamp was low. Corvinus's arm was under her head, and her hand was on his chest, and she could feel his heart going, slow now, steady, like a man who had run a long way and stopped.

"That was a mistake," she said.

"It was."

"We are going to do it again."

"Probably."

She laughed, and then stopped laughing, because the laugh had turned into something else in her throat, and she lay still until it passed. He did not say anything. He stroked her hair, and waited, and after a while she was able to breathe normally again.

"Tell me about your life," he said. "Not the war. Not the Senate. The life."

"There is no life. There is the house."

"Tell me about your wife."

He was quiet. "Aemilia is a good woman. She is cleverer than I am and she has known it since the second year of our marriage and she has never once said so. She came to me at eighteen with a dowry and a trunk of books and a great deal of hope, and I gave her a cold house and a mother who hated her and a husband who was somewhere else even when he was in the room." He looked at the ceiling. "I have tried to be kind to her. Kindness is not the same as love, and she knows the difference, and she has never complained. That is the worst of it. She has never complained."

"She came to see me at the baths."

He turned his head. "She did what?"

"She came alone, without a slave, and she asked me who Psyche was." Cassia watched his face. "She knows something is wrong. She does not know what. She has been in that house for nineteen years and she has been lied to by everyone in it, and she is beginning to understand that the lies are not for her protection."

Corvinus closed his eyes. "I should have told her."

"Yes."

"I did not know how."

"Men never do. That is why women learn to read faces." She propped herself up on one elbow. "If we survive this, Lucius, you will tell her. Not because it is good for the family, and not because it will make you feel better. Because she is your wife and she has earned the truth, and because a house built on a lie will fall on the people inside it, and I know that better than anyone in Rome."

"Tell me about the house."

So she told him. She told him about buying it with money from the brothel and the brickworks and the shipping venture, and about the strongroom behind the panel, and about Thais and Hermeros and Bathyllus, and about Sabina learning her letters and Celer and his fish sauce and Chrysis and her brother. She told him about the clients, and the games, and the parties, and the way a senator will tell a woman things at midnight that he would not tell his own brother at noon. She told him about the black book, though not where it was kept.

"And you are happy?" he said.

"I am busy."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only answer I have." She turned her head on his shoulder. "Happiness is a thing that happens to other people. I have a house, and a fortune, and twenty-three people who depend on me, and a seat at every table in Rome that matters, and no one has called me a slave in sixteen years. That is not happiness. It is better than happiness. Happiness can be taken away."

"Can it?"

"Everything can be taken away. That is the first thing I learned and the last thing I will forget." She sat up, pulling the coverlet with her. "Now. The bargain. What do we do about Felix?"

He sat up too. The mood changed, the way it does when two people who have been lovers remember that they are also, in the eyes of the world, two parties to a negotiation.

"Felix wants three hundred thousand," he said. "I do not have it. If I sell the vineyard, I will have perhaps a hundred and fifty. My mother has jewellery. My wife has a dowry, which is not mine to touch. The house is mortgaged to a banker named Sittius."

"Then we do not pay him."

"He will go to the consul."

"Let him. A delator who goes to the consul with a story and no evidence is a delator who gets fined." Cassia pushed her hair back. "The question is not Felix. The question is who is paying Felix. Find that out and we can break him. A man like that has no loyalty. He has a price, and when someone else offers a better price, he will sell his patron in a heartbeat."

"How do we find out?"

"I will find out. I have people in this city who owe me favours, and one of them is a clerk in the office of the prefect of the grain supply, and another is a woman who does the hair of the wife of a man who is close to Pactus." She looked at him. "You will do something else. You will go to your mother and you will ask her what she knows about the midwife."

He was silent.

"She gave me the cup, Lucius."

"I know."

"She told me it would make it easier. She told me the child would be buried under the fig tree. She stood in the room while the midwife took him away, and she told her to remember what she had been paid." Cassia's voice was very calm. "I have spent twenty-four years believing that she was the only person in that house who was kind to me. I would like to know whether I was wrong."

"My mother is dying."

"I know."

"She has perhaps three months. Perhaps less. The physician will not say, but I have seen it before. She is in pain all the time now, and she does not sleep, and she has begun to talk about my father as though he were in the next room." He looked at his hands. "If I ask her, she will tell me the truth, and it will kill her."

"Then it will kill her," Cassia said. "She has had sixty-seven years and a house on the Palatine and a son who has spent his whole life trying to please her. I had a night in a cell and a cart to Puteoli. I am not going to be careful with her feelings."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded.

"I will ask her," he said. "But not tonight. Tomorrow. When she is awake and lucid and has her medicine." He got up and began to dress. "And Cassia—"

"Yes?"

"Whatever she says. Whatever she did. I did not know. I want you to know that I did not know."

"I know," Cassia said. "That is the worst of it."

He stopped with his cloak half on. "What?"

"If you had known, you would have been a monster. If you had known and done nothing, I could hate you cleanly, and it would be simple, and I would be free of you." She pulled the coverlet up. "But you did not know, and you did not come, and you married a woman you do not love and you have spent twenty-four years being a good man in a bad house, and I cannot hate you cleanly. I have tried. I have tried for twenty-four years. It does not work."

He came back to the bed and stood over her. In the lamplight his face was tired and open and entirely without defences.

"I will come to Tibur with you," he said. "When you go. I will find a way."

"You will not. If you are seen on the road with me, the whole city will know by the Ides, and then the accusation will not matter because the scandal will be worse." She shook her head. "You will stay in Rome and you will do what I tell you. That is the bargain."

He bent down and kissed her forehead, and then her mouth, and then he went out. She heard him in the corridor, and the garden door, and the rain, and then Bathyllus's voice, low and unfriendly, and then nothing.

She lay in the bed for a while with her eyes open. Then she got up and dressed and went into the kitchen, where Tertulla was sitting at the table with a cup of wine and a face like a thundercloud.

"Well," Tertulla said.

"Well."

"Are you going to tell me, or am I going to have to guess?"

"You are going to have to guess," Cassia said, and sat down, and poured herself a cup, and drank it in one go. "And then you are going to be angry with me, and I am going to let you, and then we are going to have supper."

Tertulla looked at her for a long moment. Then she reached across the table and took her hand.

"I am not angry," she said. "I am frightened. There is a difference."

"Yes," Cassia said. "I know."

On the Palatine, the lamps were still burning in the old woman's room.

Corvinus came in out of the rain and stood in the doorway of the atrium, dripping, and looked at the masks in their cupboards, and at the small bronze lamp that burned before them, and at the slave who had come to take his cloak and was waiting, patiently, to be told what to do.

"Leave it," he said. "Go to bed."

Aemilia was standing at the end of the corridor, in her nightgown, with a lamp in her hand.

"You are wet," she said.

"It is raining."

"I heard the door. I thought it was a thief." She looked at him. "You have been with her."

He did not answer.

"I am not going to make a scene in the corridor at midnight, and I am not going to weep, and I am not going to ask you to choose, because I know what you would choose and I do not want to hear it said out loud." Her voice was perfectly steady. "I am going to ask you one thing. When this is over — whatever this is — will you tell me the truth? All of it? Not the version that protects me. The truth."

"Yes," he said.

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she stepped aside to let him pass.

"Your mother has been asking for you," she said. "She has been asking for you since the ninth hour. She will not say why. She has sent the boy out twice to look for you in the street." She turned away. "Whatever she wants, Lucius, hear her out. She is dying, and dying people are the only honest people in this city."

The slave went. Corvinus walked through the house to his mother's room and found the door open and the physician's boy asleep on a stool outside it. He went in.

Sulpicia Varia was propped up on her pillows with her eyes closed. Her face was the colour of candle wax, and her hands on the coverlet were so thin that he could see the shape of the bones. The room smelled of camphor and sickness and the lavender they put in the linen.

She opened her eyes.

"You went to her," she said.

"Yes."

"You smell of her."

"I know."

"Did she tell you that I poisoned your father?"

The room was very quiet. The lamp on the table burned without a flicker. Outside, the rain had stopped, and the night was cold and clear, and the stars over the Palatine were the same stars that had shone on a garden at Tibur twenty-four years before.

"No," Corvinus said. "She told me that you gave her a cup."

"I did."

"Did you kill him?"

His mother looked at him for a long time. Her eyes, in the ruin of her face, were still the eyes he had known since he was a child: pale and cold and entirely without self-deception.

"Not with the cup," she said. "The cup was for her, and it was only poppy and honey and a little of the stuff the physician used for the pain. It was not poison. She was not poisoned. She was asleep." She shifted, and winced, and went on. "Your father died of an apoplexy, in his bed, at the third hour of the morning, and the physician was called and he signed the certificate and he was paid for his signature, and I have never told anyone in this house a word of what happened before that, and I am not going to tell you now."

"Did you kill him?"

"I did not put anything in his cup," she said. "I put something in his wine at dinner, and he drank it, and he went to his room, and he was dead by morning. It was not enough to kill a strong man. He was not a strong man. He was a bully and a coward and a man who beat his son with a strap and called it education, and his heart was bad, and the physician had told him two years before that he must not excite himself." She closed her eyes. "I did not know whether it would kill him. I hoped it would. I have hoped it every day for twenty-four years, and I have confessed it every month to a priest who is now dead, and I am not sorry."

Corvinus stood by the bed. His hands were shaking. He was forty-five years old, and a consular, and an augur, and he had spoken in the Senate a hundred times, and he could not think of a single thing to say.

"The child," he said at last.

"What child?"

"Do not. Do not do that. The child. My child. The boy she was carrying." He heard his own voice rise and did not stop it. "The midwife is alive. She is at Tibur. She has something to give her. What did you do with my son?"

Sulpicia Varia opened her eyes. She looked at him with an expression that he had never seen on her face before, and that he would think about for the rest of his life: not guilt, not defiance, but calculation.

"He is dead," she said.

"Is he?"

"Yes."

"Then where is the grave?"

"Under the fig tree. As I told her."

"Then I will have it opened."

"No," his mother said, "you will not." And for the first time in the whole of his life, her voice was not cold; it was frightened. "You will not open that grave. You will not go to Tibur. You will not speak to the midwife. You will let this die, Lucius, as I have spent twenty-four years making sure that it dies. There is more at stake here than a girl and a brat. There is the house. There is the name. There is the Asian command, and the augurate, and everything your father and his father and his father's father built." She pushed herself up on her elbows. "If this comes out, we are finished. Not disgraced. Finished. There is no house, no name, no money, no marriage for your daughter, no future for your son. You will be the man who killed his father, or you will be the man whose mother killed his father and who said nothing. Either way, you are finished, and the Sulpicii are finished with you."

Corvinus looked at his mother. The rain had stopped. The lamp burned. Somewhere in the house, his wife was asleep, and his children were asleep, and the masks in the atrium looked out of their cupboards into the dark.

"Then I am finished," he said.

He turned and went out. Behind him, in the room, his mother called his name once, and then again, louder, and then the physician's boy woke up and came running, and there was the sound of a woman weeping, which was a sound he had never heard in that house before, and which he would hear in his dreams for years afterward.
