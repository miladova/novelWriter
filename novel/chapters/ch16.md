The strongroom was behind the panel in the tablinum, and it had a bronze door and a lock that had cost more than the couch in the dining room, and only three people in the house knew where the key was kept: Cassia, Hermeros, and Thais.

On the morning of the seventeenth of June, Cassia opened it and found the black book where she had left it, and the strongbox where she had left it, and the wooden box with the receipt where she had left it, and everything in its place. She stood in the little room with the lamp in her hand and looked at the shelves for a long moment, and then she crouched down and looked at the floor.

There was a mark in the dust by the bottom shelf. It was a small mark, the kind a knee makes when a person kneels down and then gets up again, and it was fresh, and it was not hers. She had not knelt in this room in a month.

She stood up slowly. She put the black book on the desk in the tablinum and opened it and went through it page by page, and the pages were all there, and the entries were all there, and nothing had been torn out or added. She held the book up to the window and looked at the edges of the pages, and on the last twenty pages there was a faint shine, a ghost of wax, the kind of mark that is left when a page has been pressed against a wax tablet and copied.

She sat down.

"Hermeros," she said.

He came. She showed him the book and the shine and the mark in the dust. He looked at all of it for a long time, and then he sat down opposite her and put his hands on his knees.

"When?" she said.

"Not this morning. The dust is fresh but the shine is old — three weeks, perhaps a month. It has been done more than once." He turned a page. "They were careful. They did not take the book out of the room. They came in, they copied, they went out, and they put everything back where it was."

"Who has been in this room?"

"Everyone, domina. It is the tablinum. The whole household comes through it. The women bring the linen, the boys sweep the floor, Celer comes in to argue about the price of fish, the steward comes in with the accounts." He paused. "But the strongroom is another matter. The strongroom is locked, and the key is in your room, and only three people know where it is."

Cassia looked at him.

"Four," she said. "I told Sabina where it was, two years ago, when I was ill. I told her to fetch the strongbox."

"Sabina is twelve."

"Sabina is twelve and she would die before she stole from me, and she is not strong enough to open the door in any case." Cassia closed the book. "Who else has been in the house with time and quiet? Not the kitchen. Not the women upstairs. Someone who could come and go without being noticed, who could be in the tablinum for a quarter of an hour without anyone thinking it strange."

She sat for a long moment. Then she said, "Send for Ione."

---

Ione came in with her hair half-dressed and a comb in her hand. She was to be freed at the end of the month; the papers were drawn up, and she had been making her arrangements, and she had been happier in the last three months than Cassia had ever seen her.

"You wanted me," she said.

"Close the door."

She closed it. Cassia did not ask her to sit.

"Someone has been copying the black book," Cassia said. "For a month, perhaps longer. Pages pressed onto wax, transcribed somewhere else, sold to someone. I want to know who."

Ione's face did not change. "And you think it was me."

"I think you are leaving in two weeks. I think you have been saving money for years. I think you have been seen twice in the last month with a man in the Subura, and I think you have been careful not to tell me who he is." Cassia's voice was level. "I think you have the knowledge, the opportunity, and the motive."

"The motive." Ione's voice was low. "I have been with you for nine years. I have kept your secrets and I have sat with your clients and I have smiled at men who made my skin crawl, and I have never once asked you for anything that you did not offer. And you think I would sell you for money."

"I think people do a great many things for money."

"Yes," Ione said. "They do. I have seen it. I have been the thing that is sold." She put the comb down on the desk. "I did not take your book. I do not know who took your book. And I am not going to stand here and be questioned like a slave in a market."

"You are a slave."

The room was very quiet. Ione's face went white and then red and then white again.

"Yes," she said. "I am. Until the end of the month. And then I will be a freedwoman with forty thousand sesterces and no house and no name, and you will be a rich woman with a house and a book and a friend who has been with you for nine years, and you will have lost the friend because you could not bear to be wrong." She picked up her comb. "I will stay until the papers are signed, because I gave you my word. But I will not sleep in this house again. I will sleep in the kitchen with the boys, and I will not speak to you unless you speak to me, and when the papers are signed I will go out of that door and I will not come back."

"Ione—"

"No," Ione said. "You have made your accusation. Do not take it back because it has made you uncomfortable." She went out and closed the door quietly behind her.

Cassia sat at the desk for a long time.

---

The truth came three days later, and it came from Philumena.

She came to the tablinum in the evening, when the house was quiet, and she stood in the doorway with her hands twisted in her apron and her face the colour of old wax, and she said, "It was me, domina."

Cassia looked up from the accounts.

"It was me," Philumena said again. "I copied the book. I did not take it out of the room. I pressed the pages onto wax and I copied them at night and I gave them to a man. I have been doing it for a month. I am sorry. I am so sorry." She began to cry. "They have my brother."

"Who has your brother?"

"The man. The man who paid me. He is a steward — he works for the senator Pactus. He came to me in the spring and he said that my brother was in the vigiles in the fourth cohort, and that my brother had been caught taking money from a shopkeeper, and that if I did not do what he asked, my brother would be dismissed and charged and sent to the mines." She wiped her face with her sleeve. "And I said I would do it. And he said it would be one page, and then it was another page, and then it was the names at the back, and then it was the accounts of the payments, and I did not know what to do. I could not tell you. If I told you, they would have killed him."

Cassia sat very still. The lamp burned.

"Where is your brother now?"

"They let him go. Last week. He came to see me and he did not know anything about it, and he laughed and said he had been given a warning and a fine and he was lucky." Philumena's hands were shaking. "So it was all for nothing. I sold you for nothing."

"You did not sell me. You gave them a copy of a book that is worth more than you can imagine, and they will use it to destroy this house and everyone in it, and the fact that your brother is safe does not change that." Cassia stood. "But you are not the first person to be frightened into doing something terrible, and you will not be the last, and I am not going to have you killed."

Philumena looked up. "You are not?"

"No." Cassia came around the desk. "You are going to write down everything. The man's name, his appearance, the place where you met, the dates, the words he used. You are going to sign it. And then you are going to leave this house tonight, with money, and you are going to go to your brother and tell him what you did, and he will be angry and then he will help you, and you will go somewhere far from Rome and you will not come back."

"Domina—"

"Do not thank me." Cassia's voice was hard. "You have cost me two weeks and a friend and the safety of everyone in this house. I am not being kind. I am being practical. If I kill you, I lose the only witness I have to the man who bought you, and I need that witness more than I need the satisfaction." She held out a tablet. "Write."

Philumena wrote. It took an hour. When it was done Cassia read it through twice and then sealed it and put it in the strongroom and gave Philumena a purse and a cloak and a letter to a woman in Ostia who kept a lodging house and did not ask questions.

At the door Philumena turned and said, "Domina. The lady Ione. She did not do it."

"I know."

"She was crying in the kitchen two nights ago. She said you had accused her and she did not know how to prove that she had not." Philumena wiped her face. "She is a good woman. She is better than me."

"I know that too," Cassia said, and her voice was not steady. "Go. And Philumena — when you get to Ostia, do not write to me. Do not send word. Do not tell anyone where you are going. There are men who will be looking for you, and I cannot protect you if I do not know where you are."

Philumena went. Cassia stood in the tablinum with the lamp burning and the black book on the desk and the empty doorway.

---

She sent for Corvinus that afternoon and met him at Tertulla's house, and they sat in the garden with the bees in the rosemary and the city noise coming over the wall, and she told him everything: the copies, the steward, the arsonist, the letter from Pactus, the boy gone from Praeneste.

He listened without interrupting. When she had finished he sat for a moment with his hands on his knees.

"The steward's name," he said.

"Anthus. He has been with Pactus for eleven years. He was a slave in the household and was freed in the year of the consulship of Palumbus, and he keeps a room above the gate of Pactus's house." Cassia turned her cup. "He is the weak point. A freedman who has done his master's dirty work for eleven years will be frightened, and a frightened freedman will talk if he is offered a better master and a place to hide."

"You want to turn him."

"I want to offer him a choice. He can go to the prefect's court and tell the truth and be given a pension and a house in a town far from Rome, or he can be arrested with his master and share his fate. It is not a difficult choice."

"It is if his master has something on him."

"Everyone has something on everyone," Cassia said. "That is what makes the world go round."

Corvinus looked at the garden wall. "The hearing is tomorrow. We have no time to turn a steward."

"Then we will do it without him." Cassia took a sheet of paper from her sleeve. "Junia's son is a clerk in the prefect's office. He has given me the names of the men who will hear the case, and their connections, and their debts. Three of the five are in the pocket of the palace. One is a cousin of Pactus. One is old Servilius Paetus, who hates new men and loves a good speech." She put the paper on the table. "That is our man. He will not vote for the palace and he will not vote for Pactus, and if we give him a reason to be angry, he will make a speech that will be remembered."

"How do we give him a reason?"

"We tell him the truth." Cassia folded the paper. "Not the truth about the boy. The truth about the grain. The truth about Primus and the money and the twenty years of theft. Paetus is an old republican. He will not care about a senator's bastard, but he will care a great deal about a freedman robbing the emperor and buying the Senate."

Corvinus was silent for a moment. Then he said, "If we do this, there is no going back. Not for me, not for my family, not for you. If we accuse a man in the palace of fraud, we had better be able to prove it, because if we cannot, we will be the ones in the mines."

"Then we had better be able to prove it," Cassia said.

She went to the kitchen. Ione was sitting on the bench by the door with a blanket around her shoulders and her hands around a cup.

"Ione."

"Domina."

"I was wrong." Cassia stood in the doorway. "It was Philumena. She was frightened. Her brother was threatened. I accused you because it was easy and because you were leaving and because I was afraid, and I have treated you badly, and I am sorry."

Ione looked at her for a long moment. Then she said, "I know it was Philumena."

"You knew?"

"I have known for a week. She has been crying in the linen room and she has not been able to look at you and she has been burning the candles down to nothing in the night." Ione turned her cup. "I did not tell you because I wanted to see whether you would believe me. And you did not."

"That is not fair."

"No," Ione said. "It is not. But it is what I did, and I am not sorry." She set the cup down. "You have spent your whole life being the cleverest person in the room, domina. You told me once that you never forgot anything and that you never trusted anyone and that it was the reason you were still alive. And I have watched you for nine years, and I have seen that it is true, and I have seen what it costs." She stood up. "I am going to be free in two weeks. And I am going to leave this house and I am going to be somebody else. But I want you to know that I would have stayed, if you had asked me. I would have stayed and run the house with you and grown old with you and none of it would have been yours alone. And now I am not going to stay, because you did not ask, and because you would not have believed me if I had said yes."

Cassia stood in the kitchen doorway with the lamp in her hand.

"I am asking now," she said.

Ione looked at her. For a moment something moved in her face — grief, or anger, or the last of a long affection.

"Too late," she said, and went past her, and up the stairs.

---

The man who set the fire was called Verecundus, and Bathyllus caught him on the twenty-first of June, in a wine shop near the Porta Flumentana, drinking away the last of his fee.

He was a thin man of forty with a broken nose and a brand on his forearm, and he did not deny it. He had been paid by a steward to set a fire in a house on the Esquiline, and he had been told that the house would be empty, and he had not known about the boy in the passage. When Bathyllus told him about the boy, he put his cup down and sat looking at the table for a long time.

"I did not know," he said.

"There was a child in the passage and the door was bolted."

"I did not bolt it. I was told the door would be open. I was told the passage was empty." Verecundus wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "I have done a great many things in my life, but I have never killed a child. I have children of my own."

Cassia came to the wine shop herself, with Bathyllus and two of Tertulla's men. She sat down opposite the arsonist and put her hands on the table and looked at him.

"You will write a statement," she said. "You will name the man who paid you and the man he works for. You will say what you were told and what you did and what you did not know. And then you will come with me to the prefect's court and you will say it again in front of a magistrate."

"And if I do not?"

"Then I will give you to my doorkeeper and my two friends, and they will take you out of the city and they will do to you what you did to my house." Cassia's voice was soft. "But I do not want to do that. I want the men who paid you. You are a tool. A tool is not worth destroying."

Verecundus looked at her. Then he picked up his cup and drank the rest of the wine and set it down and said, "Give me the tablet."

He wrote for an hour. His hand was shaking, and he could not spell, and the statement was full of holes and contradictions, but it named the steward and the steward's master, and it said the words *the senator Pactus*, and it was witnessed by Bathyllus and by the wine shop's owner and by a man at the next table who had been listening and had decided that he would rather be a witness than a suspect.

Cassia took it home and locked it in the strongroom with the others, and then she sat down at her desk and put her face in her hands.

"Domina," Hermeros said, from the doorway. "There is a message from the consul's clerk."

She looked up.

"The hearing has been moved," he said. "It will be held tomorrow."

---

The household came into the atrium at dusk, and Cassia stood on the step of the impluvium and told them what was happening.

"Tomorrow there will be a hearing in the Senate. The senator Corvinus will be accused of a crime he did not commit, and I will be called as a witness, and the men who are bringing the accusation will try to use this house and the people in it to destroy him." She looked at them. "I am not going to lie to you. Some of you may be questioned. Some of you may be taken to the prefect's house and asked the same questions over and over until you are tired enough to answer them. That is what the law allows. It is a filthy law, and it is the law we have."

Nobody spoke. The lamplight moved on the water of the impluvium.

"Thais will have a paper with the name of every one of you and the name of a person to go to if I do not come back. There is money in the strongbox for your freedom, and there is a letter to the lady Tertulla, and she will see that it is done." Cassia paused. "And if any of you wish to leave this house tonight, you may. I will not stop you and I will not hold it against you. Go to the door, tell Bathyllus, and he will give you a purse and a letter of introduction to a household in Ostia or Tibur."

There was a long silence. Then Celer said, "Domina, with respect, if I leave this house I will have to cook for strangers, and strangers do not appreciate the fish sauce."

Somebody laughed. Then somebody else. Then the whole household was laughing in the atrium, a little too loudly, the way people laugh when they are frightened, and Cassia laughed with them.

Nobody left.

Thais came to her room at the ninth hour with a cup of wine and a plate of bread and cheese and set them down on the table without asking.

"Eat," she said.

"I am not hungry."

"You never are. It is the most inconvenient thing about you." Thais sat down in the chair by the window. "I have been with you for twenty-two years. I have seen you in a brothel in Puteoli and in a room above a bakery and in this house. I have seen you buy your freedom and buy this house and buy every person in it. And I have never seen you frightened like this."

"I am not frightened."

"You are frightened and you are hiding it behind arithmetic, which is what you always do." Thais folded her arms. "What are you afraid of? Not the trial. You have been in worse rooms than a Senate."

"I am afraid of the boy," Cassia said. "I am afraid that Pactus has him, or that he has gone somewhere I cannot find him, and that the one thing I have wanted for twenty-four years is going to be taken away from me on the day before I have to stand up in front of the Senate and be called a whore." She put her hands flat on the table. "I am afraid that I have spent my whole life building a wall and that tomorrow I am going to find out that the wall was made of paper."

Thais was silent for a moment. Then she said, "Do you remember the first night you came to Puteoli?"

"I remember."

"You would not eat. You would not speak. You sat in the kitchen with your hands in your lap and you looked at the wall as though you were waiting for it to fall on you. And I said to you, 'You will not believe anything they tell you in the first month,' and you looked at me and you said, 'I do not believe anything anyone tells me.'" Thais's mouth moved. "I knew then that you would either die in that house or own it. There was no third thing."

"And now?"

"Now you are the woman who owns it, and you are sitting in the dark with a cup of wine you have not touched, and you are afraid." Thais stood. "You are allowed to be afraid. You are not allowed to be stupid. So eat the bread, and drink the wine, and tell me what you need me to do."

Cassia looked at her. Then she picked up the bread and ate it.

"The household," she said. "If I do not come back. The papers are in the strongroom. The will is in the box under the bed. The letter for Sabina is with the will. And the black book—" She stopped.

"The black book is the most dangerous thing in this house," Thais said. "If they take it, they will use it. Every name in it is a man who will be ruined, and every man who is ruined will blame you, and the ones who are not ruined will come for you to make sure they are not next."

"I know."

"Then burn it."

Cassia looked at the desk. The black book was lying there, open at a page of names.

"I have spent twenty-four years writing in it," she said. "It is the only thing I have that is entirely mine. Not the house. Not the money. The book. It is every favour and every debt and every secret and every lie, and it is the reason half the men in this city are afraid of me."

"It is the reason half the men in this city will hang you," Thais said. "Burn it."

Cassia sat for a long time. Then she picked up the book and carried it to the hearth and opened the door of the little bronze stove and put it in.

The pages caught slowly, because the book was thick and the paper was good, and she watched the names curl and blacken and turn to ash — Felix, Sittius, Pactus, Primus, a hundred others, a hundred secrets, a hundred small cruelties and large ones — and she felt nothing at all.

"Now the will," Thais said.

She wrote her will that night.

It was not a long document. The house went to Thais and Hermeros in trust for the household, with money for the manumission of everyone in it and enough to keep them for a year. The shipping venture went to Tertulla. The strongbox went to Sabina, with a letter that she was to open when she was eighteen. The black book she did not leave to anyone, because there was no longer a black book to leave.

The letter from Corvinus, the old one, the one that had been in the cedar box for twenty-four years, she did not burn. She put it in her sleeve.

Then she went up to her room and lay down in her clothes and did not sleep.

At dawn there was a knock at the garden door and Bathyllus came in with his face grey.

"Domina. The boy has gone from Praeneste. The potter's brother's workshop was visited yesterday by two men. The potter took the boy out through the back and they have not been seen since." He paused. "There is a message at the garden door. It is addressed to you."

Cassia took it. It was a single line, in a clerk's neat hand.

*The lady should consider whether a trial is worth a boy's life. P.*

She stood in the garden with the letter in her hand and the sun coming up over the burnt roof of the house, and she felt the whole of it come down on her at once — the fire, the boy, the black book, Ione, the trial, the letter in her sleeve — and for a moment she could not breathe.

Then she folded the letter and put it in her sleeve beside the other one.

"Bathyllus," she said. "Go to the Palatine. Tell the senator that the boy is missing and that Pactus has him, or knows where he is, and that I am going to the hearing, and that he is to do nothing until he hears from me." She turned. "And then go to Tertulla's house and tell her to find Junia's son, the clerk in the prefect's office, and to bring him to the court before the ninth hour."

"Domina—"

"Go."

She walked back into the house, through the burnt yard, into the tablinum, and she stood in front of the panel in the wall and looked at it for a moment.

Then she opened it and took out the receipt, and the two statements, and the letter from the palace, and the statement of the arsonist, and she put them all in a leather satchel and strapped it to her side.

"Thais," she said. "Bring me my veil. The dark one. And tell the household to bolt the doors and not to open them for anyone until I come back."

She went out into the street. The sun was up. The city was waking, and the whole of Rome was going about its business, buying and selling and arguing and dying, and somewhere in it a boy of twenty-four was hiding with a potter, and a senator was waiting in a cold house on the Palatine, and a consul was preparing to hear the most scandalous case of the year.

Cassia walked down the Clivus Suburanus toward the Forum with the satchel at her side and the two letters in her sleeve and the whole of her life behind her, and she did not look back, because a woman who looks back is a woman who is followed.
