The prefect's house stood at the foot of the Viminal, behind a wall and a gate, and it had a courtyard with a fig tree in it and a portico and a set of cells under the north wing that the clerks called the cellar and everyone else called the question room.

Cassia came at the second hour and was not admitted. She came at the fourth hour and was not admitted. She came at the sixth hour and stood in the courtyard with the fig tree and the portico and the clerks walking past her with their tablets, and she was still not admitted, and at the eighth hour a clerk came out and told her that the magistrate would see her for five minutes.

The magistrate was a man named Valerius, fifty, thin, with the neat hands of a man who had spent his life writing down other people's words and the flat eyes of a man who had stopped reading them.

"Domina," he said. "You have been here since dawn."

"I have."

"It will do no good. The questioning is a legal procedure. It is not a spectacle and it is not a negotiation, and there is nothing you can say to me that will change what happens in that room."

"I know," Cassia said. "I am not here to change it. I am here to ask you for something."

"Ask."

"The child. Sabina. She is twelve years old and she knows nothing, and I will give you anything you want if you will question her last, or not at all." Cassia's voice was steady. "I will give you a statement. I will sign it. I will say whatever you want me to say, in my own hand, and you may put it before the Senate and I will not deny it, if you will leave the child alone."

Valerius looked at her for a long moment. He did not look surprised, or moved, or contemptuous. He looked like a man doing arithmetic.

"Your statement would be worthless," he said. "You are the principal witness for the defence. A confession from you would be read as fear for your household, and the court would discount it, and my superiors would want to know why I had accepted it." He turned a page. "The child will be questioned with the others. She will be questioned last, and she will be questioned gently. That is the most I can do, and I am doing it because I have a daughter of my own and because you have been standing in my courtyard for six hours and it is bad for the dignity of the office."

"Gently."

"Gently means the questions will be simple and the restraints will be light and the questioner will be a man who is used to children." Valerius closed his tablet. "It does not mean she will not be frightened. It does not mean she will not cry. And it does not mean she will not be hurt, if she does not answer." He stood. "Go home, domina. There is nothing for you here."

While she waited, the courtyard filled and emptied and filled again.

There were petitioners there for every kind of business: a man whose brother had been taken for debt, a woman whose son had been arrested in a brawl, a merchant with a claim against a ship owner, two slaves with a letter from their master, a young man who had been waiting so long that he had begun to write on the wall. They sat on the benches along the portico and watched the clerks and exchanged the small, bitter currency of people who have been waiting a long time: who to bribe, which door to use, which magistrate was honest and which was not, how many days it took for a letter to reach the prefect's desk.

Cassia sat among them and said nothing, and after a while the woman with the son came and sat beside her.

"You have been here since dawn," she said.

"I have."

"Who is yours?"

"My household," Cassia said. "Twelve of them. Eleven now."

The woman nodded. "They took my son in the spring. He was a porter at the docks and he was in the wrong place at the wrong time, and now he is in the cellar and I have been here every day for three months." She looked at the gate. "They will not tell me anything. They will not let me see him. I have brought him bread every day and they take it and I do not know whether he eats it."

"They take the bread and they eat it themselves," Cassia said.

"I know." The woman's hands were in her lap. "But I bring it anyway. It is the only thing I can do."

Cassia looked at her. She was perhaps fifty, with the face of a woman who had worked outdoors all her life, and she had the same flat, patient look that Cassia had seen in the faces of the people on the slave platform, and in the face of a boy in a market, and in her own face, once, in a mirror in a brothel at Puteoli.

"What is your son's name?" she asked.

"Lucius. He is twenty-six. He has a wife and a little girl."

"Then he will come home," Cassia said, "and you will bring him bread, and he will complain that it is stale, and you will tell him that it is the best bread in Rome, and he will laugh at you." She paused. "That is what happens. It is not always what happens. But it is what happens often enough that we have to keep bringing the bread."

The woman looked at her for a long moment. Then she said, "You are not from the docks."

"No."

"I thought not. You have the look of a woman who has money and is not using it." She stood. "I hope your household comes home, domina. I hope you do not have to bring bread to a gate for three months."

She went back to her bench. Cassia sat under the fig tree until the light began to go, and then she heard, from under the north wing, the sound of a door opening, and the flat, patient voice of a man reading out a list of names.

She did not go home. She sat in the courtyard under the fig tree all day, and the clerks brought her water and did not speak to her, and the sun moved across the portico, and in the afternoon she heard, faintly, from under the north wing, a sound that she would not allow herself to identify.

---

Sabina had been in the cell for a day and a half, and she had learned three things.

The first was that the cell was cold. It was June, and the city outside was hot enough to make the stones sweat, but the cell was under the ground and the walls were old and the cold came up out of the floor and into her bones.

The second was that the men were not all the same. There was the fat one who brought the food and did not look at her, and the thin one who took her to the latrine and told her to hurry, and the one with the good voice who came in the morning and asked her questions in a way that made her want to answer them.

The third was that she must not answer them.

The good-voiced man was called Rufus. He was fifty and he had a kind face and he had been doing this work for twenty years, and he began each session by asking her whether she had slept and whether she was hungry.

"Sabina," he said, on the second morning. "You have been very brave."

She said nothing.

"You have not said a single word to me. Do you know how unusual that is? Most girls your age cry and tell me everything in the first hour. Their mothers, their mistresses, what they had for dinner. Everything. But you have not said a word." He sat down on the stool opposite her. "I am going to ask you some questions, and you are going to answer them, and if you answer them truthfully, this will be over very quickly and you will go back to your cell and have something to eat. Do you understand?"

She nodded.

"Good. What is your name?"

"Sabina."

"Sabina. A pretty name. How old are you?"

"Twelve."

"Twelve. And how long have you been in the house of the woman Cassia?"

"Four years."

"Four years. That is a long time." Rufus turned a page. "And in those four years, did you ever see the woman Cassia writing in a book? A large book, with a dark cover?"

Sabina's heart began to beat very fast. She had been told, in the atrium, on the night they were taken: *There is no black book. There never was.*

"No," she said.

"No?"

"No."

"Are you sure? Because I have been told by another member of the household that there is a book, and that the woman Cassia writes in it almost every night, and that she keeps it in a room behind the tablinum." Rufus's voice was gentle. "It is not a crime to have a book, Sabina. There is nothing wrong with a book. I only want to know what is in it."

"I do not know anything about a book."

"You see, I do not believe you." Rufus sighed. "And when I do not believe a person, I have to take other measures. Do you know what those measures are?"

She shook her head.

"Then I will tell you. There is a rope, and there is a rack, and there is a man who turns the rack. It is not a pleasant thing. It is not a thing I enjoy. But it is the law, and the law is the law, and if you do not tell me the truth, I will have to use it." He looked at her. "Would you like that?"

"No."

"Then tell me about the book."

Sabina began to cry. She did not want to cry; she had promised herself that she would not cry; but the tears came anyway, and her nose ran, and she wiped it on her sleeve, and she thought about the warm kitchen and Celer's bread and Thais's hands and the way the mistress had knelt down in the atrium and taken her face in her hands and said, *You are a child and you do not know anything.*

"There is no book," she said. "There is no book. There is no book."

Rufus looked at her for a long time. Then he made a small mark on his tablet and said, "Very well. We will try again tomorrow."

They did not use the rope that day. They did not use it the next day either. They used it on the third day, when the other members of the household had given them nothing and the magistrate was beginning to ask why the questioning was taking so long, and Sabina heard the men in the corridor outside her cell arguing about it, and then the door opened, and Rufus came in with two other men and a look on his face that was almost apologetic.

"I am sorry," he said. "I truly am."

They did not rack her. They tied her hands to a ring in the wall and they raised her until her feet left the floor, and they asked her the same questions, and she could not answer them because she was crying, and after a while they let her down, and she lay on the floor of the cell and shook, and Rufus knelt beside her and said, "There. That was not so bad, was it? Now. The book."

"There is no book," Sabina said.

He looked at her. Then he stood up and said, "Take her back. We will try again tomorrow."

She was returned to the house on the ninth day, with the others, and she was thinner and quieter and she did not cry when she saw Cassia, and she did not let go of her hand for a long time.

---

Thais was questioned on the first day and on the second and on the third.

She told Cassia about it afterward, in pieces, over the following weeks, in the way that people tell the things that have been done to them: not all at once, but sideways, in the middle of other conversations, in the pauses before sleep.

They began with her name and her age and her years of service, and she answered those. They asked her about the household accounts, and she gave them the accounts — every figure, every merchant, every debt — because the accounts were honest and there was no reason to lie. They asked her about the black book, and she said there was no black book. They asked her again, and she said it again. They asked her a third time, and she said it a third time, and then they tied her hands and raised her, and the pain was worse than she had expected, and she screamed, and then they let her down and asked her again.

"There is no black book," she said.

They did it three times that day. On the second day they did it twice, and on the second night she lay in the cell and could not feel her fingers, and she thought about a kitchen in Puteoli twenty-two years before and a girl in a cart with no shoes who would not eat, and she thought: I have been in this house since before there was a house, and I am not going to be the one who ends it.

On the third day they brought in the child.

They did not question Sabina in front of Thais. That would have been too much even for the prefect's office. But they let Thais hear it: the door of the next cell, and the voices, and then the crying, and then the silence.

She told Cassia about that afterward, too. She said, "I heard her crying, and I knew that if they asked me again I would tell them everything. I would have told them about the book and the boy and the senator and the letter and the whole of it. I would have sold the house and everyone in it to make them stop." She looked at her hands. "But they did not ask me again. They had finished with me. And by the time they came back, I had remembered that the book was burned and that there was nothing to tell them, and that the child had gone quiet because she had stopped crying and not because she had broken."

Cassia sat with her in the kitchen that night and held her hands, and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say.

They questioned Celer on the second day. He was in the cellar for four hours and came out with his apron still on and his face the colour of suet, and he was questioned about the fire, and about the oil, and about who had been in the kitchen that night.

He told them the truth, which was that he had been asleep, and that the storeroom was locked at night, and that the key was on a nail by the kitchen door, and that anyone in the household could have taken it.

They asked him whether his mistress had ever spoken of burning the house.

Celer said no.

They asked him again, and then again, and on the fourth time he said, "My mistress would burn her own hand before she burned this house. She built it out of nothing. She has spent sixteen years in it. If you want to know who burned it, ask the men who paid for it."

They did not ask him again. They sent him back to the cell, and he sat on the floor with his back against the wall and shook for an hour, and then he was sick, and then he was all right.

They questioned Chrysis on the third day, and they asked her about the senator, and about the visits, and about the book, and she told them that she was a slave and did not know anything about the affairs of the household, in Greek, and then in Latin, and then in Greek again, with a blankness that Cassia would have admired if she had been there to hear it.

She was not there to hear it. She was in the courtyard under the fig tree, and she heard nothing, and that was the worst of it.

They questioned Senno on the fourth day. He was fifteen and he had been in the house for four months and he had learned to sweep the yard and carry water and read three letters of the alphabet, and when they asked him about the book he said, in his bad Latin, "I do not know. I am new. I sweep."

The questioner wrote it down. It was the most truthful thing anyone said in that cellar all week.

They came back in a cart in the evening, and Cassia was at the door with Bathyllus and Tertulla and a physician and a table laid in the atrium with bread and wine and hot water, because she had decided that they would come back and she had decided that they would be hungry.

They came back eleven. There had been twelve.

The old gardener, Marcus, had died on the fifth night. He had been sixty-eight and he had a bad chest and he had been in the cold cell with the damp and the fear, and he had died in his sleep, and the clerk had told Cassia at the gate in the tone of a man reporting a broken dish.

"He was old," the clerk said. "It happens."

Cassia looked at him for a long moment. Then she said, "His name was Marcus. He had been with me for eleven years. He had a daughter in Ostia and two grandchildren, and he sent them half his wages every month, and he was the only person in my house who could make the roses grow." She paused. "Write that in your book. Write it down, so that when the men who did this are standing in front of the Senate, someone can read it out."

The clerk looked at her and did not write anything.

Thais came out of the cart last. She walked slowly, and her hair had gone white at the temples in nine days, and she held herself carefully, the way a person holds herself when something inside is broken. Cassia went to her and put her arms around her, and Thais stood in the street with her face against Cassia's shoulder and did not speak for a long time.

"I told them nothing," she said at last. "I want you to know that. I told them nothing."

"I know."

"They asked about the book, and about the boy, and about the senator, and I told them that I was an old woman who cooked and kept the accounts and did not know anything about the affairs of my mistress." Thais pulled back. "They did not believe me, but they could not make me say anything else, because there was nothing else to say." She looked at Cassia. "They had Sabina on the rope."

Cassia closed her eyes.

"She did not break," Thais said. "She is twelve years old and she did not break. She said there was no book, over and over, and they let her down, and she said it again the next day." Her voice cracked. "She is the bravest person I have ever known, and she is twelve years old, and she will never be the same."

Cassia went into the house. Sabina was sitting on the bench in the kitchen with a cup of hot milk in her hands, and she looked up when Cassia came in, and her face was the face of a child who has been somewhere that no child should go.

"Domina," she said.

Cassia knelt down in front of her and took her hands, and they were cold, and there was a red mark around her wrist that would fade in a week and would not fade in a lifetime.

"I am sorry," Cassia said. "I am so sorry. I should have sent you away. I should have sent you all away the moment the accusation was laid, and I did not, because I was proud, and because I thought I could protect you, and I could not."

Sabina looked at her. Then she said, "Did they find the book?"

"No."

"Did I do it right?"

"Yes," Cassia said. "You did it exactly right."

"Then it was worth it," Sabina said, and drank her milk.

---

On the eighth day, a man came to the house on the Esquiline with a letter and a proposal.

He was a neat, quiet man in a good cloak, and he would not give his name, and he sat in the tablinum with his hands on his knees and said, "My master is prepared to end this."

"Which master?"

"The senator Pactus, and the men who stand behind him. They do not want a trial. A trial is expensive and public and it makes enemies. They are prepared to withdraw the accusation, release your household, and return the boy to his father, if you will do three things."

"Name them."

"You will surrender the receipt. You will surrender the two statements. And you will leave Rome with your household and not return for five years."

Cassia looked at him for a long moment. The lamp was lit, and the house was empty, and somewhere in the cellar of the prefect's house her people were being questioned, and this man was sitting in front of her with his hands on his knees, offering her a way out.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the trial will proceed. The boy will be found and brought to Rome and his name will be read out in the Senate, and your household will be sold at the end of it, and you will be a freedwoman with a record and no house and no money." He said it without malice. "My master is not a cruel man. He is a practical one. He would rather have your silence than your blood."

Cassia sat very still. She thought about Thais with her white hair, and Sabina with the marks on her wrists, and Marcus in a grave outside the Esquiline gate, and the room at the top of the stairs with the window over the garden, and the boy in the yard with clay on his arms.

Then she thought about Sittius in his office, and Primus in the palace, and the letter in her sleeve, and the fact that a man who offers you a way out is a man who is frightened of what will happen if you stay.

"No," she said.

The man's face did not change. "You are refusing?"

"I am refusing. And you may tell your master something for me." Cassia leaned forward. "Tell him that I have been offered a way out by better men than him, and that I have refused those too, and that the reason is not courage. The reason is arithmetic. He cannot afford a trial and I cannot afford a silence, and one of us is going to be destroyed in the Senate in twenty-two days, and I have decided that it is not going to be me."

The man stood. "He said you would say that."

"Did he?"

"He said you were the most stubborn woman in Rome, and that you would rather be burned alive than give in, and that I was to tell you one more thing if you refused." He went to the door. "He said to tell you that the boy is not with his father. He said to tell you that the boy is with a man of his, in a place you will not find, and that if you appear in the Senate on the day of the trial, the boy will be sold to a farm in the south, and you will spend the rest of your life looking for him."

He went out. Cassia sat in the tablinum for a long time, and her hands were shaking, and she did not allow herself to cry.

The questioning produced one statement that mattered, and Cassia learned of it that night from Junia's son, the clerk in the prefect's office, who came to the garden door at midnight with his face grey and a copy of the summary in his sleeve.

"A kitchen boy," he said. "The youngest one. He was questioned on the fourth day and he said that you had quarrelled with a merchant in the spring, and that you had said, in his hearing, that the man would be sorry, and that you had spoken of fire."

Cassia read the summary. It was three lines long, and it was in the boy's own words, or in the words the clerk had put into his mouth.

"He did not say that," she said.

"No."

"He is nine years old. He does not know the word 'quarrel.'"

"No." The clerk looked at her. "But it is in the record, and the record is what the court will read. The prosecution will say that you burned your own house for the insurance, and that the fire killed a slave, and that you are a woman who destroys property for money. It will not be the main charge. It does not need to be. It only needs to be in the record, so that when the senators look at you they will remember that a boy died in your house and that you profited from it."

Cassia folded the paper and gave it back to him.

"Thank you," she said. "You have been a good friend to a woman you do not know."

"My mother says you are the only person who ever listened to her about my father," the clerk said. "She says you are going to destroy the man who destroyed us." He put the paper in his sleeve. "I hope she is right."

He went out. Cassia stood in the garden with the roses and the fountain and the dark house behind her, and then she went inside and found Tertulla in the tablinum with a lamp and a cup of wine and a letter in her hand.

"The empress will see you," Tertulla said. "Tomorrow, at the fourth hour, at the palace. Alone. You are to bring the letter and nothing else, and you are not to tell anyone where you are going."

Cassia looked at the letter. It was sealed with the empress's own seal, and the wax was the colour of old wine.

"Then we have one day," she said, "and one chance, and a boy in a cell and a dead gardener and a child with marks on her wrists."

"Yes."

"Then let us not waste it."

She wrote to Corvinus that night, and gave the letter to Bathyllus to carry to the Palatine before dawn.

*They have taken the household and the child has been on the rope and the old gardener is dead. I have not broken and neither have they, but I do not know how much longer I can ask them to hold. The boy is not with his father. Pactus has him, or says he has him, which in this city is the same thing.*

*I am going to the palace tomorrow to see the empress. If it works, the trial will be held in the Senate with the emperor's secretary present, and the witnesses will be heard without the rack, and Primus will be finished. If it does not work, I will be arrested at the palace gate and you will read about it in the Acta, and you will know that I tried.*

*Do not come to the house. Do not send anyone. Do not do anything brave or stupid. If I am taken, go to Tertulla and do what she tells you, because she is cleverer than both of us and she has no pride left to get in the way.*

*And Lucius. If this goes badly, and if the boy is found, do not let him be alone. Whatever it costs you. Whatever it does to your name. Sit in a room with him and tell him the truth and let him hate you for it if he wants to. A person who has been lied to all his life does not need a father. He needs a witness.*

*C.*

She sealed it and gave it to Bathyllus, and then she went to bed and slept, and in the morning she put on the plain stola and the dark veil and the ring with her seal and the knot of rosemary and lavender that Sabina had made her, and she went to the palace.

She went to bed, and slept, for the first time in nine nights, and dreamed of nothing at all.
