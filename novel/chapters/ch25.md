The Saturnalia began on the sixteenth day before the Kalends of January, and by the morning of the first day the house on the Esquiline smelled of pine and baking and hot wine and the particular smell of a household that has decided to be happy.

The whole house had been turned upside down, which was the point of the festival. The slaves were served at table by their masters; the children were allowed to give orders and were obeyed with exaggerated solemnity; the cooks were praised and the doorkeepers were given the best seats and the women wore whatever they liked. There were garlands of pine and ivy on every door, and lamps in every window, and a great many small gifts wrapped in cloth and straw and piled on a table in the atrium, and a clay figure of a little man — a sigillum, for the season — on the hearth, which Celer had made and which was, Cassia thought, the ugliest thing she had ever seen.

"You are not to say that in front of him," Thais said. "He is very proud of it."

"It has no face."

"It has a face. It has a face in the style of the old sigilla. It is a Saturnalian figure and it is not supposed to have a face."

"It has one eye and a nose and no mouth."

"That is how you know it is Saturnalian," Thais said, and went to see about the wine.

Cassia stood in the atrium and watched the household. Sabina was thirteen now, and she had been given the keys to the linen for the day and was taking her authority extremely seriously. Chrysis and Senno were arranging the presents, and Senno was arguing with her about the order in which they should be given, in a mixture of Latin and his own language that only the two of them understood. Celer was in the kitchen, shouting. Ione was at the accounts, because Ione had discovered that she liked accounts and that a woman who controls the accounts controls the house. Bathyllus was at the door in a new cloak, greeting the guests, because at the Saturnalia the doorkeeper was the master of ceremonies and Bathyllus had taken to the role with a gravity that was entirely unironic.

She had not always loved the Saturnalia.

When she was a girl in the kitchen at Tibur, the festival had been a day of extra work: the good dishes brought out, the wine mulled, the household fed and the slaves given a cup of the cheap wine and a piece of meat and told to be merry. When she was in Puteoli, the festival had been the busiest night of the year, and she had learned to smile through it and to count the hours until the green door closed. When she had bought the house, she had made the festival her own: the first year, there had been four of them at the table — Cassia, Thais, Hermeros, and a cook who had been paid too much and had left in the spring. Now there were thirty.

She stood in the atrium and watched them arrive and thought about the fact that she had spent twenty-four years making this day into something, and that the something had turned out to be a house full of people who were not obliged to be there.

There was a letter on the table by the door, brought by a palace slave at dawn, and she had not opened it. She opened it now.

It was from the empress's steward, in a careful, correct, wholly impersonal hand. It said that the emperor was well and that the preparations for the Eastern expedition were proceeding. It said that the clerk Primus had died in Campania in November, of a disease of the liver, and that his estate had been confiscated and his name struck from the rolls. And it said that the empress had heard how the household of Antonia Cassia had conducted itself in the summer, and that she had been minded, in the autumn, to write and say so, and had not, because a letter from the palace is a gift with a price on it, and she did not wish to put a price on that one.

And it said: *The empress has read the record of the trial. She thinks the boy has his mother's sense and his father's jaw, and she wishes him a good life. She asks me also to say that a house which frees its people is a house that will be spoken of for a hundred years, and that she is glad to hear that yours has done it.*

Cassia folded the letter and put it in her sleeve.

"Who is it from?" Thais said.

"A woman I have met twice," Cassia said. "She sends her regards, and a suggestion, and she is too well bred to call it one."

The first guests arrived at the fourth hour. Tertulla came with a basket of figs and a new book of poems and a bottle of wine from her own estate. Aemilia came with Corvinus and with young Sulpicia and Aulus, who had grown a beard and had become, since the summer, slightly less of a fool. Publius came late, with a girl on his arm whom nobody had met and whom he introduced as Calpurnia, and who turned out to be the girl from the Subura, the sailor's promised bride, who had not gone to Ostia after all.

Cassia looked at Publius. Publius looked back at her, and his chin came up, and his arm tightened around the girl's waist.

"Domina," he said. "This is Calpurnia. She is my wife."

There was a silence in the atrium.

"Since when?" Cassia said.

"Since the Kalends of November. We were married quietly. My father knows. My mother does not, and I would be grateful if no one told her until after the wine." He swallowed. "I paid the sailor. It took everything I had and a loan from my grandmother's jewellery, and I have not regretted it for a moment."

Cassia looked at the girl. She was perhaps twenty, with a plain, honest, frightened face and a good cloak and her hand in Publius's, and she was looking at the three Graces on the wall as though she expected them to come down and eat her.

"Calpurnia," Cassia said. "Come here."

The girl came. Cassia took her hands.

"Are you happy?" she said.

"Yes, domina."

"Does he make you laugh?"

"He makes me laugh a great deal. He is very foolish."

"Good," Cassia said. "A husband who cannot make you laugh is a husband who will make you cry." She looked at Publius. "You have done a stupid, brave, expensive thing, and I am proud of you, and your father is going to be impossible about it later, because he will be relieved and he does not know how to be relieved without giving a speech."

Publius laughed. Calpurnia looked at Cassia as though she had been told that the monster under the bed was actually a friend of the family.

Aemilia had not moved from her couch.

"Mother," Publius said.

"Calpurnia," Aemilia said, and her voice was perfectly level. "Come here."

The girl went. Aemilia looked at her for a long moment — the plain face, the good cloak, the hands that had been in a poor house and were not ashamed of it — and then she said, "You paid the sailor."

"Publius paid the sailor."

"With my mother-in-law's jewellery."

"With a loan," Publius said, from across the room, "which I am repaying at a rate that would make a Carthaginian blush."

Aemilia closed her eyes for a moment.

"Then you had better be good to her," she said, "because she is the only thing in this family that has not been bought and sold." She held out her hand to Calpurnia. "Sit by me. I have a great many questions, and I intend to ask all of them, and then I intend to be very kind to you for the rest of my life, because I have discovered that being kind is cheaper than being right."

Corvinus, at the head of the table, put his face in his hand.

"You see," he said to Cassia, "this is what my family is. We arrive at a Saturnalia dinner with a secret wife and a scandal and no money, and my wife decides to be gracious about it, and now I have to make a speech."

"You do not have to make a speech."

"I am going to make a speech."

"Please do not make a speech."

He made the speech. It was short and it was very bad, and it ended with the words "and I love all of you, even the ones I have not met," and the whole room drank to it, and Calpurnia cried, and Publius looked at his father with an expression that Cassia had not seen on his face before and would see on it many times afterward.

---

Aemilia found her in the garden before dinner, standing by the dry fountain with a cup of wine she had not drunk.

"I have sold the jewellery," Cassia said. "The emeralds went to a woman in the Subura for a sum that would have bought a small house. The rest went to a dealer on the Via Sacra. The money is in the strongbox, and it will go to the boy in the spring, when he has decided what he wants to do with the shop."

"He will be rich," Aemilia said.

"He will be comfortable. There is a difference." Cassia turned. "I have been thinking about your mother-in-law."

"So have I. I think about her every day. I think about her sitting in that chair and giving orders and never once apologising for anything." Aemilia's mouth moved. "And I think about the fact that she left the jewellery to you and not to my daughter, and that I have been angry about it for six months, and that I am not angry any more."

"What changed?"

"You did. You sold it and gave the money to the boy, and you did not keep a single piece, and you did not make a speech about it." Aemilia looked at her. "I would have kept the emeralds. I would have told myself that I was keeping them for my daughter, and I would have kept them, and I would have been wrong." She paused. "You are not a good woman, Cassia. But you are a better one than I am, and I have decided to stop pretending otherwise."

Cassia laughed. "That is the nicest thing anyone has said to me all year."

"It is not a compliment."

"No," Cassia said. "It is better."

The dinner was in the garden room, because the painted dining room was still being repaired, and the garden room had been hung with so many lamps and garlands that it looked like a cave in a fairy tale. There were thirty people at three tables, which was too many, and the food was too much, and the wine was excellent, and at some point in the second course Bathyllus stood up and made a speech about the dignity of doorkeepers that reduced the whole table to tears of laughter.

Cassia sat at the head of the middle table with Corvinus on one side and Tertulla on the other, and she looked down the room at the faces: Aemilia, who had become, over the year, something between a friend and an ally and a sparring partner; young Sulpicia, who was arguing with her husband about a gladiator; Publius and Calpurnia, who were feeding each other figs in a way that would have been nauseating if it had not been so obviously sincere; Sabina, who had been allowed a glass of wine and was behaving as though she had been given the keys to the city; Chrysis and Senno, who were sitting together and laughing at something Celer had said; Ione, who was wearing a dress the colour of a peacock and looking, for the first time in years, entirely at home.

"It is a good house," Corvinus said, quietly.

"It is a good house," Cassia said.

"Last year at this time I was in my mother's atrium, listening to her complain about the arrangements, and you were here, eating with your household, and neither of us knew that the other was alive." He turned his cup. "It has been the worst year of my life."

"And?"

"And the best. Both. I do not know how that is possible."

"It is possible," Cassia said, "because the worst things and the best things are usually the same things, seen from different ends."

At the end of the meal, when the tables had been cleared and the lamps were burning low, Cassia stood up and said, "I have a gift for the household. It is not the sort of gift that is usually given at the Saturnalia, and I am going to give it now because I am the mistress of this house and because I am too old to wait for the last day."

She took a roll of papers from her sleeve.

"These are manumission papers. There are nine of them. Thais and Hermeros and Bathyllus are already free and have been for years. But Sabina, Chrysis, Senno, Celer, and the five others whose names are on these papers are free as of today, by my hand, before witnesses, with the money for their freedom paid out of my own account and a sum for each of them to start a new life." She paused. "You may stay in this house, all of you, as free people, with wages and a contract and the right to leave. Or you may go, with my blessing and my recommendation and my friendship. There is no condition. There is no debt. There is nothing to be paid back."

The room was silent.

Sabina was the first to move. She stood up, white-faced, and said, "Domina. Are we to be turned out?"

"No," Cassia said. "You are to be free. It is not the same thing."

"But where will I go?"

"Wherever you like. You are thirteen years old and you can read and write and do sums and you have a purse of your own and you will always have a room in this house." Cassia's voice was not quite steady. "You can stay here until you are grown and then you can go and be a bookkeeper or a merchant or the wife of a ship's captain or anything you like, and no one will ever be able to sell you, and no one will ever be able to put you on a rope, and you will never have to stand in a market with a placard around your neck."

Sabina stared at her. Then she burst into tears and ran around the table and threw her arms around Cassia, and then the whole room was on its feet, and Celer was crying openly, and Chrysis had her hand over her mouth, and Senno was standing very still with a look on his face that Cassia would remember for the rest of her life.

Bathyllus did not move from the door. He stood with his hands folded in front of him, in his new cloak, with the gravity of a man who has been given the keys to the city.

"Bathyllus," Cassia said.

"Domina."

"You have been free for eleven years. Why are you still standing at my door?"

"Because it is my door," Bathyllus said. "I have been offered better doors. There was a man in Ostia who wanted me to run his warehouse, and a man on the Caelian who offered me twice the money and a house of my own, and I turned them both down and did not tell you, because you would have made a speech about it." He looked at the room, at the crying and the laughing and the papers in people's hands. "A freedman who works for wages at a door he chose is not the same as a slave who stands at one he was given. I wanted to choose it. I have chosen it. I will go on choosing it until I am too old to open the door."

Cassia looked at him. Then she said, "You are the best man in this house."

"I am the doorkeeper," Bathyllus said. "It is nearly the same thing."

Thais came and stood beside her and said, quietly, "You did not tell me."

"I did not want you to talk me out of it."

"I would not have talked you out of it." Thais looked at the room. "You have just given away your entire household."

"No," Cassia said. "I have just hired a household. It is more expensive. It is also better."

---

The boy came at the end of the evening, when the lamps were low and the guests were going and the house was full of the warm, tired, satisfied noise of a feast that has gone well.

He came to the garden door with his father, and he was wearing a clean tunic and his hair was wet from being combed, and he stood in the doorway of the garden room with a parcel in his hands and looked at the thirty people and the lamps and the three Graces on the wall.

"Gaius," Cassia said.

"Domina." He held out the parcel. "I made this. For the Saturnalia. It is not much."

She unwrapped it. It was a small clay lamp, well made, with a handle and a spout and a pattern of vine leaves around the rim, and on the bottom, pressed into the clay, was the bird.

"It is beautiful," she said.

"It is a lamp," he said. "It is the thing I make most of. Everyone needs a lamp." He looked around the room again. "My father said I should come. He said that a man who is invited to a house at the Saturnalia and does not go is a man who is afraid."

"Are you afraid?"

"Yes." He looked at her. "But I came."

Cassia looked at him. Then she set the lamp down on the table and said, "Come and meet the household. There are a great many of them and they have all been asking about you, and they will all want to tell you their opinions, and you do not have to listen to any of them."

She took him into the room. Thais was the first to come forward, and then Celer, and then Sabina, who had been crying and had washed her face and was now very composed, and then Ione, who looked at him for a long moment and said, "You have your mother's eyes," and then young Sulpicia, who had been waiting all evening and who came forward and said, "I am Sulpicia. I am your sister. I have been told not to say that, but I am going to say it anyway."

He looked at her. Then he laughed — the same laugh, the young man's laugh, surprised out of him — and said, "I have never had a sister."

"Now you have two," young Sulpicia said. "I am the difficult one."

The room filled up around him. Somebody gave him a cup of wine. Somebody asked him about the kiln. Somebody else asked him about the price of clay, and he began, hesitantly at first and then with more confidence, to explain the difference between the clay from the Tiber and the clay from the hills, and the whole room listened as though he were describing the campaigns of Caesar.

Cassia stood at the edge of it and watched.

"Look at him," Corvinus said, at her shoulder.

"I am looking."

"He is talking about clay to a room full of senators and their wives."

"Yes."

"And they are listening."

"Yes," Cassia said. "That is because he is the only person in this room who knows what he is talking about."

---

The guests went at midnight. The household went to bed. The lamps were put out one by one, and the house settled into the dark with the particular peace of a house that has been full and is now empty and is glad of both.

Cassia went out into the garden. It was cold and clear and the stars were very bright, and the fountain was turned off for the winter and the roses were black stumps, and she stood in the middle of it with her hands in her sleeves and looked up at the sky.

Corvinus came out and stood beside her.

"You should be in bed," he said.

"So should you."

"I am too old to be told what to do."

"You are forty-five. You are not old."

"I am old enough to know what I have missed." He looked at the house. "The boy is asleep in the room at the top of the stairs. He came to the door an hour ago and asked whether he might stay, because his father's house is on the other side of the city and it is cold and he has had a great deal of wine. And you said yes, and he went up the stairs, and I stood at the bottom and watched him go, and I thought: that is my son, going up the stairs of my — of her house, to sleep in a room that has been empty for twenty-four years."

"It is not empty now," Cassia said.

"No." He was quiet for a moment. "What happens now?"

"What do you mean?"

"Next year. And the year after. The emperor is going East; there is a war coming with Parthia, everyone says so. My son is a potter who has just discovered that his father is a senator and his grandmother was a murderer and his mother is the most famous woman in Rome. My daughter is married to a fool and is cleverer than all of us. My wife has discovered that she likes running a house and does not like me very much, and I do not blame her." He turned. "What happens now?"

Cassia looked at the sky.

"Now we get up in the morning," she said, "and we eat breakfast, and the boy goes back to his workshop, and you go to the Senate and vote for something you do not care about, and I go to the market and argue about the price of fish, and life goes on." She paused. "That is what happens. It is not a story. There is no ending. There is just the next day, and the next, and the next, and you fill them with what you can."

"That is a very bleak answer."

"It is a very honest one," Cassia said. "I have spent my whole life waiting for the ending. The moment when I would be safe. The moment when the past would be buried. The moment when the boy would come home and everything would be resolved. And what I have discovered, at forty-two, is that there is no such moment. There is only the house, and the people in it, and the work, and the fact that you have to keep doing it." She looked at him. "I used to think that was a tragedy. Now I think it is the only thing worth having."

Corvinus looked at her for a long time.

"I love you," he said.

"I know."

"You are supposed to say it back."

"I love you," Cassia said. "I have loved you since I was eighteen years old, and I have hated you for most of that time, and I am going to go on doing both for the rest of my life, and you are going to have to live with it."

"That seems fair."

"It is not fair at all. It is what there is."

He laughed. They stood in the garden for a while longer, and then the cold got into their bones, and they went inside, and Cassia went up the stairs and stopped at the door of the room at the top of the stairs and listened to the boy breathing.

Then she went to her own room and slept.

---

In the morning, the boy came down to breakfast.

He came into the kitchen with his hair standing up and his tunic creased, and he sat down at the table with the household, and Celer gave him bread and honey and a cup of hot wine, and Sabina asked him a question about the kiln, and Thais told her to let him eat, and Ione asked him whether he had slept well, and he said that he had, and that the bed was very soft, and that he had never slept in a bed that soft in his life.

Cassia came down late. The household was at the table, all of them, and the boy was in the middle of it, and he looked up when she came in and said, "Domina. Good morning."

"Good morning, Gaius."

"I have been thinking," he said. "About the shop. My father is getting old and his chest is bad, and he cannot manage the kiln on his own. I am going to need a partner, or an apprentice, or someone who can keep the accounts." He paused. "And I have been told that you are very good at accounts."

Cassia stood in the doorway of the kitchen with the cold air behind her and the warm room in front of her.

"I am the best in Rome," she said.

"Then perhaps we could do business," the boy said. "Not as mother and son. As two people who make things and sell them." He looked at her. "We could start there."

Cassia looked at him for a long moment. Then she came into the kitchen and sat down at the table and said, "We could start there."

The household went on with its breakfast. Outside, the city was waking up, and the New Year was a fortnight away, and the emperor was preparing a war in the East that would take him away from Rome and would not bring him back, and the boy in the kitchen was talking about clay, and the woman at the head of the table was listening.

The house on the Esquiline had a hole in its wall that had been patched with new tiles, and a room at the top of the stairs that was no longer empty, and a shelf above the hearth with a cup on it that nobody was allowed to use.

On the last day of December she took him out through the Porta Esquilina to see the grave.

It was a small stone, well cut, with the inscription that had cost her more than the stone: A GOOD BOY, WHO FED THE PIGEONS. There was a jar of dried flowers at the foot of it, and a bowl of water for the birds, and the ground around it had been swept.

"Who was he?" Gaius said.

"A boy in my house. He was eleven years old and he came to me from the market two years before he died, and he had the pigeons and the yard, and he did it well." Cassia looked at the stone. "In March there was a fire in this house. It was set by men who wanted to frighten me. Nolus was in a passage with a door that had been bolted from the other side, and he could not get out."

The boy was silent for a while.

"You put up a stone for a slave."

"I put up a stone for a boy who fed the pigeons." She looked at him. "You will hear, in the next year or two, a great many things about me. Some of them will be true. Some of them will be the kind of thing that is said about a woman who has money and no husband. And in the middle of it there will be this: that I had a boy in my house who was eleven years old, and he died in a fire that was meant for me, and I have never once been able to make that right." She paused. "I have spent my whole life trying to buy my way out of that. I bought a house and a business and a name and a great many other things, and none of it has worked. The only thing that has ever worked is a stone and a bowl of water and a morning in the year when the whole household comes out here and stands in the cold."

Gaius put his hand in his sleeve and brought out something wrapped in cloth.

"I made this," he said. "I did not know what it was for. I made it in the summer, when I did not know what to do with my hands."

It was a pigeon. Small, rough, fired in the kiln until it was the colour of a wet roof, with its head turned and its feet tucked in, and it was not a beautiful object, but it was exactly a pigeon.

"You made this in the summer," Cassia said.

"Yes."

"Before you knew about me."

"Yes." He turned it over in his hands. "My father said I should bring you something for the Saturnalia. I brought the lamp. But I brought this as well, and I did not know why, and I have been carrying it around for a week trying to think of a reason to give it to you, and I could not think of one." He set it at the foot of the stone, beside the bowl of water. "I still cannot. But it belongs here, I think."

Cassia looked at the small rough pigeon on the ground, and at the boy standing beside her with clay under his nails, and she did not say anything for a long time, because there was nothing to say that was not either too much or too little.

"His name was Nolus," she said, at last. "You will come out here with us in March. Every year. The whole household comes, and we stand in the cold, and Celer says something that makes everyone laugh when they should be crying, and then we go home and eat too much."

"All right," Gaius said.

"All right?"

"All right." He looked at the stone. "I did not know him. But I know what it is to be a boy in a house that is not your own, and to be given a thing to look after." He crouched down and straightened the jar of flowers. "I will come in March."

They walked back through the gate in the grey afternoon, and the city closed over them, and Cassia did not look back at the grave, because she had learned, after twenty-four years, that looking back at a thing you cannot change is a way of torturing yourself, and she had had enough of it.

She lived another twenty-two years.

She saw the emperor leave for the East in the autumn of the following year, and did not see him come back; he died at Selinus in the summer of 117, and the empire passed to a man nobody had expected, and the city held its breath and then let it out again and went on buying and selling and arguing as it always did. She saw the new emperor build a wall in Britain and a temple in Rome and a great many buildings in between. She saw the boy marry Vibia, the potter's daughter from Ostia, and have three children, and she saw the eldest of them learn to throw a pot on his grandfather's wheel. She saw Sabina grow up and go into business with a ship's captain and come back every Saturnalia with a purse full of money and a great many opinions. She saw Ione buy a house of her own and run it as a lodging house for respectable women and make a fortune at it. She saw Aemilia and Corvinus grow old together in the small house on the Esquiline, in a marriage that was not a love story and was something better: a partnership of two people who had been through a war and had decided to be kind to each other afterward.

She never married. She never left Rome. She went to Baiae once, in the spring of 114, and was bored for a month, and came home and said that she had been right about being bored and that she intended to be bored again the following year, and did not go.

She kept the house until the end. She kept the accounts until the end; Ione did the sums, but Cassia read every page. She kept the three Graces in the alcove, and the cup on the shelf above the hearth, and the room at the top of the stairs, which was never empty again after the Saturnalia of that year.

***

It was a good house. It had been built out of appetite and debt and fear and a great deal of money, and it had been burned and rebuilt and dragged through the courts and the Senate and the streets, and it was still standing, and the people in it were free, and the past had come due and been paid.

That was all. That was the whole of it. A woman had built a house, and the house had held, and the people in it had lived.
