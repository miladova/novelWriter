She had been up since the fourth hour, and she had spent the first hour of the day doing something she had not done in twenty-four years: she had stood in front of the silvered mirror in her room and looked at herself as though she were a witness.

She was forty-two years old. There were lines at the corners of her eyes and a softness under her chin that had not been there at thirty, and the scar on her thumb had faded to a thin white line, and her hair, which had been black, was black still, with a few grey threads at the temples that Philumena had been allowed to pull out and that Cassia had lately begun to leave alone. She was not beautiful. She had never been beautiful, exactly. She had been striking, and then she had been handsome, and now she was a woman whose face made men in the Forum lower their voices.

"You are staring," Thais said, from the doorway.

"I am inspecting."

"You are staring, and you are frightened, and you are doing the thing you do when you are frightened, which is to look at yourself as though you were a problem that could be solved with a better dress."

"I am not frightened."

"You are frightened. I have known you for twenty-two years." Thais came in and set a cup of hot water and honey on the table. "Drink that. You will not eat, and if you faint in the Curia they will say it is a confession."

Cassia drank it. Then she put on the plain stola and the dark veil and the ring with her seal, and she stood in the atrium while the household assembled to see her off, which was a thing she had not asked them to do and could not bring herself to stop.

Sabina had made her a knot of rosemary and lavender, which was the sort of thing a child makes, and which Cassia had seen her working on for two days. She tied it to the strap of the satchel.

"For luck," Sabina said.

"Rosemary is for remembrance."

"Then it is for remembering to come back."

Cassia looked at her for a moment. Then she bent down and kissed her on the forehead, which she had done perhaps three times in the child's life.

"I will remember," she said.

She went out into the street. The morning was hot and bright and the city was already full of the noise of the day, and she walked down the Clivus Suburanus with Bathyllus behind her and the satchel on her shoulder and the smell of rosemary and lavender rising from the knot at her side.

At the corner of the Vicus Cyprius there was a crowd outside a wine shop, and she stopped, because the crowd was looking at something, and because a woman who is on her way to the Senate should know what the city is looking at.

It was Felix.

He was being taken from a cell to the prefect's house for his own hearing, and he was in chains, and he was walking between two men of the guard with his head up and his hair in his eyes, and he looked smaller than she remembered, and older, and entirely without the smile.

He saw her. He stopped, and one of the guards pushed him, and he did not move.

"Domina," he said.

The crowd turned. Cassia stood at the edge of it and looked at him.

"I kept my word," Felix said. "I gave you the statement. I told them everything I knew. And they came for me the next morning, and they took my house, and my wife is with her sister, and my children—" He stopped. "You said my children would never want."

"They will not."

"Then why am I in chains?"

"Because you were a delator for six years," Cassia said, "and because the men you served have decided that you are more useful to them in a cell than at a witness stand, and because I am not the only person in Rome who can keep a promise." She stepped closer. "I will not forget your children. I will not forget what you did. And when this is over, if I am still standing, I will come for you."

Felix looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed — the old laugh, the one with the knife in it, but tired now, and without much hope.

"You are the only honest person in this city," he said, "and they are going to crucify you."

The guard pushed him on. The crowd closed behind them. Cassia stood in the street with the knot of rosemary and lavender at her side and her heart going like a drum, and then she turned and walked on toward the Forum.

The Curia Julia was full before the seventh hour, and the crowd outside it filled the Forum from the rostra to the basilica, and the whole city had the air of a festival that had gone wrong.

Cassia came on foot, because a litter would have been a provocation, with Bathyllus behind her and the satchel strapped to her side. She wore a plain stola and no jewellery except the ring with her seal on it, and her hair was bound up in the old way, and she had made Thais promise not to come, and Thais had promised, and Thais was somewhere in the crowd anyway.

The crowd parted for her and did not part kindly. There were voices. Somebody laughed. Somebody said a word that she had not been called in sixteen years, and Bathyllus turned his head, and she said, "No," and walked on.

At the door of the Curia the lictor stopped her.

"Women are not admitted."

"I am a witness."

"Women are not admitted."

"I am a witness summoned by the consul," Cassia said, "and if you turn me away, you will have to explain to him why the principal witness in the case of the century is standing in the Forum with the fishwives." She held out the summons. "Read it, if you can read."

The lictor read it. Then he stood aside.

The interior of the Curia was cool and dim and smelled of stone and wool and the particular smell of a great many nervous men. The benches were full. The consuls sat on their curule chairs at the end of the hall, and the clerks sat below them with their tablets, and the senators sat in their ranks in their white togas with the purple borders, and the whole thing looked, for a moment, exactly like what it was: a room full of old men who had spent their lives learning how to destroy one another with words.

Corvinus was on the front bench with his supporters. He was pale and composed, and he wore the white toga, and he had the look of a man who had not slept and had decided that it did not matter. Aemilia was not present; women were not admitted, and Cassia was admitted only because she was a witness, and she understood, walking up the aisle of that chamber, exactly how much of a favour it was and exactly how much it would cost.

Pactus sat on the other side with the prosecutor's bench, and beside him was a lawyer named Crispus with a thin face and a good voice, and behind him were the witnesses, and among them Cassia saw, for the first time in twenty-four years, a small, bent, frightened old woman in a good cloak, with a stick, and a granddaughter beside her.

Psyche looked at her. Cassia looked back. Neither of them moved.

The consul called the house to order. The clerk read the charge: that Lucius Sulpicius Corvinus, consular, augur, had procured the death of his father, Marcus Sulpicius Corvinus, by poison, in the villa at Tibur, in the year of the consulship of Domitian and Silanus; and that the said Lucius Sulpicius had done so in order to inherit his father's estate and to conceal his own misconduct with a slave of the household.

The chamber was very quiet when the clerk finished. Then the murmuring began, and the consul struck the floor with his staff and called for silence.

Crispus rose. He was a good lawyer, and he made a good speech: measured, reasonable, full of references to the dignity of the house and the sanctity of the family and the duty of the Senate to root out impiety wherever it was found. He spoke for half an hour. He did not mention the slave girl by name. He did not mention the child. He said that the prosecution would prove that the accused had been in the house on the night in question, that he had quarrelled with his father, that he had been heard to threaten him, and that after the death he had inherited the estate and had gone to Syria within the week.

All of it, Cassia thought, was true except the first and the last. Corvinus had not been in the house that night; he had been three days gone. But the rest — the quarrel, the threat, the inheritance, the flight — the rest was true, and it was the truth arranged in the shape of a lie, and she understood, watching the faces of the senators, that the shape was what mattered.

Then it was Corvinus's turn.

He rose. He did not use a written speech. He stood in the middle of the floor in his white toga and said that he had loved his father, that he had been in Syria when his father died, that he had received the news at Antioch and had wept, and that he had spent twenty-four years believing that his father had died of an apoplexy, as the physician had certified. He said that he did not know what had happened in that house on that night, and that he had come to the Senate to find out, and that he asked only for the truth.

It was a good speech. It was better than good; it was the speech of a man who had spent his life in that room, and it moved several of the older senators visibly, and Cassia saw old Paetus nodding.

Then Crispus rose again and said, "The house will hear the first witness. The midwife Psyche, of Tibur."

---

The old woman was helped to the stand by her granddaughter. She was so thin that her cloak hung on her like a shroud, and she held the stick with both hands, and when the consul told her to speak she spoke in a voice that carried, because she had spent forty years delivering babies in a village and a woman in that trade learns to be heard.

She told the truth. She told all of it: the cup, the night, the child, the cry, the mistress with the lamp, the dealer, the receipt, the twenty-four years. She did not spare herself. When Crispus asked her whether she had been paid to tell this story, she said, "I have been paid to keep silent for twenty-four years. Nobody has paid me to speak."

The chamber erupted. The consul struck the floor three times. Cassia sat very still and watched the faces of the senators, and saw what she had expected to see: not horror, not pity, but calculation. Every man in that room was working out what the story meant for him, and for his faction, and for the Asian command, and for the emperor.

When Psyche was done, Crispus rose and said, "The house has heard the midwife. The prosecution asks that the woman Antonia Cassia be called, the freedwoman of the household, the mother of the child."

The consul looked at Cassia. "The witness will come forward."

She came forward. The floor of the Curia was cold through her shoes. She stood where the old woman had stood and looked up at the consuls and then at the benches, and she thought: I have been in this city for twenty-four years and I have never once been allowed in this room, and now I am here, and I am here because of him.

"State your name and status," the clerk said.

"Antonia Cassia. Freedwoman. I keep a house on the Esquiline and I have done so for sixteen years."

"You are the mother of the child described by the witness?"

"I am."

"You were a slave in the household of Marcus Sulpicius Corvinus at Tibur in the year of the consulship of Domitian and Silanus?"

"I was."

"Tell the house what happened on the night in question."

She told it. She told it in the same voice she used for the household accounts: flat, precise, without ornament. The cup. The pain. The cry. The woman with the lamp. The morning. The cart. The house at Puteoli. She did not look at Corvinus while she spoke, and she did not look at Pactus, and she did not look at the old woman with the stick.

When she had finished, the chamber was silent.

Crispus rose. "The witness has told the house that the accused was not present at the birth."

"He was not."

"And that she was sold to a house of prostitution in Puteoli three days later."

"She was."

"And that she has spent twenty-four years building a fortune in this city, and that she now keeps a house frequented by senators, and that she has been for many years the intimate friend of the accused."

"The accused is a client of my house, as half the Senate is a client of my house," Cassia said. "If the Senate wishes to know which of its members have been in my dining room, I will be happy to provide a list. I do not think it will improve the dignity of this debate."

There was laughter, and the consul struck the floor, and Crispus's thin face tightened.

"You are a freedwoman," he said. "You are not a Roman matron. Your word in this house is worth what the word of a freedwoman is worth."

"Then it is worth exactly as much as the word of a freedman," Cassia said, "and there are three of them in this room who count the emperor's money."

This time the laughter was louder, and the consul had to stand to restore order, and Cassia saw Paetus laughing openly on the front bench.

Crispus waited for the noise to die. Then he said, "The house will note that the witness is hostile. The prosecution asks that the household of the woman Cassia be handed over for questioning, as the law allows, in order that the truth of her testimony may be tested."

The chamber went quiet.

"The law allows it," Crispus said, "because a freedwoman's household is not a Roman household. Her slaves are not the slaves of a citizen. They may be questioned under torture, and their evidence may be taken, and this house may judge for itself whether the testimony of the freedwoman is true."

Cassia stood very still. She had known it was coming. She had known it since the moment she had agreed to testify, and she had told herself that knowledge was the same as preparation.

The consul looked at her. He was an elderly man with a tired face, and he had been consul twice, and he had the look of a man who had seen a great deal and wanted to go home.

"The request is within the law," he said. "The household will be handed over to the prefect's office tonight, and the questioning will begin tomorrow. The trial will be held in thirty days." He struck the floor with his staff. "The house is dismissed."

---

The crowd outside the Curia was waiting for her.

They had heard, somehow, already — the city was like that, the city had a thousand mouths and every one of them was at the door of the Curia — and they pressed in on her as she came down the steps, and the noise was like a wave.

"Whore!" a man shouted. "Murderer's whore!"

"Where is the boy?" a woman screamed. "Where is the poor child?"

"Did you kill the old man too?"

Bathyllus put himself between her and the crowd, and two of Tertulla's men came out of nowhere and made a wall, and they got her across the Forum to the edge of the Sacra Via, and there the crowd thinned and the shouting faded and she was able to breathe.

Aemilia was waiting at the corner, in a plain litter, with the curtains drawn.

"Get in," she said.

Cassia got in. The litter moved off. Aemilia sat opposite her with her hands in her lap and her face white.

"Publius was in the gallery," she said. "He heard all of it. He came out before the crowd and he is at home now with his sister, and his sister is asking questions that I cannot answer." She looked at Cassia. "They will take your household tonight."

"I know."

"Children. Old women. The boy from the market."

"I know."

"And you will let them."

"I have no choice." Cassia's voice was steady. "If I resist, they will take them anyway and they will charge me with obstruction, and then they will have an excuse to put me in a cell, and then there will be no one to stand up in that chamber in thirty days and tell the truth."

Aemilia stared at her. "You are a cold woman."

"Yes," Cassia said. "It is the only way I have found to stay alive."

The litter stopped at the corner of the Clivus Suburanus. Cassia got out. Aemilia caught her sleeve.

"My husband did not do it," she said.

"I know."

"Then save him."

"I am trying."

"Try harder," Aemilia said, and the curtains closed, and the litter went up the hill.

---

They came at the ninth hour, as the lamps were being lit: a magistrate's clerk and eight men of the urban cohorts, with a warrant and a cart.

Cassia had prepared them. They stood in the atrium in their clean tunics with their bundles at their feet, all of them, from Thais to the smallest kitchen boy, and she had spoken to each of them alone that afternoon and told them what to say and what not to say and what to do if they were frightened.

The clerk read the warrant. Cassia listened without expression.

"The household is to be sold to the state for the duration of the questioning," the clerk said. "They will be housed in the prefect's cells and returned to you at the conclusion of the trial, less the costs of their maintenance."

"Less the costs," Cassia said. "And if they do not survive the questioning?"

The clerk looked at her. He was a thin man of fifty with the face of a clerk who has read a great many warrants.

"Then they will not be returned," he said.

Thais came forward first. She had put on her best cloak and her hair was done, and she looked, in the lamplight, like a woman going to a funeral that she had arranged herself.

"Domina," she said. "The keys."

Cassia took the keys from her belt and gave them to her. It was a small ceremony, and it meant nothing, and both of them knew it.

"Come back," Cassia said.

"I will come back," Thais said. "I am too old to die in a cell. It would be undignified."

Sabina came next. She was twelve years old and she was white to the lips, and she walked across the atrium with her bundle in her arms and her chin up, and when she reached Cassia she stopped.

"Domina," she said. "What do I say?"

"Nothing," Cassia said. "You say nothing at all. You are a child and you do not know anything and you have never heard of Tibur. If they ask you questions, you cry. If they hurt you, you cry louder. If they tell you that I have said something, you say that you do not believe them, because I would never say anything that would hurt you." She knelt down and took the child's face in her hands. "And Sabina. Whatever happens, you do not tell them about the black book. There is no black book. There never was."

"There is no black book," Sabina said.

"Good girl."

Chrysis came with Senno. The boy had been in the house for four months and had put on weight and colour and had begun to work in the garden, and he stood in the atrium with his bundle and looked at the men with the sticks and did not move.

"Domina," Chrysis said. "He is not a slave."

"He is a slave of this household. The warrant does not care that I bought him for you." Cassia looked at the boy. "Senno. You will go with your sister. You will do what she says. And when this is over, I will buy you again if I have to, and I will free you, and you will have a trade and a name."

The boy looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, "You said you did not own me."

"I did not own you," Cassia said. "The law did. It is a difference I have been explaining to people for twenty years, and I am beginning to think that no one is listening."

They went out one by one, into the street, into the cart. Celer went, and the kitchen boys, and the women from upstairs, and the old gardener, and the boy who fed the pigeons. The clerk counted them twice. The men of the cohort stood in the street with their sticks and their bored, patient faces.

Bathyllus stood in the doorway with his arms folded. He was a freedman; they could not take him.

The last to go was Thais. She stopped at the door and looked back at the house — the atrium, the impluvium, the three Graces in the alcove, the burnt patch on the wall where the fire had been — and then at Cassia.

"Twenty-two years," she said.

"Twenty-two years," Cassia said.

"Keep the lamps burning," Thais said. "I do not want to come back to a dark house."

She went out. The cart moved off. The door closed.

Tertulla came at midnight, alone, by the garden door, with a basket of food and a flask of wine and a face like a woman who had been crying and had decided not to.

"You did not eat," she said.

"I am not hungry."

"You never are. It is the most inconvenient thing about you." She set the basket down on the table in the small dining room. "I have been to the prefect's house. They are in the cells under the courtyard — the men on one side and the women on the other. The clerk says they will be questioned tomorrow morning, beginning with the oldest." She paused. "That is Thais."

Cassia sat down slowly on the couch.

"I know."

"They will question her first because she has been with you longest and knows the most, and because they think an old woman will break quickly." Tertulla sat down opposite her. "Do you want me to try to buy the clerk?"

"No. If we bribe him now, he will report it, and they will question her harder." Cassia's voice was flat. "There is a way these things are done. They will question her, and she will give them nothing, and then they will question the next one and the next, and at some point one of them will break, and that is what they are counting on. It is not a search for truth. It is a machine for producing a statement."

"And you are going to let it run."

"I am going to let it run, and I am going to be in that chamber in thirty days with the statements I have, and I am going to put the men who did this in front of the Senate and watch them explain themselves." Cassia looked up. "And I am going to hate every hour of it. Is that what you want to hear?"

Tertulla looked at her. Then she reached across and took her hand.

"No," she said. "I want to hear that you have a plan that does not require a twelve-year-old girl to be questioned by men with rods."

Cassia was silent.

"I have a plan," she said at last. "I do not know if it will work. It requires a man in the palace, and a woman I have never met, and a letter that could get us all killed." She looked at Tertulla. "And it requires me to ask you to do something that you will not want to do."

"Name it."

"I need you to go to the house of the empress's steward. Your cousin's wife is a client of his sister. I need you to get me an audience with the empress, before the trial, alone, without the knowledge of the consul or the prefect or Pactus."

Tertulla's face did not change. "The empress does not receive freedwomen."

"The empress receives whoever her steward lets through the door. And the empress is a woman who has spent thirty years married to a soldier and has no children of her own, and who has a reputation for caring about justice, and who is bored." Cassia leaned forward. "I am going to give her the letter from Primus, and I am going to tell her what her husband's money has been paying for, and I am going to ask her for one thing: that the trial be held in the Senate with the emperor's secretary present, and that the witnesses be heard without the rack."

"And if she refuses?"

"Then I will go to the trial with what I have, and I will probably lose, and my household will be sold, and the boy will be a scandal, and you will be the only person left in Rome who will speak to me." Cassia's mouth moved. "So I would prefer that she does not refuse."

Tertulla was silent for a long moment. The lamp burned. In the empty house the silence was enormous.

"I will go tomorrow," she said. "And Cassia—"

"Yes?"

"When this is over, if we are still alive, I am going to take you to Baiae for a month, and I am going to make you eat, and sleep, and sit in the sun, and you are going to be bored, and it will be the best month of your life."

Cassia laughed, and it hurt, and she said, "I do not know how to be bored."

"I know," Tertulla said. "That is why it will be the best month of your life."

Cassia stood in the empty atrium with Bathyllus and listened to the cart go down the hill, and the sound of it got smaller and smaller and then stopped, and the house was quiet in a way that it had never been quiet before.

"Domina," Bathyllus said.

"Leave me."

"Domina—"

"Leave me," Cassia said, and her voice broke on the second word, and Bathyllus went out into the yard and stood there in the dark with his back to the door, and Cassia sat down on the edge of the impluvium in the empty house and put her hands over her face.
