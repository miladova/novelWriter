The city forgot the trial in three weeks, which was longer than Cassia had expected.

At first it was everywhere: the wine shops and the baths and the barbers and the steps of the temples, and every man in Rome had an opinion about the woman who had brought down a consul and a banker and a clerk in the palace, and half of them had been in her house and the other half had wanted to be. For a fortnight she could not walk down the Clivus Suburanus without being pointed at, and there were people who came to the garden door and asked to see her, and one man who came with a lyre and wanted to sing her praises, and whom Bathyllus removed with a firmness that was almost courteous.

Then the emperor dedicated another monument, and the games were announced, and a grain ship went down off Sicily with a cargo of Egyptian wheat, and the city found something else to talk about.

That was how it went. Cassia had known it would. She had lived in Rome for twenty-four years, and she had seen the city forget a fire, a plague, a war and a murder in less time than it took to forget a trial. It was not cruelty, exactly. It was appetite. There was always something new to eat.

The consequences did not go away, though. They arrived one by one over the following months, like the bills after a wedding.

The first was the clients. On the day after the verdict, half the men who had dined at her table for years did not send their usual messages. By the end of the week, two of them had sent letters explaining that they were going to the country for the summer. By the end of the month, the house was taking a third of what it had taken in the spring, and Celer had begun to complain about the waste of food, and Thais had begun to look at the accounts in a way that Cassia did not like.

The second was Corvinus. The Senate stripped him of the augurate in September, by a vote that was not close. The Asian command went to a man named Verus, who had no qualifications except a good name and a wife with connections. Corvinus took the news quietly. He had expected it. But Cassia saw him afterward, in the garden of the Palatine house, standing under the masks with his hands behind his back, and she knew that something in him had gone out.

The third was the house on the Palatine. The mortgage had been held by Sittius, and Sittius was dead, and his heirs — a nephew and a cousin and a bank in Alexandria — had no interest in the Sulpicii. They called in the loan in October. Corvinus could not pay it. The house was sold at the end of November to a man who had made his money in bricks and who intended to knock it down and build an insula on the site, and the family moved into a smaller house on the Esquiline, two streets from Cassia's, with a garden and a portico and no history at all.

The masks went into the new atrium. There was nowhere else to put them.

Young Sulpicia came in September, alone, in a plain cloak, with a basket of figs.

She was sixteen now and she had been married for three months, and she had the look of a woman who had discovered that marriage was not the end of the world and not the beginning of one either, but a long room that had to be furnished. She sat down in the tablinum and put the figs on the desk and said, "I have come to ask you something, and I want you to tell me the truth."

"I will try."

"Did my grandmother kill my grandfather?"

Cassia looked at her. The girl's face was steady and her hands were steady and she had her grandmother's chin.

"Yes," Cassia said.

"Did my father know?"

"No. He suspected, at the end, but he did not know until she told him."

"Did you know?"

"I knew that she gave me a cup on the night my son was born, and that my son was taken from me, and that she was in the room. I did not know about your grandfather until the spring." Cassia turned her cup. "Your grandmother told me herself, in the last week of her life, and I have not told anyone what she said except your father and the Senate. I am telling you now because you asked and because you are old enough to know and because a person who is lied to by her family for sixteen years has a right to the truth."

Sulpicia was silent for a long time. Then she said, "I loved her."

"I know."

"She was cruel to my mother and cold to my father and she arranged my marriage without asking me, and I loved her. She used to let me sit in her room when I was small and she would tell me about the masks. She knew the name and the office of every one of them. She used to say that a family is a thing you carry, like a lamp, and that it is your job to carry it without dropping it." Her eyes were wet. "And she killed my grandfather and sold your son. How do I hold both of those things?"

"You hold them the way you hold everything," Cassia said. "You stop trying to make them into one thing. She was a woman who loved you and who did something terrible. Both of those things are true. You do not have to choose."

Sulpicia wiped her eyes. "My husband is a fool," she said.

"Yes."

"He is not a bad man. He is just — he talks about the games all the time. All the time. He has an opinion about every gladiator in Rome and no opinion about anything else." She blew her nose. "My mother says I should be patient and manage the house and have a son and he will improve. My grandmother would have said the same. What do you say?"

"I say have the son, and manage the house, and take the money, and keep the purse I gave you, and learn something that he does not know you can do." Cassia leaned forward. "And when you are thirty, and he is still talking about the games, you will have a life of your own that he has never once looked at, and you will be able to be kind to him, because you will not need him to be anything other than what he is."

Sulpicia looked at her. "That is a very cold answer."

"It is a warm answer that has been in the cold for a long time." Cassia picked up a fig and ate it. "Now. Tell me about the house. Is the cook any good?"

Ione came back in October.

She came to the garden door in the evening, alone, with a bag over her shoulder and a cloak that had seen better days, and Bathyllus came to the tablinum with a face that was trying very hard to be neutral.

"There is a woman at the door," he said. "She says you will know her."

Cassia put down her pen.

"Show her in."

Ione came in. She was thin and brown from the sun and she had cut her hair short in the way of a woman who has decided to stop being decorative, and she stood in the middle of the tablinum with her bag still on her shoulder and looked at Cassia.

"I heard," she said. "About the trial. About the house. About the old woman. About the boy." She set the bag down. "I was in Baiae. I was keeping a house for a man who did not want a house, and it ended badly, as those things do. I have spent the summer being somebody else and I find that I do not like her very much."

Cassia stood up.

"I am sorry," she said. "For what I said in the kitchen. For what I did not say. For —"

"Stop." Ione held up her hand. "I did not come back for an apology. I came back because I heard that you had lost half your clients and that the house was in trouble and that Thais was doing the accounts on her own, and because I am good at accounts, and because I have nowhere else to go, and because — " She stopped, and her voice changed. "Because I have been free for four months and I have discovered that freedom is a great deal of work and very little fun, and that I was happier in your kitchen than I have been anywhere since."

Cassia looked at her for a long moment. Then she said, "You are hired. The pay is bad and the hours are worse and the household is full of people who have been through too much, and the cook is impossible."

Ione's mouth trembled. "That is the best offer I have had all year."

"Then take it." Cassia came around the desk. "And Ione. I was wrong. I was wrong about the book and I was wrong about you and I was wrong to accuse you, and I have thought about it every day since you left. You were the best thing in this house and I did not see it until you were gone."

Ione looked at her. Then she put her bag down on the floor and said, "Well. I am back now. You will have to be cleverer than that if you want to get rid of me."

They embraced in the middle of the tablinum, and Thais came in and saw them and stopped, and then came and put her arms around both of them, and the household gathered in the doorway and someone began to laugh, and someone else began to cry, and Celer said, "Does this mean I have to cook for one more?" and Ione said, "Yes, Celer, and I want the fish tonight, with the sauce, not the other sauce," and the whole house settled back into itself like a thing that had been holding its breath.

---

Aemilia came in the last week of October, to the new house, and asked Cassia to help her hang the masks.

The new house on the Esquiline was smaller than the old one and newer and had no history at all, and the atrium was bright and low and the cupboards for the masks had been made by a carpenter in the Subura who had never made one before. Aemilia stood in the middle of the floor with her hands on her hips and looked at the wall.

"They do not fit," she said. "The old ones were made for a wall three feet higher. They look like a row of provincial ancestors."

"They are the same ancestors."

"They are the same wax and the same names and they look ridiculous." Aemilia sighed. "My mother-in-law would have had the carpenter flogged."

"Your mother-in-law is dead."

"Yes," Aemilia said. "That is the only good thing about this house."

Cassia laughed. She had not expected to like Aemilia, and she had discovered over the summer that she did, in the way that two women who have been through the same war like each other: not with affection, exactly, but with a shared and slightly grim understanding.

"The jewellery," Aemilia said, when the masks were hung. "Have you sold it?"

"Not yet. I have a buyer in mind for the winter. The emeralds are worth more than the rest put together, and there is a woman in the Subura who deals in old stones and does not ask questions."

"Give the money to the boy."

"I intend to."

"Give it to him as money, not as a story," Aemilia said. "A potter with a workshop needs a kiln and a cart and a boy to turn the wheel. He does not need a chest of his grandmother's jewellery and a letter about the old days. Give him the tools of his trade and let him think that the lady in Rome is a patron with more money than sense." She paused. "And when he is older, and when he asks, tell him the rest."

Cassia looked at her. "You have thought about this."

"I have thought about nothing else since the funeral. I have a son who is twenty-two and a daughter who is sixteen and a husband who has lost everything, and I have discovered that the only thing I am good at is arranging things so that the people I love are not hurt any more than necessary." Aemilia straightened a mask. "It is not a noble talent. But it is mine."

The potter came in November, on a wet afternoon, with clay under his nails and a letter in his hand.

Gaius Vibidius had aged in the six months since Cassia had last seen him. He was greyer and thinner and there was a tremor in his hands that had not been there before, and he sat down in the tablinum and put the letter on the desk between them.

"He is home," he said. "They brought him back from Aricia in August. He was not hurt. They had told him that he was being kept for his own safety, and he believed them, because he is a trusting boy and because he had no reason not to." He paused. "He knows there is something wrong. He has known since the men came to the workshop at Praeneste. He has been asking questions, and I have been lying to him, and I am not a good liar."

Cassia looked at the letter. It was unsealed and it was addressed to her, in a careful, awkward hand.

"He wrote that," the potter said. "Himself. He asked me to bring it to you. He does not know who you are. He thinks you are the lady who paid for his apprenticeship, or a patron of the shop, or something of that kind. He has been told that a great lady in Rome took an interest in him, and he has decided that he wants to thank her." The potter's mouth twisted. "He is a good boy. He does not know that he is in the middle of anything."

Cassia picked up the letter.

*Domina. My father says you are the lady who paid for my apprenticeship and for the new kiln. I do not know why you did it and I do not know who you are, but I want to thank you. The kiln is very good. I have made a set of cups that I am proud of, and if you would like them I will send them to you. My father says I am not to ask questions, and I am trying not to, but I would like to know your name, if it is not a secret. Yours, Gaius Vibidius Nestor.*

She read it twice. Then she put it down on the desk and looked at the potter.

"What have you told him?"

"That you are a patron. That he should be grateful. That he should not ask questions." The potter's hands were on his knees. "I have lied to him for four months, domina, and it is eating me alive. He is twenty-four years old. He has a right to know who he is. But if I tell him, he will want to know why I did not tell him before, and then he will want to know what happened to his mother, and then —" He stopped.

"And then he will want to know about me."

"Yes."

Cassia sat for a moment. The rain came down on the roof. Somewhere in the house Ione was arguing with Celer about the fish.

"There is a thing I have not told you," she said. "And I am going to tell you now, because you have earned it and because I cannot ask you to lie to your son for me."

She told him about the trial. She told him about the receipt and the sealed record and the names struck out and the promise she had made to the empress. She told him about the chest of jewellery and the money that was waiting for the boy. And she told him what she had decided.

"I am not going to take him from you," she said. "Not now, not ever. He is your son. You have raised him and fed him and taught him the wheel and the kiln and the price of clay, and I will not undo twenty-four years of that because I happen to have given birth to him." She paused. "But he has a right to know. Not today. Not tomorrow. But some day, when he is ready, and when you are ready, and when I can tell him the whole truth in a room with a door that locks and a cup of wine in his hand. And then he can decide what he wants to do with it."

The potter looked at her for a long time. Then he said, "And if he wants to know you?"

"Then I will be here."

"And if he does not?"

"Then I will be here anyway," Cassia said. "That is what a mother is. It is not a thing you earn or a thing you deserve. It is a thing that happens to you, and then you spend the rest of your life standing in a room waiting for someone to come through the door."

The potter wiped his eyes with the back of his hand and said, "I am sorry. I am not a man who cries in front of ladies."

"You are not in front of a lady," Cassia said. "You are in front of a woman who has been crying in kitchens for twenty-four years." She stood. "Now. Tell me about the kiln. And tell me about the cups. And then go home and tell your son that the lady in Rome was very pleased with his letter, and that she would like the cups, and that she will write to him herself."

---

She wrote to him that night.

It took her four hours and eleven drafts, and the letter she finally sent was three lines long.

*Gaius Vibidius Nestor. I am glad the kiln is good. I would like the cups. Send them to the house on the Esquiline with the green door, and tell the doorkeeper that they are from the potter on the Via Ostiensis, and he will bring them to me. Your name is not a secret. It is a good name and you should be proud of it. A friend.*

She sealed it and gave it to Bathyllus to take to the Via Ostiensis in the morning, and then she sat in the tablinum with the lamps burning and the rain on the roof and her hands in her lap.

Thais came in with a cup of hot wine and set it down.

"You have written to him," she said.

"I have written to him."

"What did you say?"

"Nothing. Everything. I do not know." Cassia picked up the cup. "I said I would like the cups and that he should be proud of his name."

Thais sat down opposite her. "It is a beginning."

"It is not a beginning. It is a letter about cups."

"Everything is a letter about cups," Thais said, "until it is not."

---

The house on the Esquiline began to fill again in November.

It was not the same as before. The great men did not come back; they had found other houses, or they were afraid of the empress's shadow, or they had simply moved on to the next amusement. But the men who came now were different: merchants with money and no name, equestrians who had made their fortunes in the provinces, a young senator from Gaul who wanted to be seen at the right tables, two of the new consuls' clerks, a shipowner from Ostia with a wife who was cleverer than he was.

They came because the house had been through the fire and had come out the other side, and because in Rome a house that has survived a scandal is more interesting than a house that has never had one.

Cassia did not enjoy it. She had enjoyed the old house, in the old days, when she had been building something and every dinner was a brick. Now she sat at the head of the table and watched the new men talk and thought about the fact that she had spent twenty-four years building a wall and that the wall had burned and been rebuilt and that she was still, at forty-two, sitting at the head of a table, listening to men.

The cups came at the end of November, four days before the Kalends of December.

There were six of them, wrapped in straw in a wooden box, and they were red-glossed and thin-walled and good, better than anything in Cassia's kitchen, and on the bottom of each one, pressed into the clay before firing, there was a small mark: a bird.

Bathyllus brought the box into the tablinum and set it on the desk, and Cassia took the cups out one by one and set them in a row and looked at them.

"They are very good," Thais said, from the doorway.

"They are better than good. They are the work of a man who knows what he is doing and is proud of it." Cassia turned one over in her hands. "He has been making cups since he was a boy. He has been making them for fifteen years, in a yard on the Via Ostiensis, and no one in this city knows his name, and he is better at his trade than half the men in the Senate are at theirs."

She put the cup down.

"There is a letter," Bathyllus said.

It was short, and it was in the same careful, awkward hand.

*Domina. These are the cups. I hope they are what you wanted. My father says I am to thank you again for the kiln and for the apprenticeship and for everything, and I am thanking you. I would still like to know your name. Not because I am ungrateful, but because a man likes to know who his friends are. Yours, Gaius Vibidius Nestor.*

Cassia read it twice.

"Are you going to tell him?" Thais said.

"Not yet."

"He has asked you twice."

"I know." Cassia folded the letter and put it in the drawer with the other one, the old one, the one that had been in the cedar box for twenty-four years. "A name in a letter is a strange thing. It arrives without a face. He would read it in his yard and look up and see nothing but the racks and the kiln, and he would have to imagine the woman who wrote it, and he would imagine someone smaller and quieter and kinder than I am." She closed the drawer. "There is a great deal that has to be said before he knows my name, and I am not going to say it in a letter about cups. I am going to say it to his face, in a room, with his father there."

"So what will you write?"

"That the cups are the best I have ever owned. That is true, and it is not the whole truth, and it will do for now."

She wrote the letter that night. It was four lines long: the cups, the price of the clay, a request that he send six of the red-glossed ones, and a line at the end that took her an hour and said only that his father was a good man and that she hoped the kiln was drawing well. She did not sign it with her name. She signed it as she had signed the first: *a friend.*

She sealed it and gave it to Bathyllus, and then she went and stood in the garden in the cold, with the roses cut back to stumps and the fountain turned off, and she thought about a boy in a yard with clay on his arms, and about the fact that he had asked her name twice and she had given him nothing but cups.

The house on the Palatine was sold on the last day of November, and Cassia went to see it empty.

Corvinus took her through it himself: the atrium with the empty cupboards where the masks had been, the tablinum where his father had told him what he was going to do, the garden where he had stood with his wife and watched the light go out of the sky, the room where his mother had died.

"It is a good house," he said. "It was a good house for four hundred years. And now a man who sells bricks is going to knock it down and build a block of flats, and in fifty years no one will remember that it was ever anything else."

"That is what happens to houses," Cassia said.

"Yes." He looked around the empty atrium. "That is what happens to everything."

They went out together into the street, and the door was closed behind them, and a workman was already chalking a mark on the wall.

"Where are you going?" Cassia said.

"To the new house. Aemilia has been there since Tuesday, arranging the furniture and complaining about the drains." He paused. "And then, when the potter agrees to see us, to the Via Ostiensis. To meet my son."

"And what will you say to him?"

"I do not know." Corvinus looked at the street. "I have been a senator for twenty years and I have made a hundred speeches, and I do not know what I will say to a potter of twenty-four who has my eyes and my father's jaw." He looked at her. "What will you say to him?"

"I wrote about cups," Cassia said.

He laughed — a real laugh, the first she had heard from him in months — and took her arm, and they walked down the hill together through the crowds and the noise and the ordinary business of the city.

"You are melancholy," Tertulla said, one night in December, when the guests had gone.

"I am tired."

"You are melancholy. I have known you for seven years and I can tell the difference." Tertulla sat down beside her. "What is it?"

"I do not know." Cassia looked at the three Graces in the alcove. "I have everything I wanted when I was thirty. The house, the money, the name, the freedom. And I sit at the table and I listen to a man from Ostia talk about his ships and I think: is this it? Is this what I spent twenty-four years building?"

"What else would you have?"

"I do not know." Cassia turned her cup. "I think I would like to be bored. I have never been bored. Not once, in forty-two years. And I think that a person who has never been bored has not really been alive."

Tertulla looked at her for a long moment. Then she said, "In January I am going to Baiae. There is a house there, with a garden and a view of the sea and a cook who is not as good as Celer and a great many old women who talk about their ailments. Come with me. For a month. We will do nothing. We will sit in the sun and be bored and you will complain about it every day."

"And the house?"

"The house will keep. It kept while you were in the palace and in the Senate and in the prefect's courtyard. It will keep while you are in Baiae." Tertulla took her hand. "You have spent your whole life being necessary. Try being unnecessary for a month. It will not kill you."

Cassia looked at the garden, and the roses, and the three Graces with their painted hands on one another's shoulders.

"Perhaps," she said. "In the spring. When the boy has decided."

"Then in the spring," Tertulla said, and poured her another cup.

They sat up for another hour, talking about the shipping venture and the price of Egyptian wheat and a senator's wife who had run off with a charioteer, and then Tertulla called for her cloak and her litter and went home, and the house settled.

After she had gone, Cassia went out into the garden with a lamp.

It was a clear night and cold enough for a cloak, and the roses were cut back and the fountain was off and the fig tree stood in the corner like a man with his hands in his pockets. She set the lamp on the wall and stood for a while in the dark, and then she went back inside and up the stairs and opened the door of the room at the top.

It had been dusted that morning, because it was dusted every morning. There was a bed with clean linen on it and a stool and a chest and a small window that looked out over the roof of the bakery next door, and on the chest there was a cup: one of the six, red-glossed and thin-walled, with a bird pressed into the bottom of it.

She had put it there in November, on the day the box came, and she had not told anyone. It was not a shrine. It was not a claim. It was a cup on a chest in a room that nobody used, waiting for a man who had asked her name twice and had been given nothing but cups.

"Some day," she said, to the room, to the cup, to nobody at all. "Some day I will tell him."

Then she shut the door and went down to the accounts.
