The house of the Sulpicii stood on the slope of the Palatine where the hill turns its shoulder against the Circus, and it had stood there, in one form or another, for four hundred years. It had been burned in the year of the great fire and rebuilt; it had been seized in the civil wars and recovered; it had been mortgaged twice and redeemed twice, and the second redemption had cost the family a vineyard in Etruria and a great deal of pride. The walls were old republican stone, plain and massive, and the door was cedar and bronze and had been repaired so often that there was hardly an original piece of it left. A stranger might have thought it shabby. A Roman would have known that it was the opposite of shabby, because a house that does not need to look rich is a house that has been rich for a very long time.

The atrium was high and dark and cold in winter, and along one wall stood the imagines: the wax masks of the Sulpicii, each in its wooden cupboard, each with its name and its offices painted underneath. There were consuls there, and praetors, and a censor, and a man who had been sent as ambassador to a king in the East and had come home with an eye missing and a treaty. The masks were wax and would melt in a fire; they were also, in the strange arithmetic of Roman memory, more real than the men had been. On feast days a slave was paid to open the cupboards and anoint the masks with oil, and the whole atrium smelled of wax and old wood and the particular dust of a family that had been important for four hundred years.

Sulpicia Varia had the masks polished twice a year. She had done it herself when she was younger. Now she had a woman in to do it, and she sat in her chair and watched, and complained about the woman's technique.

She was sixty-seven and dying, though the household had agreed not to say so. The growth had begun in her breast two winters before, and the physician had wanted to cut it out, and she had refused, and then she had allowed him to cut into it, and then it had come back, and now it was a hard, hot thing under her collar that she carried around with her like a purse she did not intend to open. She had grown thin. Her face had become all bone. But her eyes were the same as they had always been, pale and cold and entirely without self-deception, and when she rang the little silver bell beside her chair, the whole house came running.

On the morning after the Kalends, the house came running.

Her son came first, because he was the eldest and because he had learned, at an early age, that the cost of not coming was higher than the cost of coming. He came in his formal tunic, with his hair combed and his chin shaved, and he stood at the foot of her couch and waited to be told what he already knew.

"You were seen talking to her," Sulpicia Varia said.

"Yes."

"In the portico of the library. In front of half the Senate."

"There were three senators and a man from the treasury. The rest was crowd."

"Three senators is a crowd in this city. Three senators is a pamphlet." She shifted in her chair, and the movement cost her, and she did not allow it to show. "What did she want?"

"She wanted nothing. She was there because she had not been invited."

"And you spoke to her because?"

"Because she is a source of information."

"She is a source of something." The old woman's mouth was a thin line. "You are forty-five years old, Lucius. You have a wife, a son, a daughter, and a career that is not yet over. You have the augurate and the consular rank and the Asian command within your reach. And you are standing in a public portico talking to a woman who could destroy you with a single sentence, and you are telling me it is because she is informed."

"She is informed. She knows about Felix."

"Then pay Felix."

"Felix wants three hundred thousand."

"Then pay him three hundred thousand."

"I do not have three hundred thousand."

Sulpicia Varia looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed, a dry, papery sound that turned into a cough. The slave behind her chair moved forward and she waved him away.

"You do not have three hundred thousand," she said. "You have a house on the Palatine that is falling down, a vineyard in Etruria that has not made a profit since the drought, a son who owes money to half the moneylenders in the city, and a daughter who must be married in the spring to a man whose family will want a dowry that you do not have. And you have the Asian command, which would pay for all of it and which you will not get if this accusation reaches the consul. And you are telling me you cannot find three hundred thousand sesterces."

"I am telling you I cannot find them honestly."

"Honestly." She said the word as though it were a bad smell. "Your father would have found them."

"My father," Corvinus said, "would have found them by selling someone."

"Yes," his mother said. "He would. And the house would still be standing, and the name would still mean something, and I would not be lying in this chair watching my son lose everything his grandfathers built because he has developed a conscience at the age of forty-five."

The room was silent. The slave by the door studied the floor. Outside, in the corridor, a servant went past with a basket of linen, and the sound of her feet was very loud.

"I have not developed a conscience," Corvinus said, at last. "I have developed a memory. They are not the same."

"They are worse." His mother closed her eyes. "Send for the steward. Tell him to sell the Etrurian vineyard."

"It is the last thing we have."

"It is a vineyard. I am the last thing we have." She opened her eyes. "And tell your wife I want to see her. And send the girl to me. She has been at the mirror again, and I will not have her married with paint on her face like a Greek."

He went out. In the corridor he stood for a moment with his hand against the wall, and the cold of the stone went through his palm and up his arm. He thought about the portico, and the noise of the crowd, and the way she had said *I was once sold, and no one came,* which she had not said to him, but which he had heard, somehow, in the middle of everything else. He thought about the library and the smell of cedar and the twenty-four years.

Then he straightened his tunic and went to find the steward.

The women of the house were in the small room off the garden, where the sun came in through a window of clear glass and the loom stood against the wall. Aemilia Celsa was at the loom, because she was always at the loom in the morning, and because a woman who is always at the loom cannot be accused of idleness. She had been weaving the same length of cloth for a month. It was a good cloth, the colour of wheat, and it would make a fine mantle for her husband, and she had no intention of finishing it.

Sulpicia Varia Minor was on the stool by the window with a book in her lap. She was fifteen and had her grandmother's bones and her father's eyes and a mouth that was always on the edge of something. She was not reading the book. She was watching the garden, where a blackbird was pulling at the ivy, and she was thinking about the Lupercalia, and about a boy.

"Your grandmother wants you," Aemilia said, without looking up.

"I know."

"Then go."

"In a moment."

"Now."

Sulpicia put the book down and went, and Aemilia wove four more lines of the wheat-coloured cloth before she set the shuttle down and said, to the empty room, "She is going to be a great deal of trouble."

The girl came back a quarter of an hour later with her face washed and her hair bound and a sulky set to her shoulders that meant her grandmother had been at her. She sat down on the stool again and said, "She wants me to marry Aulus Proculus."

Aemilia did not stop weaving. "Does she."

"She says he is steady. She says his father is a fool but the son will inherit, and a fool's son is better than a clever man's daughter, whatever that means."

"It means she has been talking to the lawyers."

"I do not want to marry Aulus Proculus."

"Then you had better find a husband who will be more useful to your father and less annoying to you, and you had better do it quickly." Aemilia looked up. "You are fifteen. You have perhaps a year before the choice is made for you. If you want a say in it, you will have to be cleverer than the choice."

The girl stared at her. "Did you choose my father?"

"No."

"Do you regret it?"

Aemilia considered the question longer than a mother should. "I regret very little," she said at last. "It is a luxury I was not raised to afford. Your father is a decent man, and a proud one, and he has spent his life trying to be the man his father was and the man his mother wants, and he has failed at both. That is not the worst thing a husband can be." She picked up the shuttle. "If you want a reason to dislike him, you will find one. Everyone does. But do not pretend it is his fault that the world is arranged the way it is. It is not his fault. He merely profits from it, which is worse, and which he knows."

Sulpicia was silent. Then she said, "The woman at the forum. The one he was talking to."

"What about her?"

"Grandmother says she is a whore."

"Your grandmother says a great many things." Aemilia's hands moved. "The woman is a freedwoman with a fortune and a house full of senators. She is not received in any house on this hill, and every man on this hill has been to hers. That is not a whore. That is a mirror. When you are older, you will understand the difference, and you will find that the mirror is more frightening."

"Are you afraid of her?"

Aemilia looked at her daughter for a long moment. "Yes," she said. "But not for the reason you think."

In the afternoon Corvinus went down into the Forum to see a man about a loan. The man was a banker named Sittius, who had a stall under the Basilica Julia and a face like a closed door, and who had lent money to three generations of Sulpicii and had been repaid by two of them. The negotiation took an hour and ended with a rate of interest that was only slightly worse than ruin.

Coming out, Corvinus found his son waiting for him.

Publius Sulpicius Corvinus was twenty-one, and he had his father's height and his mother's colouring and none of either's restraint. He was dressed in the newest fashion, which meant his tunic was too fine for the season and his cloak was too short for the weather, and he had spent the morning, Corvinus suspected, doing something expensive.

"Father."

"Publius."

"I need to speak with you."

"You need money."

"That too." The boy fell into step beside him. They walked along the edge of the forum, past the stalls of the money changers and the lawyers and the men selling hot wine, and the boy talked, and the father listened, and neither of them looked at the other.

"It is the Asian command," Publius said. "Everyone says you will have it. Pactus is telling everyone you will not. He says a man with a son like you cannot be trusted with a province."

"And what does he mean by a son like me?"

"He means my debts. He means the gambling. He means the girl in the Subura." The boy's voice was carefully light. "He means that I am not serious. That is the word he uses. Not serious."

"And are you?"

"I am twenty-one."

"So was I, once."

"Yes, and you were serious. You were serious because grandfather made you serious. He made you stand in the atrium and look at the masks and tell him what each one had done for Rome, and if you got it wrong he beat you." Publius laughed, without humour. "I have heard the story. Everyone has heard the story. You tell it at dinner when you have had wine, and everyone laughs, and mother looks at the table."

Corvinus stopped walking. They were in the shadow of the basilica, and the noise of the forum went on around them, and a pigeon was pecking at something on the pavement between their feet.

"Your grandfather," he said, "was a hard man."

"He was a monster."

"He was a Roman of the old school, and he believed that the family was more important than any man in it, and he lived by that belief, and he made other people live by it, and he died by it." Corvinus's voice was flat. "I did not beat you. Perhaps that was my mistake. I have spent my life trying to be a different sort of father, and I have produced a son who thinks that a debt is a thing that happens to him."

Publius's face flushed. "I did not come here to be lectured."

"Then why did you come?"

"Because I need thirty thousand sesterces and you are the only man in Rome who can get them without asking me what they are for."

"What are they for?"

"For a girl."

Corvinus looked at him. His son looked back, defiant and frightened and twenty-one, and for a moment, in the middle of the forum, with the pigeons and the money changers and the noise, Corvinus saw himself at twenty-one in a garden at Tibur with his hands full of roses and a girl's laughter in his ear, and he felt the ground move under him.

"What girl?"

"None of your business."

"Publius."

"She is not a respectable girl. She is a girl in a house. Not that kind of house," he added, quickly, seeing his father's face. "A house of women. A decent house, kept by a freedman named Scaurus. Her mother is dead. She is free, and she has been promised to a sailor in Ostia, and the sailor has paid a deposit, and if I do not pay the rest he will take her." He stopped. "I have made a mess of it."

"Yes," Corvinus said. "You have."

They stood in the shadow of the basilica and looked at each other.

"I will not give you thirty thousand sesterces to buy a girl," Corvinus said. "I will give you ten thousand to pay your debts, and you will come with me to the moneylender and you will sign the agreement in your own name, and if you default he will take your horse and your rings and your cloak, and you will walk home in your tunic, and the whole city will see you do it."

"Father—"

"That is the offer."

The boy's jaw worked. Then he said, "And the girl?"

"The girl is not a transaction. If she is free, she cannot be bought. If her mother has promised her to a man in Ostia, then the promise is the business of her family and not of yours. If you want her, you will have to be a man who is worth wanting, and that is not something I can buy for you."

Publius stared at him. Then he laughed, a short, bitter laugh, and said, "You sound like mother."

"I have been told worse."

"No," the boy said, and turned away. "No, you have not."

He walked off into the crowd. Corvinus watched him go, and then he stood for a while in the shadow of the basilica, with the noise of the city going on around him, until a clerk came up and said, "Senator? The banker is waiting for the signature."

He signed. The rate was worse than he had feared and better than he had deserved. He came out of the banker's stall with the tablet under his arm and walked up the hill through the Forum and the Velia, past the arch of Titus and the vendors of fruit and the men selling copies of the new decree, and by the time he reached the Palatine his legs ached and his mind had gone quiet in the way it did after a day of small humiliations.

The house was cold. It was always cold in January. His mother kept her rooms warm out of a sense of her own importance, but the rest of the house was heated only when there were guests, and there were rarely guests now. He went through the atrium without looking at the masks, which was a habit he had developed as a boy and had never broken, and found his wife in the garden, wrapped in a mantle, watching the light go out of the sky.

"You are late," Aemilia said.

"The banker wanted to explain himself. Bankers always want to explain themselves. It is how they justify the interest."

"Did you get it?"

"Some of it. At a rate that would make a Carthaginian blush." He sat down on the bench beside her, and she moved a little to make room, which was the closest thing to affection that had passed between them in a week. "Publius came to see me."

"I know. He came home in a temper and kicked the door of his room and then asked the cook for bread and cheese, which is his way of apologising." She looked at him. "What did you say to him?"

"I told him I would not buy him a girl."

"Good."

"You do not ask who she is."

"I do not need to. He is twenty-one and he has been in love four times this year, and each time it has been the end of the world and each time the world has survived." Aemilia drew her mantle closer. "What else?"

"I told him he should be worth wanting."

She turned her head. "That was either very cruel or very kind."

"I do not know which. I have never known which." He looked at his hands. "My father would have beaten him. My father would have beaten him and then bought the girl and then beaten him again for the expense. And the terrible thing is that the house would have been stronger for it. The boy would have learned to be afraid, and fear makes men careful, and careful men keep their money. I have not beaten him, and he is not careful, and I am borrowing money at twelve per cent from a man who used to sell olives in the Subura."

"Your father is dead," Aemilia said. "You cannot keep measuring yourself against a dead man. He will always win. He has the advantage of not having to live with the consequences."

Corvinus laughed, once, without much sound. "You sound like her."

"I know. It is why I am still here." She said it without malice. "Your mother sent for me this morning. She wants the girl married to Aulus Proculus by the summer. She wants the vineyard sold. She wants the Asian command and she wants the accusation dealt with and she wants the masks polished before the Kalends of February, and she wants it all done by other people, because she cannot do it herself anymore."

"She is dying."

"Yes."

"She will not admit it."

"No. She will not." Aemilia was quiet for a moment. "I have been in this house for nineteen years. I came here at eighteen and I did not know what a family was. I thought it was a thing you joined, like a priesthood or a guild. I thought there would be rules and I would learn them and then I would belong." She looked at the dark garden. "There are no rules. There is only your mother, and the masks, and the money. And now there is this accusation, which no one will explain to me, and a woman on the Esquiline whom no one will name, and a husband who goes to the forum in the morning and comes home at night with a face like a closed book."

"I am sorry," Corvinus said.

"I did not ask for an apology. I asked for the truth." She turned to him. "Whatever it is, I can bear it. I have borne worse than the truth. What I cannot bear is being the last person in my own house to know it."

He looked at her. In the fading light, with her hair coming down and her mantle clutched at her throat, she looked like a woman who had been beautiful once and had put the beauty away in a chest, and had never taken it out again because there was no occasion to.

"Not tonight," he said.

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only one I have."

She rose. "Then it will have to do." At the door to the house she stopped. "Lucius. The girl. If she is not to marry Aulus Proculus, she will have to marry someone. Find her someone. Not for the family. For her. There is a difference, and it will matter to her, and it may one day matter to you."

She went inside. Corvinus stayed in the garden until it was fully dark, and the slaves came out with the lamps, and the house on the Palatine settled into the night with the sound of old stone cooling and an old woman coughing in her room above.

The house on the Esquiline had a visitor that night, and Cassia, who had been expecting one, was not surprised when Bathyllus came to the tablinum with his face set in the expression he reserved for young men with money.

"There is a party at the door," he said.

"Whose?"

"The Sulpicius boy. And two others. They have been drinking, and one of them has brought a jar of wine as a gift, and they are all wearing their cloaks like men who have never been told no."

Cassia put down her pen. She had been going over the accounts with Hermeros, and the lamp was low, and her eyes were tired, and she had spent the whole day thinking about a midwife in Tibur and a ring with two snakes on it.

"Where are they now?"

"In the street. The boy sent his name up as though it would open the door by itself."

"Did it?"

"Not yet." Bathyllus's mouth twitched. "I thought you would want to decide."

"Bring them in. The boy only. The others can wait in the garden and be looked at."

"And the wine?"

"Take it and thank them. It is probably the best thing they own." She stood and smoothed her stola. "And Bathyllus—"

"Yes?"

"Put them in the small dining room, not the painted one. Let them see the house but not the Graces. I want the boy to understand that there is a part of the house he has not been invited into."

The young man who came into the small dining room ten minutes later was handsome and drunk and trying to hide both. He had his father's height and his father's eyes, and he had the particular swagger of a boy who has been told all his life that the name he carries is a shield. He stopped in the doorway and looked at Cassia, and his face did something complicated.

"Domina," he said, and bowed too low.

"You are Publius Sulpicius Corvinus."

"I am."

"Then you are welcome in my house." She gestured to the couch. "Sit. You have come a long way, and you have brought wine, which is more than most men bring."

He sat. A slave brought cups, and the boy poured for both of them, and his hands were not quite steady. He drank too fast, and then he said, "You know my father."

"I know of your father."

"You know him."

The room was quiet. In the garden, one of his friends laughed, loudly, and was hushed.

"Yes," Cassia said. "I know him."

"He does not know I am here."

"I had assumed not."

"He would be angry." The boy turned his cup. "He is always angry, but he is quiet about it. He has made a career of being quiet about it. You know what he is like. You must know. Everyone knows what he is like except him."

"What is he like?"

"He is a man who has never made a mistake in his life, and who cannot understand why everyone else keeps making them." Publius drank. "He gave me ten thousand sesterces today and made me sign a paper. He thinks that is generosity. He thinks that is being a good father. He thinks that if he is careful enough and quiet enough and respectable enough, the world will not notice that the whole thing is a lie."

Cassia set her cup down. "What whole thing?"

The boy looked at her. The drink was in him, and the anger, and something else — a hurt that had been growing in him for a long time, like a tooth.

"The house," he said. "The name. The masks. My grandmother sitting in her chair talking about honour. My mother weaving the same cloth every day of her life. My father going down to the forum to borrow money from a man he despises so that he can keep up a house that is falling down, and then coming home and telling me that a man must live by the truth." He laughed. "The truth. The truth is that this city is a game, and the people who win are the ones who admit it, and my father has spent his whole life pretending not to play."

"And you?" Cassia said. "Are you winning?"

The boy's face changed. For a moment he looked very young, and very like his father, and not at all like a man who thought the city was a game.

"I came here," he said, "because I wanted to see what it looked like."

"What what looked like?"

"Your house. You." He looked around the room. "My father will not say your name. My grandmother prays about you. My mother mentions you at dinner as though you were a disease. I have lived my whole life in a house where you are a thing that is not spoken of, and today I saw my father stand in a public portico and talk to you as if the whole world were not watching, and I thought — I thought I should see for myself."

"And what do you see?"

He looked at her for a long time. The lamp was between them, and the wine, and the noise of his friends in the garden, and the whole weight of the house above and around them.

"I see a woman," he said, "who is not a monster."

"No," Cassia said. "I am not."

"Then why does he hate you?"

The question was so naked, and so young, that she almost answered it. She looked at the boy's face, which was his father's face, and at his hands, which were not his father's hands, and she thought: he does not know. He does not know anything. He has been raised in that cold house with the masks on the wall and the mother in the chair, and no one has ever told him the truth about anything, and he is drowning in it.

"Your father does not hate me," she said. "He hates what I know."

"What do you know?"

"Everything," Cassia said, "that your family has spent a great deal of money to forget."

The boy stared at her. Then he said, quietly, "Tell me."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because it is not mine to tell, and because you are drunk, and because the telling of it would cost more than you can pay tonight." She stood. "Go home. Tell your friends the evening was a success and the lady of the house was beautiful and nothing happened. It is the most useful lie you will tell this year."

He did not move. "If I come back? Sober?"

"Then I will consider it."

He rose, unsteadily. At the door he turned and said, "My father said something today. He said I should be a man who is worth wanting. What does that mean? What does it mean to be worth wanting?"

Cassia looked at him. In the lamplight, with his cloak askew and his face open, he looked like a boy who had come a long way to ask a question and had been given a riddle instead.

"It means," she said, "that you should find out what you are worth and then make sure it is true. Most men never do. They spend their lives being told what they are worth by other people, and they believe it, and they die believing it. Your father is one of them. He is worth a great deal to the Senate and the army and the name on the wall, and he is worth almost nothing to himself, and he has never once noticed the difference."

The boy's face worked. Then he bowed, and went out, and she heard him collect his friends in the garden, and the three of them go laughing and stumbling down the Clivus Suburanus into the dark.

Bathyllus came in to clear the cups.

"Domina."

"Yes?"

"The boy's father will hear of this. He has eyes on the hill, and eyes in the street, and the boy is not careful."

"I know."

"And the boy knows something, or he thinks he does. He will talk."

"He will not talk. He is too frightened of his father." Cassia picked up her pen. "But he will come back. And when he does, he will be sober, and he will ask me again, and I will have to decide what to tell him."

She looked down at the accounts. The figures swam. She set the pen aside and sat for a moment with her hands flat on the desk, and then she said, "Bathyllus, send word to Tibur. Tomorrow. Not through Hermeros. Through the woman who sells flowers in the Subura. Tell the midwife that I will come before the spring, and that she is to stay alive until I do."

"Domina."

"And Bathyllus—" She looked up. "The boy is not to be admitted again. Not unless I say so. Not unless I am here."

"Yes, domina."

She was alone then, with the lamp and the accounts and the ring on the desk before her, two snakes twined around a staff, worn thin by twenty-four years of someone else's hand.
