Tibur, in the summer of the year eighty-eight.

The villa stood on the shoulder of the hill above the falls, where the Anio came down out of the hills and broke itself white on the rocks and then ran green and slow through the meadows below. The house was old and cool and full of the sound of water. It had a portico with a vine on it and a garden of clipped box and a small bathhouse with a mosaic of dolphins that had been put in by the great-grandfather and repaired by every generation since. From the terrace you could see the whole wide plain and, on a clear day, the smoke of Rome, thirty miles off, hanging over the city like a stain.

Cassia had come to Tibur with the household in the month of June, as she had come every summer since she was twelve. She was eighteen. She was a maid to Sulpicia Varia, which meant that she carried the mistress's fan and her medicine box and her writing things, and stood behind her chair in the afternoons, and was invisible in the way that a good maid is invisible, which is to say that she saw everything and was seen by no one.

She had been born in Rome, in a room over a laundry near the Circus, to a woman named Daphne who had washed clothes for the Sulpicii and had died when Cassia was nine. She did not remember much of her mother. She remembered the smell of lye and lavender, and a song about a boat, and hands that were always red and cracked and always gentle. When Daphne died, the household had kept the child, because a child is an asset and the Sulpicii had never thrown away an asset. She had worked in the kitchen, then in the linen, then in the mistress's rooms, and she had learned to read and write from the steward's clerk, who had taught her in exchange for nothing more than her company and the occasional slice of bread and honey, and who had died of a fever in the year eighty-five and left her the notebook he had copied his accounts into.

The family came to Tibur in June, and the young master came in July.

Lucius Sulpicius Corvinus was twenty. He had been away for two years — a military tribune with the legion in Syria, then a winter in Athens, then a slow road home through the islands — and he came up the hill from the road in the late afternoon with dust on his boots and a sunburn on his neck and the look of a man who has discovered that the world is larger than the house he was raised in. He was tall and lean and there was a new scar on his forearm and a new way of standing. Cassia saw him from the terrace, where she was shaking out the mistress's cushions, and he looked up and saw her, and neither of them spoke.

That evening there was a dinner. The father presided: Marcus Sulpicius Corvinus, fifty-three, consul twice, a man with a face like a coin that had been in circulation too long. He had the family's height and none of its grace. He was a hard man and a proud one, and he had spent his whole life in the conviction that the world was divided into men who were equal to him and men who were not, and that the second category was very large and the first very small. His wife sat at his right, silent and beautiful, with her fan and her medicine box and her pale eyes. She was forty-three that summer. She looked ten years younger, and she had the kind of composure that comes from never having been contradicted by anyone who mattered.

They talked about the war in the north, and the emperor's temper, and the price of the villa's wine. The young master said little. He ate slowly and looked at the garden and once, when the conversation turned to the household and the steward listed the slaves who had been bought and sold that spring, he looked at his father with an expression that Cassia, standing behind the mistress's chair with the fan, could not read.

After dinner he found her in the garden.

"You are Daphne's girl," he said.

"I am."

"I remember you. You used to bring the water to the men in the vineyard."

"You used to drink it and say thank you."

"Did I?"

"Every time. The others did not."

He looked at her for a long moment. The light was going, and the cicadas were loud, and the water from the falls came up the hill as a low, continuous sound, like a crowd very far away.

"I am sorry about your mother," he said.

"Thank you."

"I was at school in Rome when she died. I heard about it. My mother said she had been a good servant." He paused. "It seemed a small thing to say."

"It is a small thing," Cassia said. "It is also more than most people said."

He laughed. It was a surprised laugh, a young man's laugh, and it changed his face entirely. "You are not what I expected."

"What did you expect?"

"I do not know. A maid."

"I am a maid."

"Yes," he said. "That is what surprised me."

***

The summer went like this: a garden, a fountain, a boy who came to the kitchen for bread and stayed to talk. It was not a seduction. That is what Cassia would have said afterward, if anyone had asked her, and no one did for twenty-four years. It was not a seduction, because a seduction is a thing one person does to another, and this was a thing that happened to both of them at once, the way a rainstorm happens, the way a fever happens.

He found excuses. He came to the kitchen for water, for bread, for the key to the bathhouse, for a message he could have sent a slave for. He sat on the bench by the door and watched her work and talked about Syria, about the desert, about the strange gods he had seen and the men he had met and the letters he had tried to write home and torn up. He asked about her, and listened to the answers, which was the most dangerous thing of all. He asked what she thought of the poems of Catullus, which he had been reading in Athens. She had not read Catullus. The next day there was a copy of Catullus in the kitchen, left on the bench as though it had grown there.

She read it at night by the lamp in the slaves' corridor, and when she came to the poem about the girl's sparrow she cried, and she could not have told anyone why.

He kissed her first in the store-room, between the jars of oil and the baskets of figs, in the hot dead hour of the afternoon when the whole house was asleep. It was a clumsy kiss, and brief, and afterward he stepped back and said, "I should not have done that," and she said, "No," and neither of them moved, and then he did it again.

They met in the vineyard, in the little stone hut where the pruner kept his tools. They met in the bathhouse after the house was asleep, in the cold pool, with the dolphins looking at them from the wall. They met in the meadow below the falls, in the long grass, with the water roaring and the sun on their backs, and he traced the scar on her thumb with his finger and asked where she had got it, and she told him, and he said, "You will never have another scar like that. I will see to it." He said it with the absolute seriousness of a boy of twenty, and she believed him, because she was eighteen, and because the light was gold, and because no one had ever promised her anything.

He told her about his father. He told her in pieces, in the dark, with his head in her lap. He told her about the atrium and the masks and the beatings that were not beatings but "lessons," and the way his father could make a room cold by walking into it, and the way his mother had learned to be a wall. He told her that he was afraid of becoming his father, and she said that he would not, and he said, "How do you know?" and she said, "Because you are afraid of it. Your father is not afraid of anything."

She did not tell him about herself. There was not much to tell. She had no story, only a life: the laundry, the kitchen, the corridor, the lamp, the book. She told him about her mother's song and about the steward's clerk who had taught her letters, and he listened as though these were the adventures of Odysseus.

The mistress watched them. Cassia knew it, the way one knows that a spider is in the corner of a room. Sulpicia Varia said nothing, did nothing, gave no sign. She sent Cassia to the village with a message and sent the young master to the vineyards with the steward, and arranged the days so that they were never alone in the house at the same time, and it made no difference at all.

One afternoon in the middle of August she kept Cassia behind after the other maids had gone and made her sit on the stool by the window and read to her from a book of letters. It was a book of Pliny's letters, and the one she chose was about a man who had lost his wife and could not stop writing about her.

"Read that again," the mistress said, when she had finished.

Cassia read it again.

"Now tell me what it is about."

"A man who is grieving."

"No," Sulpicia Varia said. "It is about a man who wants everyone to know that he is grieving. There is a difference. The first man weeps in the dark and says nothing. The second man writes a letter and has it copied and sends it to eleven friends." She took the book back and closed it. "You are clever. Cleverer than the maids I have had. Cleverer, I sometimes think, than my son."

"I am not, domina."

"You are. He is good and he is honest and he is not clever, and that is why he will be eaten alive in this city unless he learns to be quieter than he is." She looked out of the window at the garden. "You are clever, and you are pretty, and you are a slave, and those three things together are the most dangerous combination a woman can have. Do you know what happens to women like that?"

"No, domina."

"Either they are destroyed, or they learn to be the ones who destroy." The mistress turned back to her, and her pale eyes were steady. "I have been watching you since you were twelve. You have a good heart and a bad temper and you do not forget anything. Those are useful things. They will not save you, but they are useful." She stood. "Go and tell the cook that I want the fish plain tonight, with no sauce. He will be offended. Let him be offended. It is good for cooks."

Cassia went. At the door the mistress said, "And Cassia. Do not be alone with my son in the afternoons. The house is not as empty as it looks."

In the last week of August she missed her bleeding. She did not think about it at first. Then she thought about it, and counted, and thought about it again, and one morning she was sick in the gutter behind the kitchen, and Melissa, the under-cook, a sharp-eyed woman who had seen everything twice and been surprised by none of it, looked at her and said nothing, and then, later, left a cup of mint tea on the bench where Cassia would find it.

She told him on the terrace, at night, with the plain dark below them and the smoke of Rome gone and the stars very close.

He was quiet for a long time. She watched his face and saw, for the first time, the thing that she would see again and again for twenty-four years: the way he went still when he was afraid. Not pale. Not shaking. Still, like a man in a temple waiting for the augury.

"Then we will be married," he said.

"Your father will never permit it."

"Then we will go away."

"Where?"

"Anywhere. I have money of my own — not much, but enough. We could go to Syria. I have friends there. There is a farm near Antioch that belongs to a man who owes me a favour." He took her hands. "Cassia. Look at me. I am not my father. I will not do what he would do."

"I know," she said. She did not know. But she wanted to say something that would make it true.

They had four days. On the fifth day the steward came for her in the middle of the afternoon and said that the master wished to see her in the tablinum.

***

Marcus Sulpicius Corvinus was standing at the window with his hands behind his back. He did not turn when she came in. The room was hot and smelled of ink and old paper and the wax of the seals. There was a bust of his father on the desk, and a letter open beside it, and a cup of wine that had not been touched.

"Close the door," he said.

She closed it.

"You have been fucking my son."

The word was not shouted. It was placed on the desk like a coin, precisely, in the tone of a man who has already decided what everything is worth.

"I have not—"

"Do not." He turned. His face was calm. "I have eyes in this house. I have had eyes in this house since before you were born. The gardener saw you in the vineyard. The bath slave saw you in the pool. The girl who does the linen saw you on the terrace at night with your hands in his hair." He came around the desk. "You are pregnant."

She said nothing.

"You are pregnant," he said again, "and you will tell me the truth, or I will have the physician examine you in front of the household, and then I will have you whipped for insolence, and then I will sell you to a man I know in Puteoli who buys girls for his house and does not ask them questions."

"Yes," Cassia said. "I am pregnant."

"By my son."

"Yes."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he sat down behind the desk and poured himself a cup of wine and drank it off, and set the cup down, and said, in the same calm voice, "You have been in this house since you were twelve. You have been fed and clothed and taught to read, which was not my doing and which I have always thought was a mistake. My wife is fond of you, in her way. You have had a better life than most of the people in that kitchen, and you have had it because we are a family that takes care of its own." He looked up. "And this is how you repay us."

"I love him."

"You are a slave."

"He loves me."

"My son is twenty years old and has been in the East for two years and has never in his life been told no by a woman. He does not love you. He wants you. There is a difference, and it is the difference between a man and a boy." He reached for the letter on the desk. "He will be sent to Syria at the end of the month. There is a staff posting with the governor. I had intended to refuse it. I will accept it now."

Cassia stood very still. The room was hot. Somewhere outside, a bird was calling, over and over, the same three notes.

"And me?"

"You will be sold. Not here — there is no market at Tibur, and I will not have the household talking. You will go to Puteoli, to a woman named Lusca, who keeps a house and is discreet, and who will pay me a fair price for a girl with a pretty face and a child in her belly. She will take the child off your hands. It will be exposed, or sold, or given to a farm; I do not care which. You will not see it." He set the letter down. "That is the arrangement. You may go."

She did not move. She was eighteen years old, and she had been a slave all her life, and she had learned in that life that there is a moment in every conversation when a person with power has finished speaking and is waiting for you to be afraid. She had seen it a hundred times. She had never been on the other side of it before.

"Does your wife know?" she said.

His eyes narrowed. "What?"

"Does she know about Puteoli? About Lusca? About the child?"

"That is not your concern."

"She will find out. She always finds out. She knew about the girl in the kitchen at Rome, the one you sent away in the spring. She knew about the wine merchant's daughter. She knows everything, and she says nothing, and one day she will say something, and it will not be to you."

The blow came fast and flat, an open hand across the face, and it knocked her against the door. She tasted blood. She stood up, and put her hand to her mouth, and looked at him.

"Get out," he said.

She got out.

***

Sulpicia Varia came to her that night, in the corridor by the linen room, with a lamp in her hand.

Cassia was sitting on the floor with her back against the wall. Her face was swelling. She had been sitting there for three hours, because there was nowhere else to go, and because the house was small and every corner of it had a door that led to a room where she had been happy.

The mistress stood over her. She was in her nightgown, with her hair down, and without her jewels and her fan she looked smaller and older and strangely ordinary.

"He has told you," she said.

"Yes."

"And you believe him?"

"Why would he lie?"

"He would not lie. He is many things, but he is not a liar." Sulpicia Varia set the lamp down on the floor between them. "My husband has made arrangements. I have come to make a different one."

Cassia looked up at her.

"I have no power in this house," the mistress said, "except the power of a woman who has been married to that man for twenty-six years and has learned where the keys are kept and which of the servants can be trusted and which of the physicians is in debt. I cannot stop him selling you. I cannot stop him sending my son away. But I can make it easier." She crouched down, her skirts folding on the stone, and her face came into the lamplight. "I can make it quick. There is a draught I can give you, on the night it happens. You will sleep. You will not feel the birth as you would otherwise feel it, and you will not remember much of it afterward. And when you wake, it will be over, and you will be on the road, and you will not have to carry the memory of it for the rest of your life."

"Why would you do that?"

"Because you are a person," Sulpicia Varia said, "and because I have watched you grow up in this house, and because I have no daughter of my own, and because I do not wish to think of you in that room at Puteoli remembering what was done to you here." She paused. "And because I am a practical woman, and a girl who screams is a girl who is remembered, and I do not want this house remembered."

Cassia looked at her. In the lamplight the mistress's face was kind, and tired, and entirely sincere.

"Will the child be buried?" Cassia said.

"Yes."

"Here? In the garden?"

"By the fig tree. I will see to it myself."

"Will you tell me when it is done?"

"I will tell you," Sulpicia Varia said. "I will tell you before you go."

She reached out and took Cassia's hand. Her own hand was warm and dry and very steady.

"Drink what I give you," she said. "Drink all of it. And when you wake, do not look back."

***

They came for her in the last week of September, on a night with no moon.

She had been having pains since the afternoon. The physician had been and gone, and the midwife had come at dusk with her bag and her bronze ring and her quiet, competent hands. Psyche was sixty that year, a small woman with grey hair and a face like a walnut, and she had delivered half the babies in the village and had never lost a mother. She put her hand on Cassia's belly and said, "Soon. Before morning. You will have an easy one; I can tell." And Cassia, who had been told that nothing about her life would ever be easy, had almost laughed.

The room was a cell at the back of the villa, next to the storeroom. It had a bed and a stool and a small high window, and the door had a bolt on the outside. There was a lamp on the stool and a basin on the floor, and the walls were whitewashed, and someone had scratched a fish into the plaster, a child's drawing, very old.

Sulpicia Varia came at the ninth hour with a cup in her hand.

It was a small cup, silver, and the liquid in it was dark and smelled of honey and something bitter underneath.

"Drink it now," she said. "All of it. It will make the pains easier, and it will help you sleep between them."

Cassia took the cup. She was eighteen years old and in pain and frightened, and the woman who had been kind to her was standing over her with a lamp, and outside the door a man was waiting to put her on a cart in the morning.

She drank it.

It tasted of honey and then of almonds, and then of nothing at all.

The room went soft at the edges. The lamp became three lamps, and then a smear of gold. She heard the door open and close, and voices, and the midwife saying something in a low voice, and the mistress answering, and then a long silence in which the only sound was the blood moving in her own ears.

The pains came and went, and she floated above them, and at some point she was pushing, and the midwife was telling her to push, and there was a great pressure and a tearing and a wet rush, and then a silence that went on too long.

Then a cry. Thin and furious and alive.

"A boy," Psyche said. "A fine boy. Look at him."

Cassia tried to open her eyes. The room was dark and turning. She saw, through a haze, the midwife's hands, and something small and red and moving, and then a shadow came between her and the light.

"Give him to me," Sulpicia Varia said.

The midwife said something. Cassia could not hear the words. She heard the tone — a question, a protest.

"Give him to me," the mistress said again, "and remember what you were paid, and what you were told, and what will happen to you if you forget it."

The baby stopped crying. Cassia heard the small, wet, working sounds of a newborn being wrapped in cloth, and then the sounds were muffled, and then they were somewhere else in the room, and then they were at the door.

"Psyche," the mistress said. "The cup. All of it. And then you will go to the steward and tell him it is done, and you will say nothing to the girl, and in the morning you will tell her the child did not breathe."

"Domina, he is breathing now—"

"He will not be breathing by the time she wakes," Sulpicia Varia said, "because he will not be here. Do you understand me?"

"Give him to me," the mistress said again, "and do what you were paid to do."

And then Cassia's body, which had been holding her up out of the dark for a long time, let go, and she went down into it, and the last thing she heard was the crying, going away, getting smaller, until it was only a sound in a dream.

***

She woke in the morning to a grey light and a pain like a knife and the taste of almonds in her mouth.

The room was empty. The basin had been emptied and the floor washed. The lamp had been taken away, and the bed had been changed, and she was clean and dressed in a plain shift, and there was a cup of water on the stool.

She got up. It took a long time. She went to the door and found it unbolted, and she opened it, and the corridor was empty, and the storeroom door was open, and the morning light was coming in through the colonnade at the end.

Psyche was in the kitchen, drinking a bowl of broth. She looked up when Cassia came in, and her face did something complicated.

"Where is my son?" Cassia said.

The midwife set the bowl down.

"Domina," she said.

"Where is my son?"

"Stillborn," Psyche said. "He did not breathe. It happens. He was a fine boy, but he did not breathe, and the mistress took him to be buried."

"You said he cried."

"The medicine makes you dream." The midwife's eyes were steady. "You were dreaming. I am sorry. He did not breathe."

Cassia stood in the kitchen with her hand on the table. The room was warm. The fire was lit. Somewhere a dog was barking, and a woman was singing, and the world was going on exactly as it had gone on before.

"I want to see him," she said.

"He is buried."

"Then dig him up."

"Domina—"

"Dig him up. I want to see him. I want to see his face."

Psyche looked at her for a long moment. Then she said, "The mistress has given orders. You are to be on the cart by the third hour. If you are not on the cart, the steward has been told to tie you to it." She picked up her bowl again. "I am sorry. I have delivered a hundred babies in this village and I have never lost a mother, and I have lost three children, and it never gets easier, and there is nothing I can say to you that would help." She drank her broth. "Eat something. The road to Puteoli is long."

The cart came at the third hour. It was a farm cart, driven by a man who did not look at her, with a load of wine jars and a folded blanket on the boards. The steward walked her to it. The household watched from the doors and the windows — the cook, the gardener, the girl who did the linen, the boy who fed the pigeons. No one said anything. There was nothing to say that would not be reported.

The last thing she saw of Tibur was the fig tree at the corner of the garden, with the morning sun on it, and the earth below it disturbed.

She was in Puteoli by the evening of the second day. Lusca's house was three streets up from the harbour, with a green door and a lamp over it, and a woman with dyed hair and a fat, kind, entirely merciless face who looked her over the way a farmer looks at a horse.

"Eighteen," Lusca said. "Pretty. Not too thin. What did she have?"

"A boy," the driver said. "Stillborn."

"Good. No brats to worry about." She took Cassia's chin in her hand and turned her face to the light. "You will do. You will do very well. Get her inside and fed, and then we will see about the blood."

The green door closed. The bolt went across. Somewhere in the house a woman was laughing, and a man was shouting about a cup, and the sea was three streets away and could be heard in every room, like something enormous breathing in its sleep.

A woman came out of the kitchen with a bowl of soup and a hunk of bread and stood in front of her. She was small and brown and neat, and she looked at Cassia the way a physician looks at a wound, without pity and without disgust, and she said, "Eat."

Cassia did not move.

"Eat," the woman said again. "You have been on a cart for two days and you have a fever coming and if you do not eat you will be no use to anyone, including yourself." She put the bowl into Cassia's hands. "My name is Thais. I run the kitchen and the accounts and the girls when Lusca is drunk, which is most of the time. You will do as I say and you will not cry in front of the clients and you will not run, because there is a man at the back door with a stick and he is quicker than he looks." She turned to go, and then turned back. "And you will not believe anything they tell you in the first month. Not the men, not the girls, not the mistress. Nothing. Do you understand?"

"Yes," Cassia said.

"Good." Thais looked at her for a moment longer. "What was his name?"

Cassia's mouth opened. No sound came out.

"The child's name," Thais said. "Or the father's. Either."

"I do not know," Cassia said. "I never gave him one."

"Then give him one now," Thais said, "and keep it to yourself, and never say it out loud in this house. A name is the only thing they cannot take from you if you do not tell them what it is."

Cassia stood in the dark of the entrance hall with her hands at her sides and thought about the cry. She had heard it. She knew she had heard it. And for twenty-four years afterward, whenever she was very tired or very ill or very close to sleep, she would hear it again — thin and furious and alive, going away from her, getting smaller, until it was only a sound in a dream.
