The Lupercalia fell that year on a day of thin sunshine and a wind that came off the hills and smelled of snow. By the second hour the streets around the Palatine were packed, and by the third the whole lower city had poured into the Circus Valley and the Forum and the slopes of the hill, and Rome was doing what Rome did best, which was to turn a sacred rite into an excuse for a party.

The house had been awake since dawn. The women were doing one another's hair, which on a festival day was a serious business and involved a great deal of shouting. Celer had been up since the third hour making honey cakes for the household and a dish of lentils for the workers, because Celer believed that a festival without lentils was a pagan festival and a pagan festival was bad for the digestion. The kitchen boys had been given the morning off and had spent it in the street throwing nuts at a cat.

Chrysis had not wanted to go. She had been quiet since the Saturnalia, and quieter since the news from Tibur, and she stood in the doorway of the kitchen with her mantle already on and her face set in the expression of a woman who has decided to be brave in public and is not looking forward to it.

"You will go," Cassia said.

"I do not want to."

"You will go, and you will laugh at the men with the goatskin, and you will eat something, and you will come home tired, and tomorrow you will feel better. That is what festivals are for." Cassia straightened her collar. "And Chrysis. If anyone speaks to you in the street, you will be polite and you will keep walking."

"Who would speak to me?"

"Nobody. That is the point." Cassia looked at her. "The man who came about your brother has not come back. That is either because he was lying, or because he is waiting for you to become frightened enough to pay him. Either way, he will come back, and when he does, he will come to the house, and I will deal with him. You will not."

Chrysis nodded. At the door she stopped and said, without turning, "Domina. If my brother is alive, and if he is bought and brought here, what will he be? To me, I mean. Will he be my brother, or will he be a boy I have bought?"

Cassia was silent for a moment.

"He will be both," she said. "That is the difficulty."

Cassia did not go to the Lupercal. She had gone once, at nineteen, and had been struck across the back with a goatskin thong by a young man in a mask who had laughed and run on, and she had felt the sting of it for two days and had not become pregnant, and had drawn her own conclusions. But the house went. The house always went. Thais took the women in a body, with Bathyllus in front and two of the kitchen boys behind, and even the ones who had to work that night were allowed the morning, because a festival is a thing that belongs to everyone, including the people who have nothing else.

Sabina went with Thais. She had been given a new cloak and a handful of nuts and a mask of a laughing satyr that was too big for her, and she came back at the fifth hour with her mask askew and her hair full of straw and a look on her face that Cassia recognized, because it was the look of a child who has seen the city for the first time and has begun to understand that it is not a thing that has been arranged for her benefit.

"There were men with no clothes on," Sabina said.

"There are always men with no clothes on. It is the point of the festival."

"They had a goat. They cut its throat and smeared the blood on two boys and then they laughed and ran." Sabina's eyes were wide. "One of them had a knife and he was laughing. And a woman was screaming because a man had hit her with the thong and she was bleeding."

"Did she want to be hit?"

"I do not know. She was laughing too." Sabina frowned. "I did not understand it. Everyone was laughing and there was blood. Is that what a festival is?"

Cassia looked at her. The child was twelve years old and had just discovered the central fact of the city, which was that it was built out of things that were funny and things that were cruel and could not always tell them apart.

"Some of it," Cassia said. "The rest is bread and wine and the day off."

"I liked the part with the bread," Sabina said.

***

Cassia watched the festival from the roof.

The house had a flat roof over the kitchen wing, reached by a narrow stair and a door that was kept locked, and from it you could see the whole valley of the Circus and the slope of the Palatine with its palaces and its temples, and the smoke rising from a hundred cooking fires, and the river shining away to the west like a length of bronze. Tertulla came up to join her, and Hermeros, and Chrysis, who had been silent for days and who stood at the parapet with her arms wrapped around herself.

"They will be at the Lupercal now," Tertulla said. "The priests will be sacrificing a dog and a goat, and the young men will be anointed, and then they will run."

"I know how it goes."

"You have not told me why you are watching."

Cassia looked at the crowd. It was a moving thing, a single organism with a thousand heads, surging along the valley and up the Via Sacra and around the hill. You could see the bright spots in it: a woman's yellow mantle, a litter with a gilded pole, a group of youths in matching red cloaks carrying a goat's head on a pole. You could hear it as a single sound, a low roar with laughter in it, and occasionally a scream.

"I am watching for a girl," she said.

"Which girl?"

"The senator's daughter."

Tertulla came and stood beside her. "Why?"

"Because she will come."

"How do you know?"

"Because she is fifteen and her grandmother is dying and her mother is unhappy and her father has a secret, and because a girl in that house will do anything to find out what the secret is." Cassia's eyes moved over the crowd. "And because she has been told all her life that my house is a place of sin, and there is nothing more attractive to a clever girl than a sin she has been told about."

"Cassia. If that girl comes to your door—"

"She will not come to the door. She is too well brought up for that. She will come to the festival with a slave and she will slip away in the crowd and she will stand in the street and look at the house from a distance, and then she will go home and lie about it." Cassia pointed. "There. By the fountain. In the blue cloak, with the plain mask."

Tertulla looked. "How can you tell?"

"Because she is standing still. Everyone else is moving. A girl who has run away from her slave does not move; she stands and looks and tries to decide whether she has made a mistake."

The blue-cloaked figure was standing at the corner of the street by the fountain, a hundred paces away, with a cheap clay mask over her face and her hood up. She was alone. She was looking at the house, and then at the street, and then at the house again.

Cassia went down the stair.

***

She came out of the garden door into the alley and walked around to the front of the house, and the festival crowd filled the street and the noise was enormous. The girl saw her coming and did not move. She was tall for fifteen, with her hood up and the mask covering the top half of her face, and her hands were clasped in front of her in the way of a girl who has been taught to stand.

"You are lost," Cassia said.

"I am not."

"Then you are waiting for someone, and the someone has not come, and you have been standing here for a quarter of an hour, and your slave is looking for you in the crowd and will find you in a moment and will be very frightened and will tell your mother." Cassia smiled. "Take off the mask."

The girl did not move.

"Take it off," Cassia said, "or I will call the vigiles and tell them there is a young woman of good family alone in the street at a festival, and they will take you home to your father, and you will have to explain why you were standing outside my house."

The mask came off. The face underneath was young and fierce and frightened, with her father's eyes and her grandmother's bones, and a mouth that was trying very hard not to tremble.

"I am Sulpicia Varia," she said.

"I know."

"How do you know?"

"Because I have seen your father's face, and because you have his eyes, and because you are standing exactly the way he stands when he is trying not to be afraid." Cassia looked at her. "What do you want?"

The girl's chin came up. "I want to know what you did to my family."

The noise of the festival went on around them. A man with a drum went past, and a group of women with garlands, and a dog running with something in its mouth. Cassia looked at the girl in front of her and felt, absurdly, a kind of tenderness.

"Nothing," she said. "I have done nothing to your family. Your family has done a great deal to me."

"What does that mean?"

"It means that you are fifteen years old and you have been sent here by a curiosity you do not understand, and that if I told you the truth you would not sleep tonight, and you would go home and say something to your mother, and she would say something to your father, and the whole city would know by the Ides." Cassia took her arm. "Come. You cannot stand in the street. There is a wine shop on the corner with a room at the back, and the woman who owns it owes me a favour, and we will have a cup of hot wine and you will tell me what you think you know."

The girl came. She came because she was fifteen and because the alternative was to go home, and because Cassia's hand on her arm was firm and warm and entirely without menace.

The back room of the wine shop was small and dark and smelled of wine and wet wool and the sausages that were frying in the front. Cassia sat the girl down on a bench and ordered hot wine with honey and a plate of bread and olives, and the girl sat with her hands around the cup and looked at her.

"You are not what I expected," Sulpicia said.

"What did you expect?"

"A monster."

"I am a woman who runs a business. It is the same thing to some people." Cassia drank her wine. "Now. What do you think you know?"

"I know that my father goes to your house."

"Your father has never been to my house."

The girl's eyes widened. "But—"

"He has never been to my house. He came to a house on the Caelian, once, to speak to me about the accusation that is being prepared against him, and before that I spoke to him for a quarter of an hour in a public portico in front of half the Senate. That is all. Whatever you have heard, whatever your grandmother has said, whatever the servants whisper, that is the truth." Cassia set her cup down. "Your father is not my lover. He was, once, a very long time ago, before you were born, and it ended badly, and he has spent his life since then trying to be a man his father would have approved of, and I have spent my life trying to be a woman who does not need anyone's approval. That is the whole of it."

"That is not the whole of it."

"No," Cassia said. "It is not. But it is as much as you are going to get from me today."

The girl was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "My grandmother is dying."

"I know."

"She talks in her sleep. She says a name. Psyche. And she says, 'Tell her nothing.' And my mother went to the baths and came home and would not speak to anyone, and my father came in at midnight soaked to the skin and went to my grandmother's room and there was shouting, and then my grandmother was crying, and my grandmother has never cried in her life." Sulpicia's voice was steady, and her eyes were bright. "I am not a child. I am fifteen, and I am to be married next year to a man I have met twice, and I am to spend the rest of my life in a house like that house, with the masks on the wall and the lies in the corridors. I would like to know what I am marrying into."

Cassia looked at her for a long time. The girl looked back. She had courage; that was certain. Whether she had judgment was another matter, and judgment was the thing that could not be taught in an afternoon.

"Not today," Cassia said. "Today you will go home, and you will tell your slave that you were separated in the crowd and that you are sorry, and you will be forgiven because you are fifteen and pretty and your family is important. And then, if you still want to know, you will come to me on a day that is not a festival, with your mother's knowledge, and I will tell you what I can." She leaned forward. "But understand me, Sulpicia. What I tell you will not make you happy. It will not make your family better. It will not make your marriage easier. It will only mean that you know, and knowing is a burden, and once you have it you cannot put it down."

"Tell me about my grandmother," Sulpicia said. "Not the illness. The other thing. The thing no one will say."

"Your grandmother is the cleverest woman I have ever met and the most frightened," Cassia said. "She has spent her whole life making sure that nothing in that house is ever out of place. Not the masks, not the linen, not the marriages, not the servants. She believes that if everything is in its proper place, nothing bad can happen. And when something bad does happen, she puts it somewhere, and she shuts the door, and she never opens it again." She turned her cup. "You are like her. You have her bones and her courage and her hardness. You will need all three. But you will have to learn to open doors, or you will end up in a chair in a cold room, counting the linen, with a son who is afraid of you."

The girl stared at her. "You are not afraid of my grandmother."

"I am terrified of your grandmother."

"You do not sound it."

"Because I have spent twenty years learning not to sound afraid. It is a skill, like spinning. It can be learned." Cassia leaned back. "You are to marry Aulus Proculus. That is what your grandmother wants."

"How did you know?"

"Because his father is a fool and his mother is dead and the boy is weak and will inherit, and because your grandmother has never in her life made a match that did not improve the family's position by at least one step." Cassia's mouth twisted. "Aulus Proculus is not a bad man. He is a boy who has been told all his life that he is promising, and he believes it, and he will spend his inheritance proving it. If you marry him, you will run his house and his money and his career, and he will be grateful for it until the day he decides he would like to be the one in charge, and then there will be a scene, and then he will have an affair, and you will have a son, and the whole thing will settle into a shape that is comfortable for everyone except you."

"You are very cheerful about it."

"I am honest about it. There is a difference." Cassia reached across the table and touched the girl's wrist. "If you must marry him, then marry him, and make yourself the one who holds the purse and the keys and the clients' names. Do not spend your life waiting for him to be worthy of you. He will not be. Spend it making sure you are never in a position where his worth matters."

"I would rather know," the girl said.

"Everyone says that at fifteen."

"I am not everyone."

Cassia looked at her. Then she smiled, and it was a real smile, and it made her look, for a moment, like the girl she had been in a garden at Tibur with a book of poems in her lap.

"No," she said. "You are not."

She walked the girl back to the edge of the crowd and watched her find her slave, a thin woman with a basket who fell on her with a cry, and then she stood for a while in the street with the festival going on around her. A group of young men went past with goatskin thongs, laughing, and one of them struck a woman on the shoulder and she shrieked and laughed and hit him back. A child was selling sparrows in a cage. A man was asleep in a doorway with a wreath on his head.

Then the shouting changed.

It happened quickly, the way it always did. A knot of men in the middle of the street, a scuffle, a scream that was not laughter, and then the crowd surging backward, and a woman shrieking, and the bright sudden smell of blood in the cold air. Cassia was pushed against the wall of the wine shop. Bathyllus was there, somehow, his arm across her, his staff raised. The crowd opened and closed like water, and in the middle of it a young man was lying on the stones with his hands around his belly and his mouth open, and another young man was standing over him with a knife, and then the knife was gone and the young man was running and the crowd was closing over the body.

"Keep moving," Bathyllus said. "Domina. Keep moving."

They got her back to the house. She stood in the atrium with her hands shaking and her veil torn, and Thais brought her a cup of wine, and she drank it and sat down on the edge of the impluvium and looked at the water.

"There was a boy," she said. "In the street. He was stabbed."

"I heard," Thais said. "They have taken him to the temple of Aesculapius. He will probably live. They usually live. It is the ones who are stabbed in the throat who do not."

"He was someone's son."

"Yes," Thais said. "He was."

Cassia put her face in her hands. She did not cry. She had not cried in twenty-four years, not properly, not since a cart on the road to Puteoli, and she was not going to start now, in the atrium, in front of the household, with the festival going on outside.

"Send word to Tibur," she said. "Today. Not tomorrow. Today. Tell the granddaughter that I am coming within the month, and that she is to keep the old woman alive, and that if she sends me word that the old woman is dead before I get there, I will know that she was paid to let it happen, and I will come anyway, and I will be less polite."

Thais went. Cassia sat by the water and watched the light move on it, and outside the festival went on, and the city ate and drank and laughed and killed itself in small ways, and the sun moved across the roof of the House of the Graces and began, slowly, to go down.

***

He came after dark, in a mask.

She was in the garden room with the lamps lit and the shutters closed, and Bathyllus brought him in without a word and shut the door behind him. He was wearing a dark cloak and a cheap clay mask of a comic actor, and he took it off and stood there with his hair flattened and his face tired, and Cassia felt her heart turn over in her chest like a fish in a net.

"You are mad," she said.

"I know."

"Half the city saw you at the Lupercal. If anyone recognized you—"

"Nobody recognizes a man in a mask at a festival. That is the point of the mask." He put it down on the table. "And I could not wait. I have been to Tibur."

She went very still.

"I did not go myself. I sent a man — a freedman of mine, a Greek, discreet, who used to be a courier for the governor of Syria. He went up the day after the Ides and he came back this morning." Corvinus sat down. "The midwife is alive. She is very old and she is blind in one eye and she lives with her granddaughter's family in a house with a fig tree in the yard. There is a box under her bed. She will not let anyone touch it. She says she is waiting for a woman from Rome."

"Did he see her?"

"He saw her. He told her he came from you. She wept. She said —" He stopped. "She said, 'Tell her I did what I could, and I have kept it all these years, and I am not afraid of her.'"

Cassia sat down slowly on the couch opposite him. The lamp between them was steady. She could hear her own breathing.

"And Felix himself?" she said. "What has he done since the Saturnalia?"

"He has been to the consul's clerk twice. He has been to the prefect of the grain supply. He has been seen at the house of Pactus, and at the house of a man named Scaurus in the Subura, who keeps a house of women and lends money." Corvinus's mouth tightened. "And he has been to my son. Publius came to me last week and told me that a man had approached him in the forum and asked him questions about his grandfather, and offered him money for letters."

"Did he take it?"

"No. He came to me. He was frightened, and he came to me, and I —" Corvinus stopped. "I was proud of him. It is the first time in his life he has done something I could be proud of, and it took a delator to bring it out of him."

"Bring him to the house."

"What?"

"Not to mine. To Tertulla's. He is twenty-one and he is frightened and he has begun to understand that his family is not what he thought it was. If we are to fight this, we need him on our side, and he will not be on our side if he finds out the truth from a stranger." Cassia looked at him. "Tell him something. Not everything. Enough that he knows he is trusted."

"He will hate me."

"He will hate you less than he will hate being lied to." She paused. "And Lucius. Your daughter came to see me today."

He went white. "She what?"

"She came to the festival with a slave and slipped away and stood in the street outside my house for a quarter of an hour, and I brought her into a wine shop and gave her hot wine and told her nothing." Cassia held his eyes. "She is fifteen years old and she is cleverer than her brother and she is frightened of nothing, and she is going to find out the truth whether we tell her or not. If you want to control how she finds it, you will tell her yourself, before someone else does."

"And Felix's men?"

"They have been there. Two of them, three days before my man, asking questions at the inn and the bathhouse and the shrine of Hercules. They did not get to the old woman. The granddaughter turned them away — she is frightened of them, but she is more frightened of the box." He looked at her. "They will go back. If they go back with money, or with a warrant, they will get in. We do not have much time."

"Then I will go tomorrow."

"Cassia—"

"Tomorrow. Or the day after. I will not wait for the spring." She looked up. "And you will not come with me."

"I said I would."

"And I told you what would happen if you did. The whole city is watching your house. If you leave Rome for two days in the middle of an accusation, Pactus will know by the evening of the first day, and he will send a man after you, and then it will not matter what the midwife says because the story will be that the senator fled to Tibur to silence a witness." She stood. "You will stay. You will be seen in the forum every day. You will go to the Senate and make a speech about the grain supply and be photographed in the act of being an ordinary, innocent, boring man. And I will go to Tibur with my own people, and I will come back with the truth, whatever it is."

He looked at her. In the lamplight, with the mask on the table between them and the shutters closed and the festival dying down outside, he looked like a man who had been given a sentence and was trying to decide whether to appeal.

"I hate this," he said.

"I know."

"I have spent twenty-four years letting other people decide what happened to you, and I am doing it again."

"No," Cassia said. "You are doing what I tell you to do. That is not the same thing." She came around the table and stood in front of him. "Lucius. Look at me. I am not the girl in the vineyard. I am a woman with a house and a fortune and twenty-three people who depend on me, and I have been making my own decisions since I was twenty-two years old. I do not need you to protect me. I need you to do what I say, and to be in the forum tomorrow, and to be dull. Can you be dull?"

"I have been dull for twenty-four years."

"Then it should come naturally."

He laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him — and reached up and took her hand and pressed it to his mouth. Then he pulled her down and kissed her, and the mask fell off the table and broke on the floor, and neither of them cared.

Later, in the dark, with the festival finally over and the city gone quiet, she lay with her head on his chest and listened to the house settling around them. Somewhere a door closed. A lamp guttered and went out. The wind came off the hills and rattled the shutters and smelled of snow.

"Lucius," she said.

"Yes."

"If the boy is alive. If he is alive and we find him." She stopped.

"Yes?"

"What will you do?"

He was silent for a long time. She felt his chest rise and fall, and his hand move in her hair, and she thought he was not going to answer.

"I do not know," he said at last. "I have a son. I have a daughter. I have a wife who has done nothing wrong. I have a name that is four hundred years old and a house that is falling down and a mother who is dying and who has done something that I cannot yet think about without wanting to put my fist through a wall." His voice was low. "And I have a woman I have not been able to forget for twenty-four years, and a child I did not know I had, and no idea in the world what I am supposed to do with any of it."

"You will have to choose."

"I know."

"And you will not choose well. No one ever does." She turned her face into his shoulder. "But you will choose, and you will have to live with it, and so will I. That is what people do. They choose, and they live with it, and they die, and the house goes on."

"The house goes on," he said, and there was something in his voice that she could not read — grief, or bitterness, or the beginning of a decision.

She did not ask which.

In the morning he was gone before the light, and the mask was in pieces on the floor, and Bathyllus had swept up the worst of it and put the pieces in the hearth. Cassia stood in the garden room with a cup of hot water and looked at the broken clay in the ashes, a comic actor's grin in two halves, and thought about the fact that every mask in Rome was made of the same cheap stuff, and that underneath them all, the faces were all the same.
