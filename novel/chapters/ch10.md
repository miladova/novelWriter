The Senate met in the Curia Julia on a morning in March when the wind came up the Forum with dust in it and the light through the high windows was the colour of old honey. The house was full. It was fuller than it had been for a month, because the lot for the Asian command was to be drawn that day, and because the emperor's letter was to be read, and because every man in the chamber had heard, in one form or another, that there was going to be trouble.

Corvinus came in early and took his place on the benches with the other consulars. He had been in the Senate for twenty years and he knew its smells and its sounds the way a man knows his own house: the scrape of shoes on stone, the coughs, the murmur of the clerks, the way the noise dropped when the consul rose. He had spoken in this chamber a hundred times. He had never once come into it afraid.

He was afraid now. He could feel it in his hands. He put them in the folds of his toga and looked at the opposite benches and made himself breathe slowly, the way the physicians had taught his mother.

Pactus was already there, at the front of the benches, talking to a group of men with the easy confidence of a man who has arranged everything. He was dressed in a white toga of excellent wool, and his hair was combed forward, and he looked like a man who had never had a doubt in his life. He looked up as Corvinus came in, and smiled, and inclined his head, and turned back to his friends.

The consul called the house to order. The clerk read the prayer. The sacrifice had been made outside, and the augur had pronounced the omens favourable, and Corvinus, who was himself an augur, and who had spent his life reading the flight of birds and the appetite of chickens with a straight face, thought about how easy it was to make the gods say what you wanted them to say.

Then the consul read the emperor's letter.

It was a short letter. It praised the Senate, and the consuls, and the army, and the prosperity of the age. It spoke of the burden of empire and the need for men of experience in the provinces. And it recommended, for the proconsulship of Asia, the name of Lucius Sulpicius Corvinus, consular, augur, legate of the Second Dacian War.

There was a murmur of approval. Several men turned to look at him. Corvinus inclined his head. He did not allow himself to smile.

The consul folded the letter. "The house will proceed to the lot," he said.

Then Pactus rose.

He rose slowly, the way a man rises when he wants everyone to watch him do it, and he stood in the middle of the floor with his hands clasped behind his back and his chin lifted, and he said, "Conscript fathers. Before the lot is drawn, there is a matter that the house must hear."

The chamber went quiet. The consul looked at Pactus, and then at Corvinus, and then at the clerk.

"Speak," he said.

"I have this morning been informed," Pactus said, "that a formal accusation is to be laid against the man whose name the emperor has just recommended to this house. The accusation is one of parricide. It concerns the death of Marcus Sulpicius Corvinus, consul, censor, in his villa at Tibur, in the year of the consulship of Domitian and Silanus, twenty-four years ago."

The silence in the chamber was total. Corvinus sat very still. He had known it was coming and he had not known how it would feel, and it felt like a hand closing on his chest.

"The accuser," Pactus said, "is a Roman knight named Marcus Aquilius Felix, who has laid his information before the consul in the proper form. The witnesses are three: a physician's assistant, a steward, and a midwife. I do not know whether the accusation is true. It is not for me to know. But I know that this house cannot draw the lot for the richest province in Asia while one of the candidates stands accused of the murder of his father." He turned and looked at Corvinus. "I say this with regret. I have known Lucius Sulpicius all my life. But the dignity of this house is worth more than any man's career, including my own."

Old Servilius Paetus rose first, a consular of seventy who had been in the chamber since the year of the fire and who was known for two things: an inability to be brief and an absolute contempt for new men.

"Conscript fathers," he said, "I have known this house for fifty years, and I have never heard a man's name read out by the emperor and a charge of parricide read out in the same hour. It is indecent. It is worse than indecent; it is convenient. I do not know the knight Felix. I do not wish to. I know that a delator is a man who sells other men's misfortunes, and I know that in my father's time such men were whipped out of the Forum, and I know that the empire was no worse for it." He turned and looked at Pactus with the slow, deliberate contempt of a man who has nothing left to lose. "If the emperor has recommended Lucius Sulpicius for Asia, then the emperor has his reasons, and this house would do well to remember who recommended whom."

There was a rumble of approval from the old benches. Then a young man rose on the other side — a tribune, thirty, with the sort of voice that had been trained by a rhetor and the sort of confidence that had been trained by nothing at all — and said that the dignity of the house required that no man, however distinguished, should be permitted to stand for a province while under accusation; that the law was the same for the consular and the cobbler; and that he was sure the senator would welcome the opportunity to clear his name.

"He is sure," Corvinus murmured, to the man beside him. "He has been sure since he was born."

The man beside him, a fat consular named Rufus who had been his father's friend, did not laugh. "They will have you," he said, quietly. "Not today. Not this year, perhaps. But they will have you, and they will do it in the proper form, and the proper form is slower and crueller than a knife."

There was uproar. The consul called for order twice. Corvinus sat on his bench and listened to the noise, and it was like listening to the sea from a cliff: enormous, indifferent, and a long way down.

He rose.

The chamber went quiet again, in the way that chambers do when a man who has been accused stands up.

"Conscript fathers," he said. His voice was steady. He was surprised by how steady it was. "I will not waste the time of this house on a defence of my father's death. I will say only that I loved my father, that I was in Syria when he died, and that I have never in my life done anything to harm him. I will say further that the accusation comes at a convenient moment, from a source that has every reason to wish me harm, and that I do not believe this house will allow a man's career to be destroyed by a rumour and a delator before a single witness has been heard."

He looked at Pactus.

"But I will not ask the house to draw the lot while the accusation stands. I will ask instead that this matter be decided quickly, in public, before this house, in the proper form. I have nothing to hide. Let the accuser bring his witnesses. Let them be examined. Let the truth be known." He paused. "And let the house remember, when it is over, who brought this accusation and why."

He sat down. There was a moment of silence, and then the benches erupted, and it was not all hostile. Several men came to him and shook his hand; others looked away. The consul struck the floor with his staff and called for order, and the clerk read out the procedure: the accusation would be received, a day would be set for the preliminary hearing, the witnesses would be summoned, and the lot for Asia would be deferred until the matter was resolved.

The lot was not drawn. The house rose. Corvinus went out into the wind and the dust of the Forum with his friends around him and a smile fixed on his face, and he did not allow himself to look up at the Palatine, where his mother was dying in a cold room, and he did not allow himself to think about a woman in a house on the Esquiline who was, at that moment, probably already being told what had happened by one of her dozen informants.

He walked as far as the steps of the basilica and then stopped, because his legs would not carry him any further, and stood with his hand against a column while the city went past him.

He had been sixteen when his father had taken him into the atrium and made him recite the offices of the masks. He had been twenty when his father had told him that the girl would be sold and the child disposed of, and he had believed, in that moment, that he had reached the bottom of the man. He had been wrong. The bottom was deeper, and it had taken him twenty-four years and a dying woman's confession to find it.

*I put something in his wine at dinner.*

His mother had said it in the same voice she used to order the linen. She had said it as though she were describing a household expense. And the terrible thing — the thing he could not look at and could not look away from — was that he understood it. He had lived in that house. He had seen what his father did to the people in it, to his wife, to his son, to the girl in the kitchen. He had been too young and too frightened to stop it, and his mother had been neither, and she had done what she had done, and she had done it in the dark, and she had carried it for twenty-four years.

He did not know whether that made her a murderer or a survivor. He suspected it made her both.

He was right. She was.

***

Cassia heard the news from three people before noon: from Hermeros, who had a cousin who was a clerk in the curia; from Comptus, who came himself, white-faced, in a litter, and stood in the atrium with his hands shaking; and from the flower seller in the Subura, who sent a boy with a message that said only, *It is done. P.*

She sent Comptus home with a cup of wine and a promise, and then she sat in the tablinum and did the arithmetic. The preliminary hearing would be in a month, perhaps less. The witnesses would be summoned. Felix had the midwife's name and the physician's assistant and the old steward; he had the story, and now he had a forum. The only thing he did not have was the box.

She called the household into the atrium and told them the truth, or as much of it as they needed.

"There is an accusation against the senator Corvinus," she said. "It will come to a hearing. Before it does, there will be men in this city asking questions about this house, and about me, and about all of you. Some of them will be magistrates. Some of them will be men who are paid to ask. Some of them will be your friends."

The household stood in a half-circle and looked at her. Twenty-three people, from Thais to the boy who fed the pigeons.

"You will tell them the truth," Cassia said. "The truth is not dangerous. What is dangerous is a lie that contradicts another lie. If a man asks you whether I received the senator Corvinus in this house, you will say yes, because I did, and because it was a public reception and half the city knew of it. If a man asks you whether I went to Tibur, you will say that I have business at Tibur. If a man asks you about the past, you will say that you were not here, or that you do not know, and you will say it the same way every time."

She looked at them.

"And if a man threatens you, you will remember that a threat is not a beating, and that a beating is not a death, and that whatever happens to you, this house will not abandon you or your families. I have written it down. Hermeros has it. If I am taken, the paper goes to the lady Tertulla, and she will see that every one of you is freed and given money and a place to go." She paused. "That is not a kindness. It is a contract. You are not being asked to be brave for nothing."

There was a long silence. Then Celer, the cook, said, "Domina, we are not going to be brave. We are going to be sensible. There is a difference." And the household laughed, and Cassia laughed with them, and then she went into the tablinum and sat down and put her face in her hands for exactly as long as it took to count to ten.

She sent for Tertulla, and for Hermeros, and for Thais. She sent a boy to the Palatine with a note that said, *I know. Do not do anything. I will come to you tonight.* She sent another to the house of Aemilia Celsa, which was the same house, and which she had never in her life written to before, and which she wrote to now in the same terms, because she had learned long ago that a message sent to two people is more likely to be delivered than a message sent to one.

Proculus came at the second hour, in a litter, with his son in attendance and a face like a man who has been asked to pay for a funeral.

"I have heard," he said. "The whole city has heard. There is not a wine shop in Rome that is not discussing it." He sat down heavily. "I have come to tell you that I cannot be seen with you for a while. Not because I do not value the friendship of this house. Because a man in my position has to be careful, and because the consul has asked me to speak for the province of Asia in the debate, and because I cannot speak for a province and be seen dining with the woman who is at the centre of a scandal."

"I am not at the centre of anything."

"You are at the centre of everything, and you know it." Proculus wiped his face. "I am sorry. I am a coward. I have always been a coward. But I am a coward with a seat in the Senate, and a seat in the Senate is worth more to you than a brave man with no seat at all." He stood. "When this is over — if you are still standing — send for me, and I will come, and I will bring the best wine I have, and we will pretend that this conversation never happened."

He went out. His son, Aulus, lingered in the doorway.

"Domina," he said. "My father is a coward. I am not."

Cassia looked at him. The boy who had put his hand on Chrysis's shoulder at a dinner in December, and who had been sent back to his seat like a child, was standing in her tablinum with his jaw set and his fists clenched, and she thought that he would either become a decent man or a dead one, and that there was no way of knowing which.

"Then do something brave," she said, "and do it quietly, and do not tell anyone you have done it. That is what courage is. It is not a speech in the Senate. It is a letter written at midnight to a man who can help, and a promise kept when it would be easier to break it." She turned away. "Go home, Aulus. Look after your father. He is more frightened than he knows."

Then she went to the strongroom and took out the box and set it on the desk and looked at it for a long time.

"Domina," Thais said, from the doorway. "The lady Aemilia is here."

Cassia turned. "Now?"

"Now. She came in a plain litter with one slave, by the garden door, and she is in the small dining room, and she has been crying."

Cassia put the box back in the strongroom and locked it.

***

Aemilia Celsa was sitting on the couch in the small dining room with her hands in her lap. She had been crying, and she had stopped, and she had wiped her face and put her hair right, and she looked, in the grey light from the garden, like a woman who had come to the end of something and had decided to keep walking.

"They are saying he killed his father," she said.

"They are saying he is accused of it. It is not the same."

"It is the same in this city." Aemilia looked up. "I have been in that house for nineteen years. I have sat at that table and listened to that woman talk about honour and the family and the masks on the wall. And this morning my son came to me and told me that there is a woman in Rome who knew my husband before I did, and that there is a child somewhere, and that my mother-in-law has been lying to all of us for twenty-four years." Her voice was steady. "I am not here to shout at you. I have done my shouting, in the litter, on the way here, at my own slave, who did not deserve it. I am here to ask you what I should do."

Cassia sat down opposite her. "You should decide what you want."

"I want my family to survive."

"That is not the same as wanting the truth."

"I know." Aemilia's hands tightened. "I have spent nineteen years wanting the truth and being told that it was not my business. Now it is my business, and I find that I do not want it. I want the house, and my children, and my husband, and the life I have made, and I do not want to know what it was built on." She looked at Cassia. "Does that make me a coward?"

"No," Cassia said. "It makes you a person who has something to lose."

"And you? What do you want?"

Cassia looked at her. The garden was grey beyond the window, and the first rain of March was beginning to fall, and somewhere in the house a door closed.

"I want the boy," she said. "I want to know whether he is alive. I want to know what he has been told and who has raised him and whether he is happy. And then I want to make sure that no one in this city can ever take him from me again." She paused. "And I want the man who paid Felix to be destroyed. Not killed. Destroyed. I want him to lose his money and his name and his friends and his seat in the Senate, and I want him to live a long time afterward, in a small house, with no one to talk to."

Aemilia stared at her. "You are not a good woman."

"No."

"You are not a bad one, either."

"I am a woman who has been pushed," Cassia said, "and who has decided to push back. Whether that is good or bad depends on who is writing the history." She leaned forward. "I will make you a bargain, Aemilia. I will do everything in my power to save your husband from this accusation, not because I love him, but because the accusation is a lie built out of a truth, and I will not let a lie win. And in return, you will do three things."

"Name them."

"You will not tell your mother-in-law anything I tell you. You will not prevent me from seeing your husband when I need to. And when this is over, you will let me know the boy — if he is alive — in whatever way he wants to be known. Not as a scandal. Not as a secret. As a person."

Aemilia was silent for a long moment. The rain came down harder, and the gutters of the house began to run, and the garden smelled of wet stone and rosemary.

"I have one condition," she said.

"Name it."

"When this is over, you will not come to my house. You will not dine at my table. You will not be seen with my husband. Whatever there is between you, it ends when this is ended." She looked at Cassia steadily. "I am not a fool. I know what he is to you and what you are to him. I have known since the portico of the library. But I will not be a wife in a house where the other woman comes to dinner. I have my dignity, and it is not much, and it is all I have."

Cassia looked at her. In the grey light, with her hair coming down and her hands clasped in her lap, Aemilia Celsa was not beautiful and not clever and not kind, and she was, in that moment, the most formidable person in the room.

"Agreed," Cassia said.

Aemilia nodded. Then she said, "What do we do?"

"We make him tell the truth," Cassia said. "Not the whole truth. The part that can be proved. The delation says your husband killed his father. We will prove that he did not, because he did not, and because the person who did is dying in a room upstairs and has been waiting twenty-four years to say it."

Aemilia's face went white. "His mother."

"Yes."

"You are sure?"

"I am sure of the cup," Cassia said. "I am sure she gave it to me. I am sure she took my son out of the room. I am not sure what she put in her husband's wine. But I am going to find out." She stood. "Go home. Tell your husband I will come tonight. Tell him to say nothing to his mother until I am there. And tell your daughter—" She paused. "Tell your daughter that I am sorry she had to find out this way, and that she was right to come to me at the festival, and that when this is over I will tell her the rest."

Aemilia rose. At the door she said, "My daughter has been asking questions about you."

"She is a clever girl."

"She is a difficult girl." Aemilia's mouth moved. "I was difficult. It did not save me, but it kept me alive." She went out, her slave behind her, and Cassia stood in the small dining room and listened to the rain.

***

Felix came at dusk, alone, with his hood up and no men behind him, and Bathyllus brought him to the tablinum and did not leave the room.

"I have thought about it," Felix said.

"And?"

"I will give you Pactus." He sat down. "Not for the money. The money is useful but I can get money. I will give you Pactus because I have been a delator for six years and no one has ever offered me a chair, and because I am tired, and because I have a boy of nine and a girl of six and I would like them to grow up in a house where their father is not spat at in the street." He looked up. "But I want something else. Something you will not want to give."

"Name it."

"I want a written agreement. Under seal. That I am under the protection of your house. That if I am ever accused, you will speak for me. That my children will never want." He watched her. "I know what your word is worth. I have spent six years learning what words are worth in this city. I want it in writing, witnessed, in a place where it cannot be burned."

Cassia looked at him. The rain had stopped, and the lamp was lit, and the black book was in the strongroom, and the box was beside it, and outside the streets were full of the noise of the evening.

"You will have it," she said. "Drawn up tonight, witnessed by my steward and by the lady Tertulla, sealed with my ring. And in exchange you will give me the name of the man who paid you, and the petition, and a written statement that you were paid, and the date of the hearing."

Felix nodded slowly. Then he said, "The man who paid me is not Pactus."

"I know."

"You know?"

"I have known since the Saturnalia. Pactus is a front. Pactus wants the Asian command, and he has debts, and he has been borrowing from a banker on the Via Lata who holds mortgages on half the Palatine." Cassia turned her cup. "What I do not know is whether the banker is using Pactus or Pactus is using the banker."

"What do they have? Not the story. The proof."

"A copy of the physician's certificate, signed by a man who is dead. A statement from the steward, taken down by a clerk and signed with his mark. A statement from the physician's assistant, Faustus, who says that he was told by his master that the old man's death was not natural." Felix turned the cup. "And the midwife. She is the one they want most. A midwife who was in the room can say what she saw, and a court will believe her over a senator any day."

"Then we will have the midwife."

"She is old, domina. She is ill. She may die before the hearing, and then they will read her statement, and a statement read by a clerk is worth more than a living witness who contradicts it, because a clerk cannot be cross-examined."

"Then we will have her alive, and we will have her statement, and we will have something better than either." Cassia leaned forward. "The petition. You said it was laid before the consul in the proper form. Who drew it up?"

"A lawyer named Crispus. He works for Sittius."

"And where is the original?"

"With the consul's clerk. There is a copy in Sittius's strongbox and a copy in Pactus's house." Felix's eyes narrowed. "You are thinking of stealing it."

"I am thinking of proving that it was paid for." Cassia stood. "A delator's petition must be sworn before a magistrate. If we can prove that the witnesses were paid, and that the payment came from Sittius, then the petition is not a petition. It is a fraud. And a man who lays a fraudulent accusation before the Senate is not a prosecutor. He is a criminal."

"Both," Felix said. "The banker is a man named Sittius. He holds the mortgage on the Sulpicius house and the vineyard in Etruria and a warehouse on the Tiber. If the senator is ruined, the estate comes to him at a fraction of its value. Pactus gets the province. Sittius gets the house. And I get paid." He looked at her. "That is the whole of it. There is no great conspiracy. There is a banker who wants a house and a senator who wants a province, and they found a man who could put a story together."

Cassia sat very still. She had known it, or something like it, but knowing a thing and hearing it said are different, and hearing it said by a man who had been paid to say it was worse.

"Then we will give them a story," she said. "And it will be true."

She called for Hermeros and the wax and the seal, and they drew up the agreement that night, and Felix signed it and she signed it and Tertulla and Hermeros witnessed it, and when it was done Felix sat looking at the tablet in his hands with an expression that Cassia had never seen on him before.

"What is it?" she said.

"I do not know," he said. "I have never had a thing like this before. I do not know what to do with it."

"Put it somewhere safe," Cassia said, "and go home to your children."
