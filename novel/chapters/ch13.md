The house on the Palatine at night was a different house from the one Cassia had walked through in the daytime. The lamps were few and far between, and the corridors were full of shadows, and the masks in the atrium looked out of their cupboards with their painted eyes and their mouths that were neither smiling nor sad. She came in through the garden door with Corvinus beside her and Aemilia waiting at the end of the corridor with a lamp, and none of them said anything for a moment.

"She is bad tonight," Aemilia said. "She has been asking for you since the afternoon. She will not take the poppy, because she says it makes her stupid, and she will not have the physician, because she says he is a fool." She looked at Cassia. "She has eaten nothing since yesterday. I do not know how she is still alive."

"People like your mother-in-law do not die until they have finished what they are doing," Cassia said.

Aemilia's mouth moved. "That is the most comforting thing anyone has said to me all week."

Young Sulpicia was sitting on the stairs.

She was in her nightgown with a mantle over it and her hair loose, and she had a lamp on the step beside her and a book in her lap that she was not reading. She stood up when she saw them, and her face in the lamplight was her grandmother's face and her father's face and, in some way that Cassia could not have explained, her own.

"I heard you come in," she said.

"You should be in bed," Corvinus said.

"I am not a child."

"You are fifteen."

"I am fifteen and I am to be married in the summer, and I am the only person in this house who is not allowed to know what is happening in it." She looked at her father. "Grandmother has been asking for the lady all evening. Mother has been crying in the garden. There is a notary in the tablinum, and the slaves have been told to stay out of the west wing, and there is a woman in a burnt dress standing in our atrium." She turned to Cassia. "You are the woman."

"I am the woman," Cassia said.

"You are also the woman from the wine shop."

"Yes."

Her father looked from one to the other. "You have met?"

"At the Lupercalia," Sulpicia said. "I went to see her house and she caught me in the street and gave me hot wine and told me I was too young to know the truth." She lifted her chin. "I am not too young now."

Cassia looked at her. The girl was frightened; she could see it in the way she held the lamp, in the way her free hand was clenched at her side. But she was standing in the corridor of a house full of secrets and asking to be let in, and Cassia had done the same thing, once, at eighteen, in a garden, with a boy who came to the kitchen for bread.

"Your grandmother is dying," Cassia said. "She is going to tell us some things tonight that will be difficult to hear, and some of them are about your father, and some of them are about me, and some of them are about a boy who is twenty-four years old and who has nothing to do with any of this. You may come in, if your father permits it. But you will not speak until you are spoken to, and you will not repeat a word of what you hear to anyone, and when it is over you will go to bed and you will not ask me any questions until the morning."

Sulpicia looked at her father. Corvinus looked at Cassia, and then at his daughter, and his face did something complicated.

"Come," he said.

They went up the stairs together, and the room at the end of the corridor was lit with three lamps and a brazier, and the heat of it came out into the passage like a hand. Sulpicia Varia was propped up on her pillows with her hair in two thin braids and her hands on the coverlet, and the flesh had gone from her face so completely that she looked like a carving of herself, all bone and intention.

"Sit down," she said. "All of you. You look like a delegation."

Corvinus sat on the stool by the bed. Aemilia stood by the door with her arms folded. Cassia sat in the chair at the foot of the bed, where she could see the old woman's face and the old woman could see hers.

"You have brought the paper," Sulpicia said.

"I have brought the paper."

"Let me see it."

Cassia took the receipt from her sleeve and unfolded it and held it up. The old woman looked at it for a long time. Then she closed her eyes.

"Four hundred sesterces," she said. "I remember. I thought it was cheap."

The room was very quiet. The brazier ticked. Somewhere in the house a door closed, and a slave went past in the corridor with soft feet.

"I have been trying to decide," Sulpicia Varia said, "whether I am sorry." She opened her eyes. "I have had twenty-four years to decide, and I find that I am not. I am not sorry that I did it. I am sorry that it was necessary. There is a difference, and I would like you to understand it, because I am going to die in this room and I would like at least one person in it to understand me."

"Explain it," Cassia said.

"Your son was a scandal. A bastard child of a slave girl, born in a villa, with a father who was twenty years old and a fool. If the child had lived, my husband would have sold him or exposed him, and he would have died in a ditch, or he would have been raised a slave and sold south, and you would have been sold with him. I did what I did to save the family, and I did it to save the child, and I did it to save you, though you will not believe that." She looked at Cassia. "The man I gave him to was a potter with no children and a good trade. I had him investigated. I knew his name and his wife's name and the name of the street he lived in. I chose him because he was kind. I chose him because he would raise the boy as his own and the boy would grow up with a roof and a trade and a father, instead of a brothel and a grave."

"You sold a child," Cassia said.

"I saved a child."

"You sold my son for four hundred sesterces and you told me he was dead."

"Would you have preferred the truth? Would you have preferred to be sold to Puteoli knowing that your son was alive in Rome and that you would never see him?" The old woman's voice was thin and fierce. "I gave you a mercy. I gave you a clean grief. I gave you the only thing a woman in your position could have been given, which was the ability to believe that the child was out of pain. And you have spent twenty-four years hating me for it, and I have spent twenty-four years knowing that you would hate me, and I did it anyway."

"I would have come for him," Cassia said. "I would have crawled out of Puteoli on my hands and knees. I would have found him and taken him and raised him in a room above a bakery and I would have been poor and I would have been nothing and he would have been mine."

"And he would have been a freedwoman's bastard in the Subura, with no name and no trade and no future, and every man who came to your door would have known what his mother was." Sulpicia Varia's eyes were wet. "You are a rich woman now, domina. You have a house and a fortune and the ear of half the Senate. But you were not rich then. You had nothing. You had a body and a debt. And I took the child out of that, and I gave him to a man who could raise him, and I have paid for it every day for twenty-four years, and I would do it again."

Cassia stood up. She walked to the window and stood with her back to the room and looked out at the dark garden and the lights of the city beyond it.

"His name is Gaius Vibidius Nestor," she said. "He works with his father. He has clay on his arms and a laugh that carries into the street. He is twenty-four years old and he does not know that any of us exist." She turned. "You took that from me. Not the poverty. Not the shame. The years. You took twenty-four years of him, and you cannot give them back, and no amount of explaining will make me forgive you."

"I do not want your forgiveness."

"Then what do you want?"

Sulpicia Varia looked at her for a long moment. The brazier ticked. The lamps burned.

"I have spent my life in this house," Sulpicia Varia said, "and I have watched it eat people. It ate my husband, and it ate my son's youth, and it ate my daughter-in-law's happiness, and it would have eaten you if I had let it." She looked at Cassia. "You think I am a monster. I have thought so myself, on some nights. But I am a woman who was given a house and a name and a husband, and I was told that my duty was to keep them, and I did what I had to do to keep them, and the world did not offer me any other way to be a good woman than that."

"There was another way," Cassia said. "You could have let me go. You could have given me money and a passage to somewhere and let me take the child."

"And my husband would have found you. He had men. He had money. He would have found you within the month, and he would have taken the child and had you sold, and he would have done it in a way that would have made him feel like a just man." The old woman's voice was thin. "I knew him, domina. I knew him better than anyone alive. He was a man who could not bear to be disobeyed, and he would have hunted you to the ends of the earth, and when he found you he would have made an example of you. I did not save you because I could not save you. I saved the child because I could."

"You did not save him. You sold him."

"I sold him to a potter for four hundred sesterces, and I paid the dealer another two hundred to forget the address, and I have spent twenty-four years paying that man every year to keep his mouth shut." Sulpicia Varia's hands were shaking on the coverlet. "Do you think I do not know what it sounds like? Do you think I have not lain in this bed on a hundred nights and heard it in my own voice? I sold a child. I sold your child. I did it because the alternative was worse, and I have never been able to explain to anyone why the alternative was worse, because the only person who would have understood it is dead."

Corvinus stood up. "Mother—"

"Sit down." Her eyes did not leave Cassia's. "I am not asking for your absolution. I am telling you that I know what I did, and that I would do it again, and that I am sorry for the girl in the room and not sorry for the woman in this bed. You may make of that what you like."

"I want you to save my son," she said.

***

The statement took an hour. Cassia had brought the notary from Tibur with her — the same nervous man, who had been paid enough to be brave — and he sat at the table by the window with his tablets and his seal and wrote down every word, and the old woman gave it all: the cup, the night, the dealer, the receipt, the payment, the threats, the twenty-four years of silence.

She did not confess to killing her husband. When the notary asked, she said, "I have nothing to say about that," and looked at Cassia with her cold pale eyes and said, "That is a different matter, and it belongs to a different paper, and I will give it when I am ready."

"You may not have time," Cassia said.

"Then you had better pray that I do."

The notary sealed the statement and gave it to Cassia, and Cassia put it in her sleeve with the receipt, and Sulpicia Varia lay back on her pillows and closed her eyes, and for a moment she looked like any old woman, tired and small and finished.

"Corvinus," she said, without opening her eyes.

"Yes, mother."

"Come here."

He went and knelt by the bed, and she put her hand on his head, the way a woman blesses a child, and her fingers moved once through his hair.

"You were a good boy," she said. "You were a better boy than your father deserved and a better man than I made you. Whatever happens, remember that I did what I did because I loved this house, and because I loved you, and because I did not know how to love anything else." Her hand fell back to the coverlet. "Now go away, all of you. I want to sleep."

They went out. In the corridor Aemilia said, "I will stay with her tonight," and went back in with her lamp, and Corvinus stood in the passage with his hand against the wall and his head bowed.

Cassia stood beside him and did not touch him.

"Come," she said. "I have somewhere to go."

***

The Temple of Juno Lucina stood on the Cispian Hill, in a grove of old trees, and it was one of the oldest temples in Rome. Women went there to ask for children, and to give thanks for children, and to mourn children, and the temple was full of the small terracotta figures that mothers left: babies in swaddling clothes, hands, feet, eyes, the parts of the body that had been healed. There was a stone bench outside the door and a fountain, and the priestess was a freedwoman of sixty named Calpurnia, who had been in the temple since she was fourteen and who knew more about the women of Rome than any magistrate in the city.

They came at the ninth hour, when the temple was empty and the lamps were being lit. Cassia had brought the lock of hair and a small cake and a jar of milk, because she had asked Thais what one brought to Juno Lucina and Thais had told her, and she stood at the altar with the offerings in her hands and found that she did not know what to say.

The priestess came and stood beside her.

"You have never been here before," she said.

"No."

"I would remember. I remember all of them." Calpurnia looked at the lock of hair in Cassia's palm. "How old?"

"Twenty-four years."

The priestess did not move. "And you are only now bringing him?"

"I only now know that he lived."

Calpurnia was silent for a moment. Then she took Cassia's hand and turned it over and looked at the lock of hair in it, and her face was not kind and not unkind; it was the face of a woman who has heard everything and has decided to keep listening.

"Put it on the altar," she said. "Tell the goddess what you have come to tell her. She has heard worse."

Cassia put the lock of hair on the altar with the cake and the milk. Then she stood there with her hands at her sides and said, in a voice that did not sound like her own, "I have a son. His name is Gaius Vibidius Nestor. He is a potter on the Via Ostiensis, and he is alive, and I have not seen him since the night he was born. I am told that he is well. I am told that he is happy. I do not know if that is true, because I have only seen him from the street." She stopped. The lamp on the altar moved in a draught. "I do not know what I am asking for. I do not think I am asking for anything. I think I am telling you because there is no one else I can tell."

The priestess said, "That is what the goddess is for."

"I thought the goddess was for children."

"The goddess is for women. Children are only one of the things that happen to them." Calpurnia picked up the cake and put it in the fire, and the smell of it went up with the smoke. "I had three children. Two died in the first year and one lived to be a soldier and died in Dacia. I have been a priestess for forty-six years and I have listened to women tell me things they could not tell their husbands or their mothers or their gods. Do you know what they all say, in the end?"

"No."

"They say, 'I did what I could.'" Calpurnia turned. "And they are usually right. Not always. Some of them did what they wanted and called it what they could. But most of them, most of the time, did what they could with what they had, and the world took the rest." She looked at Cassia. "You did what you could. You were eighteen years old and you were a slave and you were in a room with a bolted door. There was nothing you could have done. And now you are a rich woman with a house and a name and money, and there is a great deal you can do. That is the difference between then and now. Not the guilt. The power."

Cassia looked at the altar. The lock of hair was beginning to singe at the edges, and the smell of burning hair was in the air with the smell of the cake.

"I do not feel powerful," she said.

"No," the priestess said. "No one ever does. That is how you know you are using it well."

***

They went back to the house on the Esquiline, what was left of it, and Cassia had the strongroom opened and the black book brought out and set on the table in the small dining room with the three Graces looking down from the alcove. The burnt smell was still in the walls, and the roof over the kitchen wing was a patch of new tiles, and the storeroom was a black hole with a plank across it.

Corvinus stood in the doorway and looked at the room.

"It is a good house," he said.

"It was a good house. Now it is a good house with a hole in it." Cassia sat down and opened the black book. "Sit. We have work to do."

He sat. She turned the pages.

"Here," she said. "Felix. He has given me a statement, and the statement names Pactus and Sittius, and the payment, and the petition. Here. Psyche. She has given me a statement, and it names your mother and the dealer and the receipt, and it establishes that the child lived. Here. The receipt itself, which names the potter and the child." She looked up. "And here, at the back, everything I know about Sittius, and his brother-in-law, and the annona, and the mortgage on your house. If we put all of it before the consul at the preliminary hearing, we can have the accusation dismissed and the delation turned back on the men who paid for it."

"Can we?"

"Yes. If we do it carefully, and if your mother gives us the other paper, and if nothing else goes wrong." Cassia closed the book. "But there is a problem."

"Only one?"

"Three. The first is Felix. He has given us a statement, but he is a delator, and a delator's word is worth very little in a court, and the moment Pactus offers him more money than I have promised him, he may change his mind. So we need to make it impossible for him to change his mind. We need a second witness who can corroborate the payment, and we need it in writing."

"Sittius's clerk."

"Sittius's clerk." Cassia nodded. "The second problem is your mother. Her statement about the child is good, but it does not clear you of parricide. In fact, it makes it worse, because it establishes that there was a death in that house that night and that she was in the room when it happened. If she does not confess to the poisoning, the prosecution will say that you did it, and that she is covering for you, and there is no paper in the world that will prove otherwise."

Corvinus was silent. Then he said, "She will confess."

"How do you know?"

"Because she is dying, and because she is proud, and because she has spent her whole life arranging things so that she would be remembered well." He looked at the table. "And because I asked her, in the corridor, before we left. I asked her to give the paper. She said she would think about it."

"Then we need the third thing," Cassia said.

"What is the third thing?"

"You." She looked at him. "You have to decide what you are going to say when they ask you about the child. Not about the murder. About the boy. Because the moment the receipt is read in that chamber, every man in the Senate will know that you had a son by a slave and that your mother sold him. They will not care about the law. They will care about the scandal, and they will use it, and it will be in every wine shop in Rome by the evening."

"I know."

"Do you? Because there is a way to do this that keeps the boy out of it. We can put the midwife's statement before the court in a closed session, and the receipt can be sealed, and the court can hear the evidence without the city hearing it. The accusation would be dismissed for lack of proof, and the boy's name would never be spoken in public." She paused. "Or we can do it the other way. We can put the whole thing before the Senate and the people, and let them see what your mother did and what Sittius paid for, and we can destroy Pactus and the banker in one afternoon. But the boy's name will be in every mouth in Rome, and his father's shop will be a place that people come to look at, and he will have to decide, at twenty-four years old, whether he wants to be a potter or a scandal."

Corvinus looked at her for a long time. The lamps burned. The three Graces looked down from the alcove with their painted hands on one another's shoulders.

"What do you want?" he said.

"I want the boy left alone," Cassia said. "I want him to have his life. I have waited twenty-four years to find him and I am not going to hand him to the city to be chewed up in the Forum." She closed the black book. "But I want Pactus destroyed more, and I want the men who burned my house to pay for it, and I want the truth to be told in a place where it cannot be buried again." She looked up. "So I am going to ask you to choose, and I am going to ask you to choose as a father and not as a senator."

Corvinus sat with his hands on the table. He was forty-five years old, and he had spent his whole life in the Senate, and he had never in that life been asked to choose between his family and his name.

"Can we do both?" he said.

"No."

"Then I choose the boy," he said. "The closed session. The sealed receipt. The truth told to the men who need to hear it and to no one else." He looked up. "And afterward, when it is over, I will go to the Via Ostiensis and I will stand in the street and look at him, the way you did, and I will decide then whether I have the right to speak to him."

Cassia looked at him. Then she reached across the table and took his hand.

"All right," she said. "Then that is what we will do."

Later, when the book was closed and the household had gone to bed, he came to her room.

She was sitting on the edge of the bed with her hair down and her hands in her lap, and she did not get up when he came in. He stood in the doorway for a moment and then came and sat beside her, and neither of them said anything for a long time.

"There is a room in this house," she said, "where I have never let anyone sleep. It is the room at the top of the stairs with the window that looks over the garden. I have kept it empty for twenty-four years. I told myself it was for guests. It was not for guests."

"I know," he said.

"I do not know what to do with it now. If he does not want to come. If he does not want to know us. If he is happy in his father's shop and his father's house and the life he has made." She turned to him. "What do I do with the room?"

"You leave it empty," Corvinus said. "And you wait. And if he never comes, you give it to someone else who needs it, and you do not make it a shrine, because a shrine is a thing that eats the people who keep it."

Cassia laughed, and it hurt. "You sound like the priestess."

"I sound like a man who has spent twenty-four years keeping a shrine." He took her hand. "I have a room in my house too. It is not a room. It is a name I do not say, and a year I do not think about, and a woman I do not look at in a crowd. I have carried it so long that I do not know what I would be without it."

"You would be a senator with a wife and two children and a house that is falling down and a mother who murdered your father."

"Yes," he said. "And I would be free."

She kissed him. It was not the desperate kiss of the garden room at Tertulla's house, and it was not the kiss of the vineyard. It was slower, and sadder, and it had in it the knowledge that they were both forty-something and both damaged and both standing in a burnt house with the smell of smoke in the walls.

Afterward she lay in the dark with her head on his shoulder and listened to the house. The window was open and the night air was cold and clean, and somewhere out in the city a dog was barking, and somewhere on the Via Ostiensis a young man was asleep, and she thought about the fact that she had found him, and that she could not have him, and that there was a kind of peace in that, and a kind of grief, and that they were the same thing.

"Lucius," she said.

"Yes."

"When this is over. When the trial is done and the boy has decided what he wants and your mother is dead and the house is sold or saved. What will you do?"

He was silent for a long time. "I do not know. I have never been able to imagine a life after this. I have been living toward this for twenty-four years without knowing it." He turned his head. "What will you do?"

"I will keep the house," Cassia said. "I will keep the house and the people in it and the business and the books. And I will go to the baths on Sundays and I will dine with Tertulla and I will grow old and I will be rich and I will be lonely in the way that rich old women are lonely, and I will be all right."

"That is a very sad answer."

"It is a very honest one." She closed her eyes. "I have been lonely for twenty-four years. It is not the worst thing in the world. The worst thing is being lonely and poor."

They sat there for a long time in the burnt house with the lamps burning and the book open between them, and outside the city went on with its noise, and somewhere on the Via Ostiensis a young man was going to bed in a room over a workshop, and he did not know that two people in Rome had just decided the shape of his life.
