The house woke before its mistress, as houses do. By the fourth hour of the December morning the water carrier had come and gone, leaving the courtyard flags dark with spill; the baker's boy had left his basket at the back door and been paid in copper by the cook; and Bathyllus, who kept the door and had once kept the peace in a gladiator's barracks, had already turned away two beggars, a woman selling Egyptian charms, and a young man who claimed to have an appointment and did not.

Antonia Cassia heard all of it from her room above the garden, the way a captain hears his ship. The creak of the well rope. The shuffle of the kitchen slaves sweeping yesterday's ash. The thin, irritable clatter of the secretary's stylus being sharpened on the stone sill. She lay a while longer under the coverlet, because the morning was cold and because lying still was one of the few luxuries she had never learned to be bored by. Then she rose, and Sabina came in with the basin, and the day began.

Sabina was twelve, small for it, with a slave's careful hands and a free child's habit of asking questions. She held the towel while Cassia washed, and said, "There was a man at the door before light. Bathyllus wouldn't wake you."

"What kind of man?"

"A man with a lantern. He had a horse."

"Then he was somebody's man, not his own." Cassia dried her face. "What did he want?"

"He said his master would send word at the proper hour."

"He said it in Latin?"

"He said it in Greek," Sabina said, "and then he said it again in Latin, louder."

Cassia smiled at that, and the smile made her look younger than forty-two and also more dangerous, because it was the smile of a woman who had learned that most men repeat themselves. She let Sabina dress her in a plain stola the colour of undyed wool and a mantle the colour of wine lees, and she put on no gold, and she did not look at the mirror longer than it took to pin her hair. The mirror was silvered glass from Alexandria, and it had been a gift from a man who had wanted something, and she had forgotten which man.

At the desk in the small room behind the atrium, Hermeros was waiting with the tablets. He was fifty-seven, a freedman of the house, bald as an egg and neat as a receipt, and he had been with her since the year she had bought the house and discovered that a fortune is not a thing a woman can carry alone.

"Show me," she said.

He showed her. The wine from Campania, forty amphorae, of which six had soured on the road and would have to be sold to a tavernkeeper who did not ask questions. The silk merchant from Berytus, who had promised payment on the Ides and would not pay. The knight who owed forty thousand and had sent a gilt cup instead of money, which was an insult with a handle on it. The baker, whose rent was three months late and who had a sick daughter. The sum that would be needed for the Saturnalia, which was less than the sum that would be needed for the Kalends, which was less than the sum that would be needed if certain people in the city decided to remember that the lady of this house had once been something other than a lady.

"Call the knight's debt," Cassia said.

"He is a knight."

"He is a knight who owes me forty thousand and thinks a cup will do. Call it. Politely. In front of his wife's uncle, if you can arrange it."

Hermeros made a small mark. "And the baker?"

"Let him be until the spring. The daughter?"

"Still ill."

"Send Narcissus with the green jar. Not the blue. The green." She turned a tablet over. "The house is full tonight?"

"Senator Proculus, four at his table, and the grain man Comptus with two. Proculus has asked for the garden room."

"Proculus always asks for the garden room. It is February in his soul." She set the tablet down. "Give him the painted room and tell him the garden is being replanted. He will complain, and then he will be pleased to have been lied to. Put Comptus next to him. Comptus talks when he is nervous, and Proculus cannot resist correcting a man who talks."

"And the women?"

"Ione at Proculus's shoulder. Chrysis on the other side, if she will. Dama to sing after the second course, not before. The man has no ear before the second course." Cassia stood. "And tell Celer that if the fish sauce is as thick as it was at the Ides, I will give his kitchen to the boy and let him watch."

Hermeros wrote it down, although he had written it down a hundred times before, because writing it down was his way of loving her.

The house was not the largest on the Esquiline. It was not even the most beautiful, though it had a peristyle garden that had cost more than some senators' estates and a bath suite that had ruined a marble merchant. It was simply the most quietly expensive house in Rome, and the price of entering it was not money. Money was the least of it. Men came to the House of the Graces because they could not say certain things in the Senate, or to their wives, or to their own mirrors; and Cassia had built her fortune on the truth that a man will pay almost anything for a room in which he is not required to be himself.

She had bought the house with money that had been made in a brothel and laundered through a shipping venture and a brickworks on the Tiber, and she had bought it from a man who had lost it at dice and had wept at the sale. She had kept his cook and his doorkeeper and his gardener, and she had changed everything else. The garden she had made larger. The atrium she had left plain, because a plain atrium made the garden look richer. The strongroom she had built herself, behind a false panel in the tablinum, and in it she kept the household's money, the deeds, the wills of three men who trusted her more than their own families, and a cushion that was not a cushion.

Her name had been given to her by a man who had bought her and then, in the way of such men, decided that he preferred to own a partner than a slave. Antonius Felix had been twenty years older and had had no children and had believed, wrongly, that gratitude was a debt that could be collected. He had freed her in his will and left her his fortune and the name. She had buried him in a tomb on the Via Appia and had paid for the inscription herself, because she had discovered that the dead are easier to be generous with than the living.

The city knew all of this, or most of it, and the parts it did not know it invented. The city said she had been a whore, which was true. The city said she had poisoned her patron, which was not; he had died of a dropsy and a bad temper. The city said she kept a book of every secret told in her house, which was true, and which was the reason half the men who came to her door came a little afraid. The city said she had no family, no name, no past. On that point the city was wrong in the way that mattered, but it was a long time since she had corrected anyone.

There were three Graces painted in the dining room, standing in a row with their hands on one another's shoulders. Aglaia, Euphrosyne, Thalia. Splendour, good cheer, abundance. The painter had been paid to make them look like Cassia and two of her women, and he had done it badly enough that it was a joke and well enough that it was a compliment. Guests liked to name which was which. They were always wrong. Cassia had learned long ago that men see what they have come to see, and that the skill of a hostess is not to correct them but to arrange the room so that what they see is useful.

At the fifth hour the household assembled in the atrium to hear the day's orders, and Cassia stood at the foot of the impluvium with her hands folded and told them. There were twenty-three people in the house, counting the children, and she knew every name, every debt, every ailment, and every grudge. She knew that Philumena the hairdresser had a brother in the vigiles who could be asked to forget a fire. She knew that Celer's wife in the Subura was pregnant again and that Celer was frightened. She knew that Bathyllus had a bad knee and would not admit it. She knew that Ione had been saving money for two years and had not yet said what for, and that Chrysis had a brother somewhere, sold south, and had not yet asked her to look for him.

She knew all of this the way she knew the accounts: because she had learned that people are not so different from money, and that both are best understood when you know where they came from and what they are doing to be spent on.

"Tonight," she said, "the senator Proculus is our guest. He is a vain man, and he has a good memory for slights. Do not give him one. He will drink more than he should and say less than he means. If he asks you about the emperor, you admire the emperor. If he asks you about the Senate, you admire the emperor. If he asks you about his son, you have heard that the young man is the hope of his house, and you have heard it from someone who knows."

A ripple of laughter. She let it run, then raised one finger.

"Mistress Chrysis will not be alone with anyone tonight. If a guest asks, he is told she is promised to the house. If he insists, he is told to ask me." Her eyes moved to Chrysis, who was standing with her hands clasped and her gaze on the floor, and who was nineteen and had been in the house for five years and still flinched at the word *promised*. "And no one goes into the strongroom, for any reason, at any hour, even if I send them. If I send them, it is not me."

They dispersed. Chrysis lingered. Cassia had known she would.

"Domina," she said.

"Not now."

"It is only—"

"Not in the atrium, where the walls have ears and the ears have mouths. Come to the garden before the guests."

The garden was winter-thin, the roses cut back to black sticks, the box hedges dark and wet. A fountain played in the middle of it, a bronze boy holding a dolphin, and the water came out of the dolphin's mouth with a sound like distant conversation. Cassia sat on the stone bench and put her hands in her sleeves.

Chrysis stood before her. She had been born somewhere north of the Danube, in a village whose name she no longer remembered, and she had been sold twice before she came here. She had black hair and grey eyes and the kind of beauty that made men lower their voices. She was also, Cassia thought, the only person in the house who did not know how beautiful she was.

"Well?"

"My brother," Chrysis said. "I have heard where he is."

Cassia said nothing.

"A man came to the kitchen door. He says he has seen him. In Puteoli. He is with a fuller. He is not well. The man says for two thousand sesterces he can be bought and brought."

"Two thousand is not a small sum."

"I know it."

"And the man who tells you this is a man who sells information, and information about a boy he has never met is worth exactly what a frightened sister will pay for it." Cassia watched her. "What is his name?"

"Gaius."

"His name. Not the boy's."

Chrysis hesitated. "I do not know his name. I did not ask."

"Then you have not heard where your brother is. You have heard a story that has your brother in it." Cassia's voice was not unkind. "I will have Hermeros ask. If the boy can be found, he will be found. But I will not buy a rumour, and I will not have you buying one either. If this man comes again, he is to be sent to me."

"He will not come again if he thinks there is nothing to be had."

"Then he was not selling your brother. He was selling your hope, which is cheaper and never runs out." Cassia stood. "Tonight you will sit beside the senator and you will be charming and you will not think about Puteoli. Tomorrow we will think about Puteoli."

Chrysis's eyes filled, but she did not cry. She had learned that too. "Yes, domina."

"Chrysis."

"Yes?"

"You are not to give that man money. Not a copper. If he is what he says, he will come back. If he is not, he will find easier fish." Cassia touched her cheek, once, briefly, the way one might touch a statue to see whether it was warm. "Go and have Philumena do your hair. The braid, not the coils. The braid makes you look like a girl who has not yet decided what she is, and men pay for that."

Chrysis went. Cassia stayed in the cold a while longer, listening to the fountain, and thought about the price of a brother, and about the fact that she had once had a price put on her own head. It had been reckoned in sesterces. It had also, in a way she had never been able to explain to anyone, been reckoned in something else.

***

The guests came at the ninth hour, which in December was already dusk. Proculus arrived first, as a senator should, with his son and two clients and a litter that had been cleaned for the occasion. He was sixty, with the soft hands of a man who had never held a tool and the hard eyes of one who had held a grudge. His son, Aulus, was twenty-four and had the look of a man who had been told all his life that he was promising.

Comptus came next, alone but for his secretary, a narrow man with inky fingers. Numerius Vibius Comptus was an equestrian who moved grain for the annona and had made himself useful to men who moved armies. He was forty-four, going grey at the temples, and he had a wife and three children and a habit of looking at Cassia as though she were a question he had almost answered.

The last to arrive was a knight named Felix, who had not been invited and had sent word that he would be grateful for a word. Cassia had him shown into the small room off the atrium, where the light was bad and the seats were low, and she kept him waiting long enough to look at the ceiling and admire the paint.

Marcus Aquilius Felix was thirty-five and handsome in the way a knife is handsome. He had been a delator for six years, and he had grown rich on other men's terror, and he had the manners of a man who had learned them from a book. He wore a signet ring with a bird on it, which was not his family's, because he had no family worth a bird.

"Domina," he said, and kissed her hand.

"You are not on the list."

"I am aware."

"Then you will understand if I ask what you want."

"A word. Not tonight. A word at your convenience." He smiled. "I have recently come into possession of a document that I think would interest you."

"Documents are rarely interesting. People are."

"This one concerns a physician."

Cassia did not move. Her face did not change. But something behind her eyes closed like a door.

"Physicians are always interesting," she said. "They know too much and they charge too much. Which physician?"

"One who is dead," Felix said. "And one who is not."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she said, "You may have five minutes after the second course. Not before. And you will not speak to my women."

"I would not dream of it."

"You would dream of it. Every man dreams of it. But you will not do it." She rose. "Come. The senator will be wondering whether I have forgotten him, and a senator who wonders is a senator who talks."

The dining room was warm and smelled of rosemary and roasting fish and the cedar oil the slaves had rubbed into the tables. The walls were painted with scenes of the countryside: vines, a villa, a hunter with a spear, a woman in a yellow dress carrying a basket. The three Graces stood in the alcove, their painted hands on one another's shoulders, their painted eyes on the guests. Lamplight moved over them, so that they seemed to breathe.

Cassia took her place at the head of the middle couch, and the room arranged itself around her. Proculus reclined at her right, his son at his feet. Comptus at her left, with Ione. Chrysis on the outer side, where she could be seen and not touched. Dama stood behind the wine, young and dark and beautiful in the way that made men uncertain of their own taste. The first course came: eggs, olives, oysters, a dish of mushrooms in garum, bread still warm from the bakery next door.

"Tell me," Proculus said, when the oysters were gone, "is it true what they say about the emperor's new forum?"

"That depends what they say."

"That it cost more than the Dacian war."

"The Dacian war cost the emperor a great deal of trouble," Cassia said. "The forum cost him money. He has more money."

Proculus laughed too loudly. "They say you have more money than the Senate."

"Then they say too much, and you should not repeat it."

"I am among friends."

"You are among my friends," Cassia said, "and I am not sure you have been introduced."

Comptus laughed into his cup. Proculus, who was not stupid, decided to be amused. "I have missed you," he said.

"You have missed my cook."

"That too." He leaned closer. The wine had loosened him. "I am told the Asian command will be decided before the spring. Corvinus wants it."

"Does he?"

"He has the votes. He has the name. He has that mother of his, who is worth three tribes on her own." Proculus bit an olive. "But there is talk. There is always talk."

"Talk is the cheapest thing in Rome," Cassia said. "I sell it myself."

"This is not my talk. This is talk with a document in its hand."

She did not look at Felix, who was seated at the far end of the room with a cup he had not touched. She picked up a mushroom and ate it slowly.

"Documents," she said, "are like mushrooms. You should know where they were picked before you put them in your mouth."

Proculus laughed again, and the laugh turned into a cough, and his son thumped him on the back with more enthusiasm than was required. The talk moved on to the games, the price of mules, a Vestal who had been seen at a wedding she should not have attended. At the second course the fish came in on a silver platter, and Dama sang, and Ione leaned into Proculus's ear and said something that made him put down his cup.

Cassia watched the room the way a general watches a field. Proculus was talking now, and the wine was talking with him. Comptus was quiet, which meant he was worried. His secretary had told Hermeros that the grain fleet had lost two ships off Sicily, and that Comptus had been asked to explain it to a man who did not accept explanations.

Young Aulus had had too much wine and was leaning toward Chrysis, not quite touching her, but close enough that his intention was visible from across the room. Cassia saw Chrysis's hands tighten on the edge of the couch. She saw the girl look toward the door, and then toward her. She saw her decide to endure it, because enduring it was what she had been trained to do.

"Aulus," Cassia said.

The young man turned, flushed. "Domina?"

"Your father is telling us about the grain fleet. It is a subject on which he is very fine. You should listen. You may be asked about it one day, when you are in the Senate, and it would be a pity if you had spent your youth learning only one thing."

The room went quiet. Proculus's face did something complicated. Aulus's flush deepened. He started to speak, and his father put a hand on his arm.

"The lady is right," Proculus said. "Sit back. Listen. You might learn something."

"I was only—"

"Sit back."

He sat back. Chrysis breathed. Cassia lifted her cup to Proculus and said, "Your son has spirit. It is a fine thing in a young man. It is a better thing when it has a rein."

"You have a gift," Proculus said, "for making a compliment sound like a sentence."

"I have a gift for both. It is the same gift."

Comptus said, quietly, "The grain fleet lost two ships."

"I heard."

"One of them was carrying my name."

"Then your name is on the bottom of the sea, and you should be glad it is not on the Senate's list." She turned to him. "What do you need?"

"Time."

"Time is what I sell least of."

"Then a friend on the prefect's staff."

"I have no friends on the prefect's staff. I have acquaintances who owe me. It is better." She thought. "Come to the house on the day after the Ides. Bring the manifest. Bring the names of the men who signed it. Do not bring your secretary, because your secretary has been talking to someone, and I do not yet know who."

Comptus's face did not change, but his hand did. "You are sure?"

"I am never sure. I am often right." She touched his wrist. "Eat. The fish is good, and a worried man should eat."

When the plates were taken away, Ione came and sat beside her, which she did not usually do in front of guests. Ione was twenty-seven and had been in the house for nine years, and she had the beauty that improves with use, like a knife. She was Greek, from a family that had once had a name in Ephesus and had lost it to a war and a bad loan. She spoke three languages and could do sums in her head and had never once asked Cassia for anything outright, which was how Cassia knew that she wanted a great deal.

"You gave the girl to the senator's son," Ione said, quietly, watching the room.

"I gave her nothing. I took her away from him."

"He will be back."

"Then I will take her away from him again."

"And when you are not here?"

Cassia looked at her. "I am always here."

Ione smiled, and it was not a warm smile, though it was a beautiful one. "You are always here, domina. But the house is not only you. The house is all of us, and some of us will be here when you are not, and some of us will have to make our own arrangements." She turned her cup by the stem. "I am not asking for anything."

"You are asking me to think about it."

"I am asking you to think about what happens to a girl like Chrysis when the house is sold, or the house is closed, or the house is raided by men with a warrant and a grudge. You think about the future. You must have thought about it. You have thought about everything else."

"I have thought about it," Cassia said.

"And?"

"And it is not a conversation for the dinner table."

Ione inclined her head. "No. It is not." She rose, and then, because she was Ione, she said one more thing, softly, with her back to the room: "You should know that the grain man has been to see Pactus twice this month. I have it from his secretary's woman. He is not as frightened as he looks. He is frightened of the wrong thing."

She went back to her place. Cassia sat with her cup and did not look at Comptus, and thought about the wrong thing, and about how a man who is frightened of the wrong thing is more dangerous than a man who is frightened of the right one.

At the third course Felix rose and came to her, and she led him into the little room off the atrium, where the lamps had been turned down and the painted ceiling was lost in shadow.

"Five minutes."

He did not waste them. "There is a man in the city who is preparing an accusation against Lucius Sulpicius Corvinus. The accusation concerns the death of his father, twenty-four years ago, at Tibur. The man has a witness. The witness is old and frightened, and has been paid a little, and will be paid more. The accusation will be laid before the consul after the Kalends. I am not the man. I am the man who knows the man."

"Why tell me?"

"Because everyone knows that the lady of this house remembers everything, and because I would rather be your friend than your enemy."

"You have been my enemy for six years."

"I have been your competitor," Felix said. "It is not the same."

"It is the same when you lose." She looked at him. "What do you want?"

"Two hundred thousand sesterces."

"That is a great deal for a rumour."

"It is a small sum for a life."

"Whose life?"

"Whosever you choose," Felix said. "The witness can remember the physician. Or the physician's daughter. Or the lady who carried the cup. Memory is a house with many doors. I am offering you the keys."

Cassia stood very still. In the other room, Dama was singing, and the sound came through the wall as though from another world. She thought of a summer twenty-four years gone, of a closed door, of the taste of honey and bitter almond. She thought of the way a man's face changes when he is certain he has won.

"Five minutes are over," she said.

Felix smiled. "I will come for your answer on the day after the Ides."

"Come on the Ides. I do not like to be kept waiting, and I do not like to be hurried, and a man who arrives early shows that he does not trust his own case."

He bowed and went out. She stood alone in the little room for a moment, and then she went back to her guests, and her face was the face of a woman who had heard nothing at all.

***

The house emptied slowly. Proculus was the last to go, because Proculus was always the last to go, and at the door he took Cassia's hand and said, "You are wasted on this house."

"I am not wasted anywhere," she said. "I am invested."

He laughed, and his litter carried him away, and the doorkeeper closed the door and shot the bolt, and the house let out its breath.

The slaves cleared the tables. The women went up. Celer came out of the kitchen with flour on his arms and asked, with the dignity of an artist, whether the fish had been acceptable, and Cassia told him it had been excellent, and he went back to his kitchen satisfied. She walked the rooms as she did every night, checking the lamps, the locks, the shutters. In the dining room the three Graces looked down at the wreck of the feast. Someone had spilled wine on the floor, and the stain was already drying to the colour of old blood.

She was in the corridor when Chrysis came to her, wrapped in a mantle, her hair down.

"Domina."

"I told you. Tomorrow."

"I know what he is," Chrysis said. "The man who says he has my brother. I know what he is. I know he is probably lying. But if there is one chance in a hundred that he is not, and I do nothing, then I am the kind of person who does nothing." Her voice shook, but it did not break. "I do not want to be that kind of person."

Cassia looked at her for a long time. The lamp between them was burning low, and the smell of the oil was sharp.

"Neither do I," she said. "Go to bed. In the morning, we will find your brother. I will pay the man, and if he is lying, I will have Bathyllus explain to him the difference between a lie and a theft. If he is not lying, we will bring the boy here and Narcissus will look at him, and he will be fed, and he will sleep in a bed with a roof over it. And you will owe me nothing but the truth about what he is to you."

Chrysis stared at her. "Why?"

"Because I was once sold," Cassia said, "and no one came."

She had been nineteen when the man had come for her, and she remembered the cart, and the smell of the road, and the way the driver had counted the money twice. She remembered the door of the room at Puteoli and the sound of the bolt. She had spent a great deal of her life since then arranging things so that she would never again be on the wrong side of a door, and she had discovered that the arrangement was never finished, and that a bolt is a thing that can be put on either side.

She said it flatly, without pity, the way one reports the weather. Chrysis's eyes filled. She took Cassia's hand and pressed it to her lips, and Cassia let her, and then sent her to bed.

It was past midnight when Bathyllus knocked at the door of the tablinum. He had a folded tablet in his hand, sealed with wax the colour of rust.

"A man brought it," he said. "A steward. He would not wait."

"From whom?"

"He said his master would be known to you."

Cassia broke the seal. The wax was stamped with a raven, wings spread, a small thing and old. She knew the seal before she knew the hand. She had seen it on a letter once, a lifetime ago, a letter that had been read to her by a man who could read and who had been paid to read it slowly.

She unfolded the tablet.

*L. Sulpicius Corvinus to Antonia Cassia. I will do myself the honour of calling upon you on the day after the Ides, at the tenth hour. I trust the house receives.*

There was no greeting, no closing, no explanation. Twenty-four years, and four lines.

Cassia stood in the lamplight with the tablet in her hands. Her face was calm. Her hands were not. She set the tablet on the desk, and then she picked it up again and read it a second time, as though the words might have changed, and then she held the edge of it against the flame of the lamp and watched the wax soften and the wood begin to blacken.

She did not burn it. She put it down, and sat, and looked at the raven until the lamp guttered.

"Bathyllus," she said.

"Domina."

"When he comes, he is to be shown into the garden room. Not the atrium. Not the painted room. The garden room, where the light is behind him and the door is at his back."

"And if he brings his lictors?"

"He will not bring his lictors. He will bring his pride, and it is heavier." She turned the tablet over. "And Bathyllus—"

"Yes?"

"Have the boy bring more lamps. I want the room bright enough that I can see his face."
