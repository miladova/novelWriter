The slave market stood in the shadow of the Temple of Castor, in the hollow of the Forum where the ground had been paved over the ruins of the old republican shops, and it opened at the third hour and closed at noon, because the dealers had discovered long ago that a man who is hungry is a man who bargains badly. There were pens along the portico and a raised platform in the middle, and on the platform, in the morning light, stood the merchandise.

Cassia came in a plain litter with Hermeros and two of the kitchen boys, and she stood at the edge of the crowd with her veil drawn and her hands folded, and she looked at the platform and did not allow herself the luxury of feeling anything.

There were forty of them that morning. A group of Gauls, big and fair and frightened, with the chalk marks on their feet that meant they were new to the city and had not yet learned to run. A Greek boy of sixteen with a lyre and a pretty face and the sort of eyes that had already learned what his price was. Two women from the Danube, sisters perhaps, holding hands. An old man with a cough who had been a gardener somewhere and would not last the year. A Nubian with a brand on his shoulder. A child of six or seven, alone, with a placard around his neck that said *good with animals.*

The placards were the worst of it. They listed everything: the origin, the skills, the languages, the flaws. *Runaway, twice.* *Epileptic.* *Barren.* *Speaks Greek and Latin, reads, writes, honest.* *Mute.* *Sings.* *Bad temper.* The dealers wrote them in chalk or paint and hung them around the neck, and the customers came and read them the way they read the edicts on the walls of the Forum, and the people wearing them stood very still and looked at nothing.

The dealer was a man named Verus, who had a stall under the portico and a slave to do the shouting and a bad chest and a good memory, and who had been in the trade for thirty years and had bought and sold, by his own estimate, nine thousand people. He knew Cassia by sight. Everyone in that business knew her by sight, because she had been sold twice herself and had once stood where his stock was standing now, and because there is no trade in the world with a longer memory than the trade in people.

"Domina," he said, when she came under the portico. "You do me honour."

"I do you business. There is a difference."

"There is. But honour is cheaper." He coughed into his hand. "Your man has been here. He wants the northern boy. The sick one."

"I want the northern boy."

"He is not much. He is fourteen, maybe fifteen, he has been beaten and he has a cough and he does not speak unless he is spoken to. I would not sell him to a farm. He would not last the season. I would sell him to a house, if the house were patient." Verus looked at her. "Four thousand."

"Two."

"Four. He cost me two and I have fed him for a month and I have had a physician look at him, which was a waste of money, because the physician said what I already knew." Verus spat. "Three and a half, and I will throw in the blanket."

"Three, and you will throw in the blanket, and you will tell me who else has been asking."

The dealer's face did not change, but his eyes did. "I do not talk about my customers."

"You do not have customers for a boy who is not on the platform. You have a boy in a back room and a knight who came asking and a woman who is offering you three thousand in cash." Cassia smiled. "I am not asking you to betray a confidence. I am asking you to tell me something I already know, so that I do not have to pay someone else to tell me. It is cheaper for both of us."

Verus looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, "Three thousand two hundred, and the blanket, and I will tell you that the knight had a bird on his ring and did not give his name and came twice."

"Three thousand two hundred," Cassia said. "Done."

She had been sold twice. The first time she had been twelve and had stood on a platform in Rome with her mother's song still in her head and a placard around her neck that said *quick, clean, honest, can sew.* The second time she had been eighteen and had not stood on a platform at all; she had been sold in a back room at Tibur, with a steward counting the money and a woman holding the lamp, and that had been worse, because a platform is at least public, and a public thing can be witnessed, and there is a kind of mercy in having witnesses. No one had witnessed the second sale. She had gone into that room a maid with a name and had come out of it a thing with a price, and the price had been written down in a book, and the book had been put in a drawer, and the drawer had been locked, and the woman who kept the key had gone to bed and slept.

She looked at the platform, and the placards, and the people wearing them, and she made herself look at each one, because she had learned that the only way to survive a thing like this was to see it clearly and not look away.

Hermeros had done the negotiating in the days before. He had found the fuller's household at Puteoli and had learned that the fuller had bought two boys from a dealer in Rome three years before, and that one of them had been sold on to a farm at Aricia, and that the other was still there, and that the fuller would sell him for three thousand sesterces, which was more than the boy was worth.

"He is not on the platform," Hermeros said. "The dealer is holding him in the back. He wants to sell him privately."

"Why?"

"Because he is sick, and a sick slave on the platform is bad for business." Hermeros's face was carefully blank. "And because a man came asking about him last week. A knight. The dealer would not say which one."

Cassia's hands tightened on her veil. "Felix."

"It is possible."

"Then we buy him today, and we buy him quietly, and we do not ask the dealer any questions that will make him raise the price." She looked at the platform. "How much does he want?"

"Four thousand now."

"Pay it."

"Domina, four thousand is—"

"Pay it," Cassia said, "and then take him to the house and have Narcissus see him before I do. I do not want to see him in this place. I want to see him in a bed with clean linen."

Hermeros went. Cassia stayed at the edge of the crowd and watched the platform, because she had learned a long time ago that a woman who is seen to be impatient is a woman who is charged more.

The auction went on. The Gauls were sold to a builder. The Greek boy went to a household on the Quirinal for a sum that made even the dealer raise his eyebrows. The two sisters were sold together, which was a mercy, and to a brothel keeper, which was not. The old gardener was not sold at all, and was led away at noon to be given bread and water and kept for the next market, and Cassia, who had seen the old gardener, and who knew what happened to old men who were not sold, found that she was looking at the child with the placard and thinking about a cell at Tibur.

The child looked back at her. He had a smudge of dirt on his cheek and the flat, patient eyes of a person who has learned that looking at the crowd is a way of surviving it.

Verus took her through the portico to the back, past a curtain and a counter and a room where a clerk sat with a ledger and a pot of ink, and into a yard where the stock that was not for the platform waited in the shade. There were eight of them. A woman with a baby at her breast. Two boys asleep against the wall. A man with a broken arm in a sling. And, in the corner, a boy sitting on a stool with a blanket around his shoulders and his hands in his lap.

He did not look up when they came in.

"Melanthus," Verus said. "This is the lady."

The boy looked up. He looked at Cassia for exactly as long as it took to see her, and then he looked at the ground again, and she understood in that moment that he had been looked at by a great many people in the past month, and that each of them had been deciding what he was worth, and that he had learned to make himself small so that the decision would be quick.

"Leave us," Cassia said.

Verus hesitated. "Domina—"

"Leave us. I will not steal him. I have a house full of witnesses and a doorkeeper who could break you in half." She waited until he had gone, and then she crouched down in front of the boy, which hurt her knees, and which she did not allow him to see. "I am not going to touch you. I am not going to ask you to stand up or turn around or show me your teeth. I am going to ask you one question, and you may answer it or not."

The boy said nothing.

"Did they take you from your village, or were you born a slave?"

"Taken," the boy said. His voice was a scrape.

"Did they take your sister too?"

The boy's head came up. For the first time there was something in his eyes that was not the flat patient waiting. It was fear, and under the fear, something else, something that had been kept alive in the dark for five years and had not quite gone out.

"Yes," he said.

"Domina," Hermeros said, at her elbow.

She turned. The boy was standing behind him, between two of the dealer's men, with a blanket around his shoulders and his feet bare. He was fourteen or fifteen, thin to the point of gauntness, with the pale skin and light hair of the north and a bruise fading on his cheekbone. He was not looking at her. He was looking at the ground.

"His name is Melanthus," Hermeros said. "That is what the fuller called him. His own name is something else. He does not say it."

"Melanthus," Cassia said.

The boy looked up. His eyes were grey and old, and there was nothing in them at all: no fear, no hope, no curiosity. She had seen that look before. She had worn it, once, in a house in Puteoli, in the first month, before Thais had taught her to keep her name to herself.

"Come," she said. "You are going to a house where no one will touch you."

The boy said nothing. He came.

***

The house on the Esquiline received him the way it received everything: with hot water and clean linen and a great deal of noise. Thais took charge. She had the boy put in the small room off the kitchen where the under-cook slept, and had the under-cook moved in with the kitchen boys, and had a bath drawn, and had Celer make broth, and had Narcissus come at once with his bag.

Narcissus was sixty-one, a Greek from Pergamon with a face like a walnut and the manners of a man who had once been rich. He had been the physician of a consular household until the consular had died and the household had sold him, and Cassia had bought him for the price of a good horse and had never regretted it. He examined the boy in the small room with the door shut, and came out after half an hour and sat down in the kitchen with his hands on his knees.

"Well?"

"His back is bad. There are scars, old ones, from a whip, and newer ones from a rod. He has been beaten on the soles of his feet. There is a cough that has been in his chest for a long time and could become something worse, and he is underfed, and he has a fever that comes and goes." Narcissus's face was impassive. "He will live if he is fed and kept warm and not beaten. He will not live if he is sent back."

"He will not be sent back."

"Then he will live." Narcissus reached into his bag. "There is something else. He does not speak much, but when he does, he speaks Greek with a northern accent, and he remembers a village and a mother and a sister. He remembers a raid, and men with swords, and a cart. He remembers his sister's name."

Cassia's heart turned over. "What is the name?"

"Chrysis," Narcissus said. "He says it the way a child says a prayer. He has been saying it for five years."

***

Chrysis was in the corridor when Cassia came out. She had been standing there for an hour, because no one had told her to go away, and because Thais had looked at her and decided that this was not a thing that could be managed by orders.

"Is it him?" she said.

"Go in," Cassia said. "He is tired and he is frightened and he may not know you. Do not rush at him. Do not cry. Sit down on the stool and talk to him about the village, and if he does not answer, keep talking."

Chrysis's face was white. "What if he does not remember me?"

"He remembers you," Cassia said. "He has been saying your name for five years."

Chrysis went in. Cassia stood in the corridor with her back against the wall and listened. There was silence for a long time, and then a sound, a single, terrible, broken sound, and then two voices, low and quick and urgent, in a language she did not speak. She stood there with her eyes closed and her hands flat against the plaster, and she thought about a cart on the road to Puteoli and a cry going away from her, getting smaller, until it was only a sound in a dream.

Thais came down the corridor with a lamp and stopped when she saw her.

"Domina."

"I am all right."

"You are not all right. You are standing in a corridor in the dark with your hands on the wall."

"I am all right," Cassia said again. "I am not the one who was sold."

Thais looked at her for a long moment. Then she set the lamp down on the bracket and put her arms around her, and Cassia let herself be held, which was a thing she allowed perhaps twice a year, and only in the dark.

***

They ate together that night in the small dining room, all of them: Cassia and Thais and Chrysis and the boy, and Hermeros, who had been invited because he had done the buying and because Cassia wanted him to see what he had bought. The boy had been bathed and dressed in a clean tunic and had been given a bowl of broth and a piece of bread, and he ate slowly, watching everyone, the way a dog that has been kicked watches a room.

"Melanthus," Cassia said.

The boy looked up.

"That is not your name."

He said nothing.

"Your name is your own. If you want to tell us, you will tell us. If you do not, we will call you Melanthus until you do." She set down her cup. "I bought you today for four thousand sesterces, which is more than you are worth, and I did it because your sister asked me to. I am not your mother. I am not your mistress. I am the woman who owns the house you are sitting in, and I own the roof and the bread and the bed, and I do not own you. Do you understand the difference?"

The boy looked at her. His eyes were old and grey and entirely without illusion.

"You bought me," he said. His voice was hoarse from disuse.

"Yes."

"Then you own me."

"No," Cassia said. "The law says I own you. The law is a thing men wrote down. It is not the truth." She leaned forward. "The truth is that when a person is bought and sold, something is done to them that cannot be undone by a piece of paper. You are not the same as you were before. You will never be the same. But that does not mean that I am the same as the man who sold you, or the fuller who beat you, or the men who took you from your village. It means that I have bought you, and that you are in my house, and that I will not beat you, and that in a year, if you want it, I will free you and give you money and a trade and a name that is your own. That is the difference."

The boy looked at her for a long time. Then he said, "Why?"

"Because no one did it for me," Cassia said.

Chrysis made a sound. Cassia did not look at her.

"Eat your broth," she said. "Tomorrow Narcissus will put something on your back and it will hurt, and the day after that it will hurt less. In a week you will be able to walk to the end of the street. In a month you will be able to work, if you want to work, and if you do not want to work you will have to learn to be bored." She picked up her cup. "There is no shame in being bored. There is a great deal of shame in the world, and most of it is deserved, but boredom is not one of the things anyone should be ashamed of."

"What is your name?" Chrysis said.

The boy looked at her. He had eaten most of the broth and his colour was better and his eyes were less like a dog's and more like a boy's, and he was tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"Senno," he said.

Chrysis's hand went to her mouth. "Senno."

"You said it wrong," the boy said. "You always said it wrong. It is Sen-no. Two n's. You said Seno, like a Roman."

"I was eight," Chrysis said. "I did not know how to say anything."

"You said it wrong," the boy said again, and then he began to cry, without any noise at all, the way a person cries who has learned that crying is a thing that attracts attention, and Chrysis got up and went around the table and knelt beside him and put her arms around him, and the boy held on to her and shook.

Cassia sat very still. Hermeros looked at his plate. Thais looked at the wall. Nobody said anything for a long time, and the lamp burned, and the house was quiet around them.

Chrysis wiped her face with the back of her hand and looked at him, and the boy looked back at her, and something passed between them that had nothing to do with Cassia and nothing to do with the house and nothing to do with the price that had been paid in the market that morning.

The boy almost smiled. It was a small thing, a flicker at the corner of his mouth, and it was gone almost at once, but Chrysis saw it, and she put her hand over her mouth, and her eyes filled.

***

Felix came at the ninth hour, when the lamps were lit and the house was warm and the streets outside were full of the noise of the evening.

He came with two men and a wax tablet and the smile of a man who has decided to be pleasant. Bathyllus showed him into the tablinum and stood by the door with his arms folded, and Cassia sat behind her desk with the accounts open in front of her and did not stand up.

"Domina," Felix said. "You are looking well."

"You are looking expensive."

"I have been fortunate."

"You have been paid." She closed the account book. "What do you want?"

"Straight to business. I admire that." He sat down without being invited. "You went to the market today."

"I go to the market every week. The household must eat."

"You bought a boy."

"I bought a boy. He was sick and I bought him. It is not a crime to buy a sick boy." Cassia's voice was mild. "What is it to you?"

The smile did not change, but something behind it did. "I have been looking for a boy," Felix said. "A boy of about that age, from the north, who was sold out of a household at Tibur some years ago. A boy who might be worth a great deal to the right person."

Cassia's hands were steady on the desk.

"There are a great many boys from the north who were sold out of households."

"There are. But this one is particular." Felix turned the tablet in his hands. "There is a potter on the Via Ostiensis, outside the Porta Trigemina, who has a son of twenty-four. The boy works in the shop. He has his father's name, which proves nothing, but he has the look of the family, which proves less. I have not been to see him yet. I have been saving him."

"I have a midwife's word for it. And a physician's assistant. And an old steward with a bad chest who is willing to say what he remembers in exchange for a pension and a promise that his daughter will not be told." He looked up. "I am assembling a case, domina. Not against you. Against the senator. But a case is a house, and a house needs a foundation, and the foundation is a story that a court will believe. And the story I am building is that the senator's father was killed by his wife, and that the wife had help, and that the help came from a girl in the household who was pregnant by the son and who was sold to a brothel in Puteoli and who has spent twenty-four years buying her way back into respectable society."

The room was very quiet. Bathyllus moved, and Cassia raised one finger, and he stopped.

"That is a very good story," Cassia said. "It has everything. A noble house, a wicked mother, a slave girl, a bastard child. The only thing it lacks is proof."

"I am getting proof."

"Then get it." Cassia stood. "And when you have it, bring it to me, and I will tell you what it is worth. Until then, you will not come to my house at night with two men and a tablet and a story. You will not speak to my household. You will not go to Tibur, and if you do, you will find that the road is longer than you think." She came around the desk. "And Felix. If you go near the boy, or the midwife, or the senator's daughter, I will not pay you. I will not bargain with you. I will find out what you love and I will take it away from you, and it will not be a thing you can buy back with a tablet and a smile."

Felix rose. He was taller than her, and he used it, the way men do.

"You are threatening a Roman knight," he said.

"I am promising a Roman knight. There is a difference." Cassia smiled. "You have three days. After that, the price is off the table and the game changes."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed, and it was a real laugh, and there was something in it that was almost admiration.

"You are the most dangerous woman in Rome," he said.

"So I am told."

He went out with his two men, and Bathyllus bolted the door behind them, and Cassia stood in the tablinum with her hands shaking and the lamp burning and the whole house breathing around her.

"Domina," Bathyllus said. "He will go to Tibur."

"I know."

"He will get there before you do."

"I know that too." Cassia went to the strongroom panel and pressed the catch and took out the black book and set it on the desk. "Which is why you are going tonight, and not in the morning. Take the flower seller's boy, and the fast horse from the baker's stable, and ride through the night. You will be at Tibur by dawn. You will go to the house with the fig tree and you will bring the old woman out, and her box, and her granddaughter if she will come, and you will put them in a cart and you will bring them to Rome by the back road, and you will not stop for anything."

Bathyllus looked at her. "And if the old woman will not come?"

"Then you will tell her that the lady from Rome is coming, and that she is coming with a warrant and a magistrate and the whole weight of the law, and that if she wants to tell her story to a court she can tell it in Rome, in a room with a fire and a chair and a cup of wine, instead of in a stable in Tibur with a man from the city asking her questions." Cassia opened the book. "She has kept something for twenty-four years, Bathyllus. She has kept it because she was afraid. Tell her that the woman she was afraid of is dying, and that the son is not his father, and that there is a house in Rome with a warm room in it and a door that locks from the inside."

Bathyllus nodded. At the door he said, "And you, domina?"

"I will be here," Cassia said. "I will be here, and I will be visible, and I will be at the Senate tomorrow with the senator's wife, and every man in this city will see me being respectable." She looked up. "Go. And Bathyllus—"

"Yes?"

"If the old woman has a box, do not open it. Not for any reason. Bring it to me sealed."

He went out. Cassia sat alone with the black book open in front of her and the lamp burning and the house settling into the night. Then she turned to a page near the end, where the entries were not accounts but names, and she wrote: *Felix. Wife deceased. Two children, a boy of nine and a girl of six, in the house of his sister on the Aventine. Sister's husband is a corn merchant named Sabinus, who owes money to three men and has been cheating the annona for two years.* She looked at it for a long time. Then she closed the book, and put it back in the panel, and went up to bed.

Thais came in with a cup of warm milk and set it on the desk without being asked.

"You have written something in the book," she said. "Something you are not proud of."

"I have written what I know."

"You know a great deal about a man's children."

Cassia looked at the page. "If he comes for my household, I will use it. A man who threatens a house should know that his own house is not safe."

"And will you use it? The sister on the Aventine? The boy of nine and the girl of six?"

"I do not know."

"You know," Thais said. "You are not that woman. You have never been that woman. You have done a great many things that would make a Vestal faint, and you have ruined men, and you have taken money from people who could not afford it, and you have kept secrets that ought to have been told. But you have never once hurt a child, and you will not start now, at forty-two, with a boy asleep in your kitchen."

Cassia was silent.

"I will keep the entry," she said at last. "It is useful. He does not know what I will do and neither do I, and that is the point of a threat." She closed the book. "But you are right. I will not touch the children. If it comes to it, I will find another way."

"Good," Thais said. "Drink your milk."

She did not sleep. She lay in the dark and listened to the house, and at some hour past midnight she heard the garden door open and close, and the sound of a horse on the stones, and then nothing. She thought about a boy with grey eyes and a bruise on his cheekbone, and about a midwife with a box under her bed, and about a cry in the night that had been going away from her for twenty-four years and was now, perhaps, beginning to come back.
