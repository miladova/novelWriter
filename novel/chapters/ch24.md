There was no speech. She had thought about writing one, and had got as far as the second line, and had put the tablet in the fire, because she had learned that speeches are for the Senate and that a room with a potter in it is not the Senate.

She spent the night before in the kitchen with Thais and Ione and Celer, and they drank wine and did not talk about the visit, and at midnight Celer made a cake that nobody wanted, and at one in the morning Sabina came down in her nightgown and sat on the bench and said, "Is he nice?"

"I do not know," Cassia said. "He makes cups and he laughs at things in doorways and he has a father who calls him in to supper."

"That does not tell you if he is nice."

"No," Cassia said. "It tells you that he is loved. It is not the same thing, but it is a good start."

At two in the morning Thais sent everyone to bed and sat with Cassia in the empty kitchen.

"There is a room at the top of the stairs," she said. "It has been empty for twenty-four years. If he comes, it will not be a room for him. He has a room. He has a house and a father and a trade. He does not need a room."

"I know."

"Then why have you been up there all week, dusting?"

Cassia looked at her hands. "Because it is the only thing I can do," she said. "I cannot give him back the twenty-four years. I cannot make his mother alive again. I cannot undo what was done to him or to me or to his father. I can dust a room and put clean linen on the bed and open the window, and that is all, and it is ridiculous, and I am going to do it anyway."

Thais reached across the table and took her hand.

"It is not ridiculous," she said. "It is the most sensible thing you have done in a year."

It had taken three weeks to arrange, and it had been arranged in the way that everything important in Cassia's life was arranged: by letter, by patience, and by other people's courage.

She had written to Gaius Vibidius three days after the cups came, in the last week of November, and he had not answered. She had written again a week later, and he had come to the house in the rain at the beginning of December and stood in the tablinum with his apron in his hands and said, "I cannot do it. I have tried four times. The words will not come out of my mouth."

"Then I will say them," Cassia said.

"He will hate me."

"He will not hate you. He may hate me. He has a right to." She had looked at him across the desk. "But he is twenty-four years old, and he has a father who has lied to him for four months, and every day that we wait is a day that he spends knowing that something is wrong and being told that it is not. He is not a child. He is a man with a trade and a girl in Ostia and a great deal of patience, and he deserves better than a lie."

The potter had cried then, quietly, into his apron, and Cassia had sat with him until he stopped.

"Bring him to the shop on the day before the Ides," she said. "I will come in the morning. Tell him that a lady is coming who has been his patron, and that she has something to tell him, and that he is to hear her out and not to speak until she is done. And be there. Whatever he says to me, be there, because he will need to look at a face he trusts."

The potter had nodded and gone out into the rain.

The Via Ostiensis was loud in the morning. Carts going down to the river and carts coming up from it, porters with sacks on their shoulders, a man with a wheelbarrow full of fish, two women arguing over a basket of onions, a dog asleep in the middle of the road and refusing to move. The potter's shop stood on the corner of a lane with a fig tree beside the door and a yard behind it where the kiln was, and the smoke from the kiln went up into the grey December air and smelled of hot clay and wood ash.

Cassia came on foot, with Bathyllus behind her and Corvinus beside her, and they stopped at the corner of the lane and looked at the shop.

"I do not know if I can do this," Corvinus said.

"You can."

"I have spoken in the Senate a hundred times. I have stood in front of the emperor. I have been accused of murdering my father and I did not shake." He looked at the door. "And I am standing in a street on the Via Ostiensis and my hands are shaking."

"That is because the Senate did not matter," Cassia said. "This does."

Gaius Vibidius came out of the shop and saw them and wiped his hands on his apron and came to meet them.

"He is in the yard," he said. "I have told him that there is a lady who has been his patron, and that she has something to tell him, and that it is important and that he is to listen. He thinks it is about money." The potter's mouth twisted. "He asked me if he was to be apprenticed somewhere else. He asked me if I was selling the shop."

"Did you tell him anything else?"

"No. I said that I would be there, and that his mother would have wanted him to hear it, and that was all." He looked at Corvinus. "You are the senator."

"I am."

"You look like him." The potter said it without warmth. "He has your jaw. I have been looking at it for twenty-four years and I did not know what it was until this summer."

Corvinus said nothing. The potter turned and led them through the shop and out into the yard.

---

The yard was small and busy: racks of drying pots, a table with tools, a boy of twelve turning a wheel in the corner, a shed with the kiln in it, and a great heap of clay under a wet cloth. The young man was standing at the table with a cup in his hand, turning it over, looking at the rim.

He looked up when they came in.

He was tall — taller than Corvinus, taller than Cassia had remembered — and he had his sleeves rolled up over his forearms and his hands and his arms were grey with clay to the elbow, and his hair was the colour of dark honey and needed cutting. He had the family jaw and the family nose and his father's eyes, and he was, Cassia thought, the most beautiful thing she had ever seen.

"Domina," he said, and put the cup down. "My father says you have something to tell me."

"Sit down," Cassia said.

"I would rather stand."

"Then stand." She looked at him. "My name is Antonia Cassia. I keep a house on the Esquiline. I am a freedwoman and I have been free for sixteen years, and before that I was a slave, and before that I was a girl in a kitchen at a villa in Tibur." She paused. "And twenty-four years ago, in the autumn, I gave birth to a son in a cell at the back of that villa, and I was told that he had died, and I was sold to a house in Puteoli three days later."

The young man looked at her. His face did not change.

"I do not understand," he said.

"You are the son," Cassia said. "Your name is Gaius Vibidius Nestor, and you were born on the twenty-eighth of September in the year of the consulship of Domitian and Silanus, and you were sold to Gaius Vibidius, potter, of the Via Ostiensis, for four hundred sesterces, and you have been raised by him as his own." She paused. "And I have spent twenty-four years believing you were dead."

The yard was very quiet. The boy at the wheel had stopped turning. The kiln hissed. The young man looked at her, and then at his father, and then at Corvinus, and his face was doing something complicated: not disbelief, not grief, but the slow, terrible work of a mind rearranging everything it had ever known.

"My mother," he said. "My mother is dead. She died when I was small."

"Your mother is the woman who raised you, and she is dead, and I am sorry for it," Cassia said. "I am not her. I am the woman who gave birth to you. There is a difference, and I do not know yet what it is worth."

"Who are you?" He turned to Corvinus. "Who is he?"

Corvinus stepped forward. "My name is Lucius Sulpicius Corvinus. I am a senator. I was twenty years old in the summer of eighty-eight, and I was in love with the girl in the kitchen, and I was told that she had died in childbirth and that the child had died with her." His voice was steady. "I did not know you existed until this year. I did not know that she was alive. I have spent twenty-four years believing that I had killed you both."

The young man looked at him for a long time. Then he said, "You are my father."

"Yes."

"And you"—he turned to Cassia—"are my mother."

"Yes."

"And my father"—he turned to Gaius Vibidius—"is my father."

"Yes," the potter said. "I bought you, and I raised you, and I have loved you every day since I carried you through that door in a basket, and I did not know that you had a mother who was alive, and if I had known I would have told you." His voice broke. "I would have told you. I want you to believe that."

The young man looked at the three of them standing in the yard.

"I need to sit down," he said.

---

They sat in the back room of the shop, on stools, with the door open to the yard and the smell of clay coming in. The potter brought a jug of water and a cup for each of them, and they sat in a row like people at a funeral.

Cassia told him everything. She did not spare herself and she did not spare Corvinus and she did not spare the old woman. She told him about the vineyard and the store-room and the book of poems. She told him about the night and the cup and the cry. She told him about Puteoli, and the green door, and Thais, and the four years. She told him about the fire and the trial and the empress and the sealed record. She told him about the chest of jewellery and the money that was waiting for him and the letter the old woman had written with a shaking hand.

It took an hour.

The young man listened without interrupting. Twice he got up and walked to the door and stood looking out at the yard, and came back and sat down. Once he put his face in his hands and sat like that for a long time.

"Tell me about her," he said, when Cassia had finished the first part. "My mother. The one who raised me. What did she look like?"

Cassia looked at Gaius Vibidius. The potter's face was working.

"She was small and brown and she had a laugh that carried," the potter said. "She could not have children of her own and she prayed at the temple of Juno Lucina for six years, and when I brought you home in a basket she sat down on the floor of the kitchen and cried for an hour, and then she got up and made you a bed in a drawer." He wiped his face. "She died when you were seven. She had a fever and it took her in three days. She called for you at the end. She made me promise to keep you and raise you and never tell you that you were bought, because she was afraid that if you knew, you would think that she had loved you less."

The young man's face was wet.

"I do not remember her," he said. "I remember a woman singing. I remember a hand on my forehead."

"That is her," the potter said. "That is all any of us get."

Cassia sat very still. She had spent twenty-four years imagining this room, and in every version of it she had been the mother, and the boy had been hers, and the woman who had raised him had been a shadow. She had not imagined this: a boy with two mothers and a father who had bought him, and all of them sitting in a room with a jug of water, and nothing to be done about any of it.

"Tell me about the girl," Cassia said, when the worst of it was out. "Your father says there is a girl in Ostia."

The young man looked up, startled, and then his face changed in a way that Cassia would remember for the rest of her life: the face of a man who has been braced for blows and is offered a chair.

"Her name is Vibia," he said. "She is a potter's daughter too. Her father has a yard by the river and he is getting old, and she runs the accounts and the slaves and the carting, and she shouts at the porters." He stopped. "She is not beautiful. She is better than beautiful. She is the sort of woman who walks into a yard and everyone stops arguing."

"She will need to be," Cassia said.

"She does not know anything about any of this."

"Then you will have to tell her. Not tonight. But you will have to tell her, because a secret like this one does not keep, and because if you marry her and she finds out from someone else, she will never forgive you, and she will be right." Cassia paused. "And when you tell her, tell her all of it. Not the version that makes me look well. The version that is true."

The young man looked at her for a moment. Then he said, "You are very like my father. He says the same thing. He says, 'Tell it all, and let them decide.'"

"Then he is a wise man, and you should listen to him."

He was quiet for a while. The kiln hissed, and somewhere beyond the wall a cart went by, and the boy at the wheel began to sing under his breath.

"Did you look for me?" he said.

"Yes," Cassia said. "Every day. For twenty-four years. I did not know your name or where you were, and I looked for you in every crowd, in every street, in every face. I have a room at the top of my house that I have kept empty for twenty-four years because I could not bear to give it to anyone else." She paused. "And I did not look hard enough. I should have gone to every potter in Rome. I should have walked the Via Ostiensis twenty years ago. I did not, because I was afraid, and because I had built a house and a business and a wall, and because I told myself that the child was dead and that looking for him was a kind of madness." She looked at him. "I was wrong. I have been wrong for twenty-four years, and I am sorry."

He looked at her for a long time.

"Why did you give me up?" he said.

"I did not give you up. You were taken from me while I was asleep. I woke up and the room was clean and the midwife told me that you had never breathed, and I was on a cart to Puteoli three days later." Cassia's voice was steady. "I have spent twenty-four years believing that you were dead. I did not give you up. I was robbed of you. There is a difference, and I need you to understand it, because if you go away from this room thinking that I chose to leave you, then the woman who sold you will have won."

The young man put his face in his hands and sat like that for a long time.

"I am angry," he said, at last. "I do not know at who. At her, I suppose. At the old woman. At the banker and the senator and the men with the farm. At you, a little. At my father, a little. At the whole of it." He looked up. "Is that allowed?"

"It is allowed," Cassia said. "It is the most sensible thing you have said all morning."

Then he said, "What do you want from me?"

"Nothing," Cassia said.

"Everyone wants something."

"Yes," she said. "Everyone does. But I am not going to ask you for anything today. I am going to tell you that I am here, and that I have been here for six months, and that I will be here in the spring, and that if you want to know me, you know where I live. And if you do not want to know me, I will understand, and I will not come to this shop again, and once a year your father will send me a message to tell me that you are alive, and that will be enough."

The young man looked at her. His eyes were his father's eyes, and they were wet.

"You have been here for six months," he said.

"Yes."

"You paid for the kiln."

"Yes."

"You wrote to me about the cups."

"Yes."

"And you did not tell me your name."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because I did not want to give you a name that you had not asked for," Cassia said. "Because a person who is told that his whole life has been a lie does not need a stranger at the door with a story. He needs time." She paused. "And because I was afraid."

The young man laughed — a short, broken, entirely unexpected laugh. "You were afraid?"

"I have been afraid every day for twenty-four years," Cassia said. "I was afraid in the cell. I was afraid in Puteoli. I was afraid in the Senate. And I was afraid of you, because you are the only person in the world whose opinion of me matters, and because I did not know whether you would hate me."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, "I do not hate you."

"You may, in a week. It is a great deal to take in."

"I know." He stood up. "I need to work. I need to put my hands in the clay and make something, and think about it, and I cannot do that with three people looking at me." He looked at each of them. "I am not going to say anything today. I am not going to decide anything today. I am going to go out into that yard and make cups, because that is what I do when I do not know what to do."

"That is a very good way of dealing with it," Cassia said.

"It is the only way I know." He stopped at the door. "Domina. Cassia. If I come to the house on the Esquiline. If I want to know you. What do I call you?"

Cassia looked at him. In the doorway, with the light behind him and the clay on his arms and his father's jaw and his grandmother's eyes, he looked like the answer to a question she had been asking for twenty-four years.

"You call me whatever you like," she said. "I have been called a great many things. I have been called a whore and a freedwoman and a patron and a friend. I have never once been called a mother." She paused. "You may call me Cassia, if you like. That is my name. It is the only thing I have that no one gave me."

He nodded. Then he went out into the yard and picked up a lump of clay and sat down at the wheel, and the boy who had been turning it moved aside, and the wheel began to spin.

---

They came out into the shop, and Gaius Vibidius followed them to the door.

"Domina," he said. "A word."

Corvinus went out into the lane. The potter stood in the doorway of his own shop with his apron in his hands and his face grey.

"I have had him for twenty-four years," he said. "I taught him to walk and to read and to throw a pot. I sat up with him when he was sick and I beat him when he was lazy and I have been prouder of him than of anything I have ever made with my hands." He stopped. "And now there is a lady in Rome and a senator on the Palatine and a chest of jewellery and a story about a villa at Tibur, and I am a potter on the Via Ostiensis with a bad chest and a workshop that is mortgaged to my brother."

"You are his father," Cassia said.

"I know that. I am telling you that knowing it does not help." He wiped his face with his apron. "I am afraid that he will go to your house and see the silver and the paintings and the three Graces and the cook and the garden, and he will think: this is what I could have had. And he will look at me and see a man with clay under his nails."

Cassia looked at him. Then she said, "Do you know what he said when I asked him what he does when he does not know what to do?"

"No."

"He said, 'I go out into the yard and make cups, because that is what I do.'" She paused. "That is not a thing he learned from a senator. That is not a thing he learned from a woman with a garden and a cook. That is a thing he learned from a man who taught him to throw a pot." She put her hand on his arm. "You have given him the only thing that cannot be taken away from him. Not the shop. Not the trade. The knowledge that when the world falls apart, there is something he can do with his hands. I could not have given him that. I could have given him a name and a fortune and a great deal of trouble, and you gave him a craft. Do not tell me that you are less than me. You are the reason he is the man he is, and if he goes to my house and sees the silver, he will come home to yours and tell you about it."

The potter looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and went back into the shop.

Cassia and Corvinus stood in the lane outside the shop.

"Well," he said.

"Well."

"He did not throw us out."

"He did not." Cassia looked at the door. "He is a good man. He has a trade and a temper and a father who loves him, and he is going to go out into that yard and make cups, and in a week or a month or a year he will come to the house on the Esquiline and we will have to explain it all over again." She paused. "And I am going to make a mess of it."

"Probably."

"You are supposed to tell me that I will not."

"I have known you for twenty-four years," Corvinus said. "You will make a mess of it, and he will forgive you, and you will make a mess of it again. That is what families are." He looked at her. "I should know. I have been making a mess of mine for twenty years."

They walked back up the Via Ostiensis in the cold, and at the gate of the city Cassia stopped and looked back at the lanes and the workshops and the smoke.

"I am not going to Baiae," she said.

"What?"

"Tertulla wants me to go to Baiae in the spring. To be bored. To sit in the sun." Cassia turned. "I am not going. I am going to stay in Rome, and I am going to be here when he comes, and if he does not come, I am going to be here anyway."

Corvinus looked at her. Then he took her arm, and they went through the gate together into the city.

That evening Corvinus came to the house, and they sat in the tablinum with the lamp between them and did not say anything for a while.

"He asked me what I did," Corvinus said. "In the yard, when you were talking to his father. He asked me what a senator does all day, and I said that a senator listens to other men talk and then talks himself, and he looked at me as though I had told him that I spent my days counting clouds." He laughed. "He asked me if I had ever made anything with my hands. I said that I had made speeches. He said that was not the same thing, and he was right."

"He is twenty-four," Cassia said. "At twenty-four everyone is certain of everything."

"At forty-five I am certain of nothing." He turned the cup. "I have lost the Asian command and the augurate and the house on the Palatine and the name that my father spent his life protecting. My mother is dead and my father is a fraud and my son is a potter who thinks I am useless. And I am sitting in a room with a woman I have loved since I was twenty, and I am happier than I have been in twenty years." He looked up. "What does that make me?"

"A man who has finally got what he wanted," Cassia said.

"I did not want any of this."

"No," she said. "That is why it is funny."

Two days later, a boy came to the green door on the Esquiline with a parcel wrapped in cloth.

There was no letter. Inside the cloth there was a cup — the same red-glossed ware, thin-walled, with the bird on the bottom — and round the rim, scratched into the clay before firing, in a rough and unpractised hand, were four words.

*For the house. From Gaius.*

Cassia stood in the kitchen with the cup in her hands and looked at it for a long time. Then she set it on the shelf above the hearth, next to the cups that had cost her a fortune, and she said, "Nobody is to use this cup. Nobody is to wash it. It is to stay on that shelf until I die, and then it is to be given to my son."

Thais looked at the cup. "He did not call you mother."

"No."

"He did not call you Cassia either."

"No."

"He called himself Gaius."

"Yes," Cassia said. "He called himself by his own name. He did not call himself my son and he did not call himself yours. He called himself Gaius, and he wrote *from* Gaius, as though the house were his to send things from." She turned the cup over. "That is more than I had this morning."

She went out into the garden. The roses were black stumps and the fountain was off and the sky was the colour of pewter, and she stood in the cold with her hands in her sleeves and looked at the fig tree that she had planted there six years before for no reason she had ever been able to explain to anyone.

Young Sulpicia came on the morning of the Saturnalia, and asked to meet her brother.

She came to the house with her mother, and she sat in the tablinum and said, "He is my brother. I have a right to meet him."

"You have a right to ask," Cassia said. "He has a right to refuse."

"Then ask him."

"I will ask him. But you will have to be patient, and you will have to understand that he has had a great deal to take in in a very short time, and that he does not owe you anything." Cassia paused. "And Sulpicia. If he agrees to meet you, you will not call him your brother. Not the first time. You will call him Gaius, and you will ask him about his work, and you will not tell him about the masks or the house on the Palatine or the trial. He knows about all of it. He does not need to hear it from a girl of sixteen who has had a comfortable life and a good marriage and a grandmother who loved her."

Sulpicia's chin came up, and then went down again.

"That is fair," she said.

"Yes."

"I do not like it."

"No," Cassia said. "But you will do it, because you are clever, and because you have discovered this year that being clever is not the same as being right, and because you want to meet him more than you want to be right."

Sulpicia looked at her. Then she said, "You are a very difficult woman."

"So I am told."

When they had gone she went and sat with Thais and Ione, who were arguing about the seating for that evening, and took the list out of Thais's hand and began to write.
