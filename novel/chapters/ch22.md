She did not sleep the night before the second day.

She sat in the tablinum with the lamps burning and the papers spread out on the desk and went through them one more time, and then she put them in the satchel and strapped it and sat with her hands in her lap and listened to the house.

The house was awake too. She could hear the kitchen — Celer had been up since the third hour, making bread that nobody would eat — and the women upstairs, and the soft, endless pacing of Bathyllus in the yard. At some point in the middle of the night Thais came in with a cup of hot wine and sat down opposite her and did not say anything for a long time.

"Are you frightened?" she said at last.

"Yes."

"Good. You should be." Thais turned her cup. "A woman who is not frightened on the night before a thing like this is a woman who has not understood it. Fear is not a weakness. It is a kind of intelligence."

"I have been frightened for six months."

"Then you have been intelligent for six months." Thais looked at her. "Do you remember what I said to you in Puteoli? The first night?"

"You said not to believe anything they told me in the first month."

"And you said you did not believe anything anyone told you." Thais's mouth moved. "You were a girl in a cell with no shoes and a fever, and you looked at me as though I were a wall you were calculating how to climb. And now you are a woman with a house and a fortune and a senator and a son, and you are sitting in the dark with a cup of wine you have not touched, and you are still calculating how to climb the wall." She stood. "You will win tomorrow. I have known it since the day you bought the house. But I want you to remember something when you are standing in that chamber."

"What?"

"That you are not the girl in the cell. You are not the woman in the brothel. You are not even the woman who built this house. You are the woman who is going to walk into the Senate of Rome and tell the truth in front of the men who have been lying about her for six months." Thais put her hand on Cassia's head for a moment. "And when it is over, you are going to come home and eat something, and sleep for a week, and then you are going to be bored, and it will be the best thing that has ever happened to you."

Cassia laughed. "Tertulla said the same thing."

"Then it must be true." Thais went to the door. "Drink the wine. Sleep for an hour. And domina—"

"Yes?"

"Wear the dark veil. It makes you look like a woman who has come to bury someone."

She came to the Curia at dawn and found the woman from the prefect's courtyard sitting on the steps.

She was in the same good but not expensive wool, and she had a basket on her arm and a look on her face that Cassia recognized because she had worn it herself, in another life: the look of a person who has been waiting a long time for something and has decided to wait one more day.

"Domina," the woman said. "I heard that you won."

"I have not won anything yet."

"You were in the chamber yesterday for six hours and the whole city knows what happened." The woman stood up. "My son is out. They let him out the day after they let out your household. He came home and he ate a whole loaf of bread and then he slept for two days, and now he is back at the docks and he is complaining about the weight of the sacks." She held out the basket. "I brought you bread. It is not much. It is what I have."

Cassia looked at the basket. Then she took it.

"What is your name?" she asked.

"Marcia."

"Marcia. I will eat this bread today, in the chamber, while a man is telling the Senate that I am a whore." Cassia tucked the basket under her arm. "And it will remind me of what I am actually doing."

The woman laughed — a surprised, unguarded laugh — and went down the steps, and Cassia stood in the doorway of the Senate house with the basket on her arm and the satchel at her side and the sun coming up over the rooftops.

The second day of the trial began with Pactus on his feet.

He had spent the night preparing, and it showed. He spoke for two hours, and he did not use a single note. He spoke about the sanctity of the family and the duty of a son; he spoke about the poisoned cup and the sack and the river; he spoke about the woman on the Esquiline and the house of pleasure and the fortune made out of the appetites of senators. He spoke about the child, and he said that the story of the child was a fiction invented by a clever woman to win the sympathy of the house, and that the receipt was a forgery, and that the midwife was a paid witness, and that the arsonist's statement had been obtained by threats.

And then he said something that changed the temperature of the room.

"The house will observe," he said, "that the woman Cassia has been in the palace. She was received by the empress in June, alone, without the knowledge of the consul or the prefect. She has been seen since in the company of the empress's steward. And the emperor's secretary sits in this chamber today, not as an observer, but as a participant." He turned. "I ask the house: is this a trial, or is it a palace arrangement? Is the Senate judging a man, or is it being told what to think by a woman who has the ear of the empress?"

The chamber stirred. The emperor's secretary did not move.

"The senator Pactus will confine himself to the evidence," the consul said.

"The house will observe that this woman has made a career out of the weaknesses of senators. She has kept a house of pleasure for sixteen years. She has taken money from men who could not afford it. She has collected secrets the way a banker collects interest, and she has used them to make herself the most powerful woman in Rome who has never held an office and never been elected to anything." He looked around the chamber. "And now she has climbed into the palace, and she has persuaded the empress to receive her, and she has brought the emperor's secretary into this chamber to sit in judgment on a Roman senator. If this house acquits the senator Corvinus on the word of such a woman, then it is not the Senate of Rome. It is the antechamber of the palace, and we are all of us servants of a freedwoman's ambition."

The chamber stirred. Several senators nodded. Cassia, watching from her seat, understood exactly what he was doing: he was not trying to prove that Corvinus had killed his father. He was trying to make the Senate afraid of her.

She rose.

"The senator Pactus has said that I am the most powerful woman in Rome who has never held an office," she said. "I am flattered. But I would remind the house that the last woman who was accused of ruling Rome was Livia, and she was the wife of an emperor, and she had an army. I have a house on the Esquiline, a cook, and a bookkeeper." She paused. "If that is power, then power in this city is even cheaper than I thought."

The chamber laughed. Pactus's face tightened.

"The house may also observe," Cassia went on, "that the senator Pactus has spent the last six months telling anyone who would listen that I am the emperor's creature, and the last ten minutes telling the house that I am a whore. I would like to know which of those accusations he would like the house to believe, because they cannot both be true, and because a man who makes two contradictory accusations in the same speech is a man who is not interested in the truth."

"I am confining myself to the evidence. The evidence is that this woman has spent six months buying witnesses, bribing clerks, and climbing into the palace through the empress's private door. The evidence is that she has a document that she says was written by a clerk in the palace, and that she has not produced it, because it does not exist." Pactus turned to Cassia. "Produce it, domina. Produce your letter. Or admit to this house that you have been lying."

Cassia stood.

"The house has asked for the letter," she said. "The house shall have it."

She took it from her sleeve and gave it to the clerk.

---

The clerk read it aloud.

It was not long. It was four hundred words, in a neat clerk's hand, dated in the year of the consulship of Domitian and Silanus, and it concerned a shipment of grain that had been diverted at Ostia and sold at a profit in Campania, and it named three men: the clerk Primus, the senator Marcus Sulpicius Corvinus, and a banker on the Via Lata named Sittius. It said that the money had been divided, and that the senator's share had been paid into an account, and that the clerk would keep his part of the bargain.

And it said, at the end, in the same neat hand: *If anything happens to me, I have taken precautions.*

The chamber was silent when the clerk finished.

"The letter is a forgery," Pactus said. "It is a clumsy forgery. The hand is not the hand of the emperor's secretary."

"The hand is the hand of a clerk," Cassia said. "The secretary was a clerk twenty-four years ago. He was not the man he is now. He was a slave in the treasury, and he was bought and freed by the emperor's father, and he has been in the palace ever since." She turned. "The house may compare the letter with the secretary's own hand. There are a hundred documents in the archive with his signature on them. The clerks of this house can do it in an hour."

The consul looked at the emperor's secretary. The secretary rose.

"The letter is mine," he said.

The chamber erupted.

---

It took an hour to restore order, and by the time it was restored, the shape of the trial had changed entirely.

The secretary did not deny it. He stood in the middle of the chamber, a thin grey man with a tablet in his hand, and he said, "I was a slave in the treasury in the year of the consulship of Domitian and Silanus. I was twenty-four years old. I was offered money to divert a shipment of grain and I took it, and I have regretted it every day since. The senator Marcus Sulpicius was my partner in the fraud. The banker Sittius held the money. And I have spent twenty-four years paying the old woman at Tibur to keep silent, and twenty-four years waiting for the day when it would come out."

He turned to the Senate.

"I have served the emperor for forty years. I have counted his money and kept his accounts and I have never taken a copper since that year. I am not asking for mercy. I am telling the house the truth, because the truth is going to come out anyway, and because I would rather tell it myself than have it dragged out of a midwife and a delator and a woman who has been trying to bury it for a quarter of a century." He paused. "The trial of the senator Corvinus is a fraud built on a fraud. The accusation was paid for by Sittius, who wanted a house. It was brought by Pactus, who wanted a province. And it was concealed by me, because I wanted to keep my place. That is the whole of it. The house may do with me what it likes."

He sat down.

There was a long silence. Then Pactus rose, and his face was white, and he said, "This is a conspiracy. The secretary has been bought by the woman —"

"By whom?" the consul said.

"By the woman Cassia, by the empress, by —"

"The senator will be silent." The consul struck the floor. "The senator will sit down and he will remain silent until he is called to answer for his steward, his banker, and his accusation."

Pactus sat down.

---

Anthus was called next.

He had spent the night in a cell under the Curia, and he came into the chamber in the morning grey-faced and shaking, and he told the truth. He told it in a flat, exhausted voice, without ornament, without excuses. He told them about the payments to Felix, the payments to the forger, the payments to the arsonist. He told them about the visit to Tibur, and the men at the inn, and the old woman in the house with the fig tree who would not be frightened. He told them about the boy.

"The boy was taken from the workshop at Praeneste on the fifteenth of June," he said. "He is being held at a farm near Aricia, on the estate of the banker Sittius. He has not been harmed. He has been told that he is being kept for his own safety, and that his father will come for him when it is safe."

"Who gave the order?" the consul asked.

"The senator Pactus."

Pactus did not move. His face was the face of a man who had been carved out of stone.

"Have the boy brought to Rome," the consul said. "At once, and safely, and with a physician. And have the senator's steward taken to the prefect's house and held on a charge of arson and murder." He struck the floor. "The house will vote."

---

The vote took an hour.

The senators voted one by one, in the old way, each man rising and speaking his verdict aloud, and the clerks wrote them down. Cassia stood at the back of the chamber with her hands folded and listened to the words, and the words were, in the end, exactly what she had expected: not a verdict on the truth, but a verdict on the politics.

Corvinus was acquitted of parricide. The vote was overwhelming; no man in the chamber was willing to convict a consular on the word of a convicted delator and a steward who had confessed to arson.

But the house did not stop there. Old Paetus rose and said that while the senator was innocent of his father's death, he was not innocent of the concealment, and that a man who had allowed a woman to be sold and a child to be given away, and who had spent twenty-four years in the enjoyment of an inheritance that had been obtained by fraud, was not fit for the Asian command. He moved that the senator be censured by the house and that his name be struck from the list of candidates for the proconsulship.

The motion passed.

Pactus was convicted of calumny, of suborning witnesses, of procuring arson and murder, and of conspiracy to defraud. He was condemned to exile, his property was confiscated, and his name was struck from the rolls of the Senate. He heard the verdict standing, and when it was read he did not speak, and he did not look at anyone, and he walked out of the chamber between two lictors with his head up and his face like a mask.

Sittius was condemned in his absence. His bank was confiscated, his house on the Via Lata was sealed, and a warrant was issued for his arrest. The prefect's men found him three days later at his villa at Baiae, in his bath, with his wrists opened and a letter on the table that said, *I have been a banker for forty years and I have never lost an argument. I do not intend to begin now.*

Primus was removed from his office by the emperor's order. The order was read in the Senate by the secretary himself, in the same flat voice in which he had confessed, and it said that the emperor thanked the house for its diligence, that the clerk would be retired to a house in Campania with a pension, and that no further action would be taken against him. The Senate received it in silence. No one was surprised.

The arsonist Verecundus was condemned to the mines. His companion, a man named Rufus who had held the lamp, was condemned to the games. Anthus, who had confessed and given evidence, was condemned to exile and the confiscation of his property.

And the household of Antonia Cassia, freedwoman, was declared free of all suspicion, and the costs of the questioning were remitted by order of the house.

Cassia stood at the back of the chamber while all of it was read, and when it was over she did not cheer and she did not weep. She turned and walked out into the sunshine, and the crowd in the Forum was waiting for her, and this time it made the sound she had heard on the steps the day before, and this time she let it wash over her.

---

The emperor's secretary was waiting for her in the portico.

He was standing by a column with his tablet in his hand and his clerk three paces behind him, and he had the look of a man who has been waiting for some time and does not intend to be kept waiting much longer.

"Domina," he said. "A word."

"Secretary."

"I have been in the service of three emperors," he said, "and I have read a great many records of a great many trials, and I have never in forty years seen a house vote a man's name out of a document." He looked at her. "The names are struck. The sheet is sealed. It is in the archive, and it will stay there, and in fifty years a clerk will open it and find two lines cut out of a receipt and a note in the margin in my hand, and he will wonder what was so dangerous about a potter."

"Will the seal hold?"

"The seal will hold as long as the men who ordered it are alive, and after that it will hold because nobody will care." He put the tablet under his arm. "I am going to Campania in the autumn. I am told there is a house and a pension and a garden, and that I am to be grateful. I am grateful. I have spent my life counting other men's money, and now I am to spend the rest of it counting figs." He paused. "I have one thing to say to you before I go, and then I will not trouble you again."

"Say it."

"You won," he said. "You won because you were the only person in that chamber who did not want anything. The prosecutor wanted a conviction. Pactus wanted a province. The banker wanted a house. The senator wanted to be forgiven. Even the old woman in the bed wanted to be told that she had done the right thing." He looked at her. "You wanted the boy, and you wanted the truth, and you were willing to lose both. That is why they could not touch you. It is also why you will never be safe in this city again, because the one thing Rome cannot forgive is a person who does not want what it is selling."

Cassia looked at him for a long moment.

"That is the most honest thing anyone has said to me in sixteen years," she said.

"I am a good accountant," the secretary said. "Honesty is a kind of bookkeeping." He inclined his head. "Goodbye, domina. I hope you find him."

He walked away down the portico with his clerk behind him, and Cassia stood by the column and watched him go.

---

Aemilia was waiting in the portico with her daughter.

She had heard the verdict from a clerk before it was read, because the wives of senators always hear things before they are read, and she stood with her hands folded and her face composed, and when Corvinus came out she looked at him for a long moment and then said, "You are acquitted."

"I am."

"And censured."

"And censured." He stopped in front of her. "And finished. The Asian command is gone. The augurate will go too, in a month, when the house votes on it. I will be a consular who cannot hold an office and a senator who cannot speak without being reminded of his father's fraud and his mother's poison and the boy he did not raise." He looked at his wife. "I am sorry. I am sorry for all of it. For the years I was not there. For the lies. For my mother. For the house. For all of it."

Aemilia looked at him. Then she said, "Do you know what I have been thinking, all through this trial? I have been thinking that I have spent nineteen years in a house where no one told me the truth, and that I would rather have a husband who is finished and honest than a husband who is successful and a lie." She took his hand. "You are not finished, Lucius. You are forty-five years old. There is a great deal of life left, and I intend to spend it with a man who has finally stopped pretending to be his father."

Young Sulpicia came forward. She was fifteen, and married, and she had been in the gallery for two days, and she looked at her father with her grandmother's eyes.

"The boy," she said. "Is he alive?"

"He is alive. He is on a farm near Aricia and Bathyllus is already on the road."

"Then bring him home," Sulpicia said. "And let me meet him. He is my brother. I have a right to meet him."

Corvinus looked at his daughter for a long moment. Then he said, "You have a right to a great many things, and I have given you almost none of them." He put his hand on her head. "I will bring him home. And then we will see what he wants."

An hour later Corvinus came and found her standing by the fountain at the edge of the Forum with Tertulla and Bathyllus. He had sent his wife and daughter home in the litter and had walked the length of the Forum alone, and he looked ten years older than he had that morning.

His toga was crumpled and his face was grey and there was something in his eyes that she had not seen there before: not relief, not triumph. Emptiness.

"Well," he said.

"Well."

"I am acquitted."

"You are."

"And censured, and struck from the list, and finished." He laughed, without any humour. "I have spent my whole life wanting the Asian command. I have mortgaged the house and borrowed from a man who wanted to destroy me and married a woman I did not love and lied to my mother and lied to my wife and lied to myself, and I have got what I wanted, which is to be alive and free and to have nothing left to want."

Cassia looked at him. The crowd was going past them, and the sun was on the fountain, and somewhere a man was selling the little clay figures again, and a woman was shouting that the senator was innocent and the gods were just.

"You have a son," she said. "Two sons. And a daughter who is married to a fool and a wife who has been braver than either of us. And a house that is falling down and a name that has been dragged through the Forum and a woman who has been in love with you since she was eighteen years old." She paused. "You have a great deal left to want."

He looked at her. Then he put his hand over his face and stood in the middle of the Forum and wept, and the crowd went past and did not notice, because in Rome a man weeping in the Forum is not a remarkable thing.

Cassia stood beside him and did not touch him, and waited.

After a while he wiped his face and said, "The boy. Anthus said Aricia."

"Bathyllus is already on the road. He will be there by nightfall."

"Then I am going with him."

"Lucius—"

"I am going with him," Corvinus said. "I have spent twenty-four years not going. I am not going to spend another night in this city while my son is on a farm in Aricia being held by a banker's men." He looked at her. "You said I had to choose. I am choosing."

Cassia looked at him for a long moment. Then she said, "Take the fast horses. Take Bathyllus and two of Tertulla's men. And Lucius—"

"Yes?"

"Bring him home," she said. "Not to me. To his father. Bring him home to the Via Ostiensis, and tell him what he needs to know, and let him decide what happens next." She paused. "And when it is done, come back and tell me."

He nodded. Then he turned and went, and the crowd closed around him, and Cassia stood by the fountain with Tertulla and watched him go.

"He will bring the boy home," Tertulla said.

"Yes."

"And then?"

"And then we will find out what the truth is worth," Cassia said. "We have spent six months fighting for it. Now we have to live with it."

---

They buried Nolus on the third day after the trial, in the ground outside the Porta Esquilina where the poor and the slaves were buried, and this time it was not a small stone and a hurried prayer.

Cassia paid for a proper funeral. There was a procession from the house to the gate, with the household in front and the flute players and the mourning women, and there was a stone with his name on it and his age and the words *A GOOD BOY, WHO FED THE PIGEONS*, which Cassia had chosen herself, and there was a feast afterward in the courtyard of the house for everyone who had come.

The whole household was there. Thais and Hermeros and Celer and Bathyllus and Sabina and Chrysis and Senno and the kitchen boys and the women from upstairs and the old gardener's daughter, who had come from Ostia with her two children and who cried through the whole of it and then ate a great deal of bread and cheese.

Tertulla came. Aemilia came, with young Sulpicia, who stood at the back and looked at the stone and did not say anything. Corvinus was still on the road to Aricia, and Cassia stood at the grave and thought about the fact that he had never met the boy in life and was now going to meet him in a farmhouse with a physician and a banker's men.

When the priest had finished and the earth had been put in, Cassia said a few words. She had not prepared them.

"Nolus was eleven years old," she said. "He came to this house two years ago from the market, and he was given the pigeons and the yard, and he did it well. He was not a clever boy and he was not a beautiful boy and he did not have a great future ahead of him. He had a broom and a bucket and eleven pigeons, and he fed them every morning before he ate his own breakfast, because he said they were always hungry and he was not." She paused. "He died because someone wanted to hurt me. That is the truth of it, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. He was killed to send me a message. And the men who killed him have been sent to the mines and to the games, and that is justice, of a kind, and it does not bring him back, and it never will."

She looked at the stone.

"But he is not a message. He is a boy who fed the pigeons. And every year, on the day of his death, this house will make an offering for him and say his name, and the pigeons will be fed, and anyone who wants to remember him will be welcome at my table." She paused. "That is all. That is the whole of it. A boy lived and a boy died and we are going to remember him, because that is the only thing any of us can do for the dead."

They went back to the house and ate and drank and told stories about Nolus, and Celer got drunk and wept and was put to bed by Thais, and Sabina fell asleep on the bench by the fountain with her head on Chrysis's shoulder, and the sun went down over the garden, and the house was full of people who had come through something together and were still, all of them, alive.

Cassia sat on the bench by the roses and watched them.

"Domina," Thais said, sitting down beside her. "The boy. What will you do if he does not want to come?"

"I do not know."

"You must have thought about it."

"I have thought about nothing else for six months." Cassia looked at the dark garden. "If he does not want to come, I will let him go. I will leave him the money from the chest and a letter, and I will not go to the shop, and I will not send anyone. And once a year I will have a message from his father to say that he is alive, and once a year I will send him something that he can throw away if he wants to." She paused. "And I will be the woman with the house on the Esquiline, and he will be the potter on the Via Ostiensis, and the city will go on, and that will be the end of it."

"And if he does want to come?"

"Then I will have a son," Cassia said, "and a house with a room at the top of the stairs that is no longer empty, and a great deal of explaining to do." She looked at Thais. "And I will probably make a mess of it."

"Probably," Thais said. "That is what mothers do."

Cassia laughed, and then, unexpectedly, she cried — briefly and furiously, with her hands over her face, the way she had not allowed herself to cry since the morning in the prefect's courtyard. Thais sat beside her and did not say anything and did not touch her, and after a while Cassia stopped and wiped her face and said, "Well. That was undignified."

"It was very dignified," Thais said. "You did it in the dark, in your own garden, in front of one person. That is the most dignified way there is."
