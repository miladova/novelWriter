The Baths of Agrippa opened to women at the fourth hour, and by the time Cassia arrived the dressing room was already full of the particular noise of Roman women pretending not to look at one another. There were senators' wives and bankers' widows and the daughters of men who had made their money inannounced and were now buying their way into the equestrian order one marriage at a time. There were two actresses, who were not respectable and did not care. There was a woman who had been a vestal until a scandal had retired her, and who now came to the baths every day and sat in the hottest room until she was red, as though she were trying to sweat out the memory. And there was Tertulla, already undressed and wrapped in a linen sheet, arguing with the attendant about the temperature of the oil.

"It is rancid," she said.

"It is the best oil in Rome, domina."

"Then Rome has gone off." Tertulla saw Cassia and waved. "There you are. Come and smell this. It is either oil or a mistake."

Cassia smelled it. It was oil, and it was not rancid, though it had been in the jar a long time. "It is fine. Stop tormenting the girl. She did not press the olives."

The attendant looked grateful. Tertulla looked betrayed. They went through into the tepidarium, where the air was warm and wet and smelled of steam and perfume and bodies, and the mosaic floor showed a Triton with a following of sea creatures, and somebody had dropped a bronze strigil and left it where it lay.

The bathing was a ritual, and like all rituals it was mostly about being seen. The actual washing took a quarter of an hour. The rest was politics. Cassia sat on the marble bench with her feet in the warm water and let the steam open her lungs, and the room arranged itself around her the way rooms always did.

"Did you hear about the grain fleet?" said a woman on her left, who had a face like a spoon and a husband in the annona.

"I heard two ships were lost."

"Three. The third went down off Corsica and the cargo was insured in Alexandria, and the insurer is refusing to pay, and the prefect is furious." The spoon-faced woman leaned in. "They say Comptus is finished."

"They have said that before."

"They have never said it with a warrant."

Cassia turned her head. "What warrant?"

"There is a man from the treasury asking questions. A man named Felix."

The steam curled between them. Cassia's face did not change.

"Felix asks questions for a living," she said. "He has been asking them for years and he has never once found an answer."

"He found one this time. He has been to the grain office twice, and to the prefect's house, and to the house of a man in the fourth cohort who signed the manifest." The woman lowered her voice. "They say he is not working alone."

"Who is he working for?"

"Nobody knows. That is the interesting part." The woman smiled, pleased to have something to trade. "You should be careful, domina. They say he has been asking about your house as well."

"Then he will find that it is very clean," Cassia said, "and that I charge by the hour."

The woman laughed, uncertain whether she had been insulted, and drifted away to tell someone else.

Two women came through from the dressing room, and one of them was the wife of a praetor and the other was the wife of a man who hoped to be a praetor, and they saw Cassia and made the small adjustment of the head that is not quite a greeting and not quite a cut. Cassia watched them go by with her hands folded in her lap.

"You could buy them both," Tertulla said, "and they know it."

"Buying is easy. It is the being bought that people resent." Cassia smiled at the praetor's wife, a wide, warm, entirely genuine-looking smile, and the woman faltered and then, because she was well brought up, smiled back before she could stop herself. "There. Now she will go home and be angry with herself all afternoon."

"You are a terrible woman."

"I am a student of human nature. It is the same thing with better manners."

A young woman came and sat down on the bench opposite, with a nurse behind her and a look of determined friendliness. She was perhaps twenty, and pregnant, and clearly at the baths on the advice of a physician who had told her that warm water was good for the child and had not told her what to do about the women who would look at her.

"You are Antonia Cassia," she said.

"I am."

"I am Lollia. My husband is in the fourth cohort of the vigiles. He says you are the most dangerous woman in Rome." She said it with pleasure, as though she had been given a present. "I have wanted to meet you for a year. My mother says I am not to speak to you and my husband says I am not to speak to you and the priest says I am not to speak to you, and I have decided that a woman with three men telling her what to do must have something worth hearing."

Cassia laughed. It was the first unguarded laugh she had given all morning, and it surprised her. "What do you want to hear?"

"How did you do it?"

"Do what?"

"All of it. The house. The money. The men who come and go and are afraid of you. My husband is a good man and he is afraid of his commander, and his commander is afraid of the prefect, and the prefect is afraid of the emperor, and the emperor is afraid of the army, and nobody in the whole city is afraid of me." Lollia's chin came up. "I would like to be afraid of something other than my mother-in-law."

The steam curled. Tertulla was watching them both with amusement and something like tenderness.

"You will not like the answer," Cassia said.

"I have not liked many answers."

"Then here it is. I did it by being useful to people who could hurt me, and by making sure that I knew one thing about each of them that they would not want known, and by never once asking for a favour without offering something in return. I did it by working when other people were sleeping, and by remembering every name and every debt, and by never letting a man see me cry. And I did it by being willing to be disliked." Cassia leaned forward. "You are pregnant, and you are young, and you are pretty, and you have a husband who is a good man. Do not do what I did. It is a poor bargain. Be liked. Be liked and be useful and be patient, and in twenty years you will have a house full of children and a husband who does not flinch when you come into the room, and that is worth more than all the gold in my strongroom."

Lollia stared at her. Then she said, "But you have the gold."

"Yes," Cassia said. "I have the gold."

She stood, and Tertulla stood with her, and they went through into the hot room, and behind them Lollia sat with her hands on her belly and her nurse fanning her and a look on her face like a woman doing arithmetic.

Tertulla came and sat beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. "Felix," she said.

"Felix."

"You are not surprised."

"I am never surprised. It is bad for the digestion." Cassia closed her eyes. "He came to the house after the Saturnalia. He wants two hundred thousand and he offered me a document, and I told him to come back after the Ides, and I have been thinking about him ever since."

"Two hundred thousand for what?"

"For a memory. His words."

"Whose memory?"

"Mine," Cassia said. "Or near enough."

Tertulla was quiet. Then she said, "You could pay him."

"I could. And then he would come back next year, and the year after, and the price would go up, and by the time I was fifty I would have bought my whole life back from him three times over. That is not a bargain. That is a subscription." Cassia opened her eyes. "There is only one way to deal with a man like Felix."

"Which is?"

"To make him more afraid of you than he is greedy for your money." She stood. "Come. I want the hot room before the consul's wife takes it, because she will want it for an hour and she will talk about her son the whole time."

The caldarium was a furnace. The floor was too hot to stand on without wooden sandals, and the air was so thick with steam that the bathers were only shapes, pale and moving, like fish under ice. Cassia climbed to the highest bench, where the heat was worst, and lay back with a folded cloth over her eyes, and let the sweat run.

Tertulla climbed up beside her. Their hands found each other in the dark of the cloth.

"You are frightened," Tertulla said, quietly.

"I am warm."

"You are frightened. You talk about subscriptions and bargains when you are frightened. When you are calm you talk about the price of fish."

Cassia said nothing. The steam hissed. Somewhere below, a woman laughed, and someone else shushed her, and the attendant came through with the bucket, sloshing water on the coals.

"I have spent twenty-four years," Cassia said at last, "building a wall. Every brick is a favour, a debt, a secret, a friend. I have been very careful. I have never once asked for more than a man could give, and I have never once let a man leave this house without knowing that I knew something about him that he would not want his wife to know." She lifted the cloth from her eyes. "And now a man I have never liked is standing outside the wall with a chisel, and the wall is made of people. My people. They will not put me to the question. They will put them to the question. And they will break, because everyone breaks, and then they will say things about me that are true and things that are not, and the Senate will not care which is which."

"You could go away."

"Where?"

"Anywhere. You have money."

"I have money in Rome. In Alexandria I have a letter of credit and a cousin of Hermeros who may or may not be honest. In Antioch I have nothing. In Athens I have a debt I have never collected." Cassia sat up. "And if I go, I am a woman who ran. A woman who ran is a woman who was guilty. The whole city will say it, and they will be right, and my house will be sold for debts within the year, and the people in it will be sold with the furniture."

"What do you want?"

Cassia looked at her. In the steam, with her hair flat to her head and her face flushed and her eyes steady, Tertulla looked like the only solid thing in a room made of vapour.

"I want," Cassia said, "to be the one who decides."

She leaned over and kissed her. It was not a public kiss, though it was not exactly private either; the steam hid them, and the women around them were occupied with their own sweating and their own gossip, and in Rome a kiss between two women in a bath was not a scandal, provided that neither of them was married to a man who cared. Tertulla's mouth was warm and tasted of the mint they gave out at the door. Her hand came up and rested on Cassia's neck, and her thumb moved once, slowly, against the pulse there.

"Not here," Tertulla murmured.

"No," Cassia said. "Not here."

They went down to the small pool in the corner, where the water was cold, and Cassia stepped in and gasped, and the shock of it went through her like a knife and cleared her head. Tertulla followed, and they stood in the cold water with their arms around each other, and the noise of the baths was a long way off, and for a moment Cassia allowed herself to be held.

"Whatever happens," Tertulla said into her ear, "you are not that girl any more. You are not the girl in the cell. You are the woman who owns the house. Remember that when you are frightened. You are not the one who gets sold. You are the one who does the selling."

Cassia laughed, and it came out broken. "That is the most Roman thing anyone has ever said to me."

"I have been practising."

They came out of the pool and were drying themselves when the attendant came to say that a lady was asking for the domina. Cassia, with her hair still dripping and her skin still cold, wrapped herself in the sheet and went out into the colonnade, where the winter sun was coming in low and gold and the water of the great pool was green and still.

Aemilia Celsa was sitting on the stone bench by the pool, dressed, composed, with a fan in her hand that she did not need. She had come alone. That in itself was a statement, because a senator's wife did not go to the public baths alone unless she wanted to be seen doing it.

"Domina," Cassia said.

"Domina." Aemilia looked up at her, and her eyes were the eyes of a woman who had been awake all night. "I have come to ask you a question, and I would like you to answer it honestly, because I have had enough of being lied to by people who love me."

"Ask."

"Who is Psyche?"

The name went through Cassia like the cold water. She stood very still, with her hair dripping onto the mosaic, and the whole great colonnade seemed to hold its breath.

"Where did you hear that name?"

"My mother-in-law said it in her sleep. Three nights ago. She said it twice, and then she said, 'Tell her nothing,' and then she woke up and asked for her medicine and would not look at me." Aemilia's fan did not move. "I have been in that house for nineteen years. I know every name in it — the slaves, the freedmen, the tenants, the creditors, the cousins in Gaul. I have never heard the name Psyche. And when I asked the old steward, he went the colour of a fish and said it was a woman who used to do the mending."

Cassia sat down on the bench beside her. It was not a friendly gesture, and it was not meant to be; it was the gesture of a woman who has decided that the conversation will be long.

"Psyche was a midwife," she said. "She was at Tibur, in the summer of the year eighty-eight. She was at a birth."

"Whose?"

"Mine."

Aemilia's fan stopped. For a long moment she did not move at all. Then she said, "There was a child."

"There was a child."

"What happened to it?"

"I was told it was born dead. I was told it did not breathe. I was told it was buried in the garden at Tibur, and I was not allowed to see it, and three days later I was sold to a man in Puteoli and put on a cart, and I did not come back to Rome for four years." Cassia's voice was even. "The midwife is alive. She is old and she is ill and she has sent me a ring and a message, and she says she has something to give me. I do not know what it is. I do not know whether she is telling the truth or whether she wants money or whether she has gone mad in her old age. But I am going to Tibur before the spring, and I am going to ask her."

Aemilia turned to look at her. Her face was white. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you asked. Because you came here alone to ask. Because you have been lied to by people who love you, and I know what that does to a person, and I would not wish it on a dog." Cassia drew the sheet closer around her shoulders. "And because whatever is coming, it is coming for your house as much as mine, and I would rather have one person inside that house who knows the truth than a whole family who does not."

Aemilia was silent for a long time. The sun moved on the water. In the distance, in the women's hall, someone dropped a bronze basin and the sound rang off the marble.

"He is my husband," Aemilia said at last.

"Yes."

"And you hate him."

"I do."

"Why?"

Cassia looked at her. There were a hundred answers, and most of them were true, and none of them were ones she would give to this woman on a stone bench in a public bath.

"Because he was there," she said, "and he did nothing. And because he has spent twenty-four years being a good man in public in order to make up for the one thing he did in private, and it has worked. He has been consul. He is an augur. Men listen to him. And I have spent twenty-four years being a woman no one will listen to, and I have had to buy my hearing." She stood. "That is the answer. You may do with it what you like."

Aemilia rose too. She folded her fan and put it in her sleeve, and she looked at Cassia for a moment with an expression that was not friendship and was not enmity.

"I will not tell him I came here," she said.

"I know you will not."

"How do you know?"

"Because you did not bring a slave, and you did not use the north entrance, and you have been sitting with your back to the door the whole time we have been speaking." Cassia almost smiled. "You are a very good liar, domina. You have spent nineteen years learning it. It is the only thing that house teaches anyone."

Aemilia's mouth twisted. "My daughter thinks I am at the temple of Juno."

"Then you had better go to the temple of Juno, or the gods will be angry and your daughter will be suspicious, and I do not know which is worse."

Aemilia turned to go. At the edge of the colonnade she stopped and said, without turning round, "The girl. Publius's girl. Her name is Calpurnia. She is in a house in the Subura that belongs to a man named Scaurus, and she is not a whore, and her mother is dead, and she has been promised to a sailor in Ostia. He has paid a deposit. If my son does not pay the rest, she will be on a boat by the Kalends of March."

"Why are you telling me?"

"Because my son will ruin himself over her, and I have no money, and you have a great deal." Now she did turn, and her face was hard. "I do not like you. I do not approve of you. I think your house is a blot on this city and I would not let my daughter cross your threshold. But I am a practical woman, and my son is going to make a mistake, and there is no one else in Rome with money and no children who might understand."

Cassia looked at her for a long moment.

"Come to the house," she said. "Not the baths. The house. On the day after the Ides, in the morning, before the men are awake. We will talk about your son, and about the midwife, and about what your mother-in-law knows. And bring the girl, if you can do it without a scandal. It is time she saw a house that is not a tomb."

The house was quiet that evening. Cassia bathed again at home, in her own bath, with Sabina pouring the water and Philumena doing her hair afterward, and she ate a light supper in the small room with Tertulla and Ione and Thais, and she did not talk about the baths.

Thais was fifty-four and had been with her since Puteoli. She had been the woman who ran the house in those years, when the house was three rooms above a bakery and the guests were sailors and the money was bad. She was small and brown and neat, and she could read and write and do sums and had never in her life been surprised by anything a man did. She came to Cassia's room after supper with the keys at her belt and said, "You have not asked me about the boy."

"The boy?"

"The flower seller came. She had word from Tibur." Thais sat down without being invited, because she had earned that. "Psyche is alive. She is very old and her eyes are bad and she lives with her granddaughter's family in the village under the hill. She has a box that she keeps under her bed. The granddaughter does not know what is in it. The granddaughter is frightened of it."

"Frightened why?"

"Because the old woman talks to it." Thais's face did not change. "She calls it by a name."

Cassia sat down at her desk. The lamp was lit, and the shutters were closed, and beyond them the city went on with its noise, indifferent as always.

"What name?"

"'The little master,'" Thais said.

Cassia put her hands flat on the desk and looked at them. They were a woman's hands, jewelled, soft, and they had once been a girl's hands, red from the wash water, and there was a scar across the left thumb from a knife in a kitchen in Puteoli. She looked at the scar for a while.

"Thais," she said. "What do you remember of the day I came to Puteoli?"

"Everything."

"Tell me."

Thais was quiet. Then she said, "You came in a cart. You had a cloak that did not belong to you and no shoes. You had been bleeding for three days and you would not let anyone look at you. You sat in the kitchen and would not eat, and when the man who owned the house came in and told you what you were to do, you looked at him as though you were measuring him for a coffin. I thought: that one will either kill him or own him. I was right both times."

"He died of a fever."

"He died of a fever three years later and you had bought the house by then and you were not sorry." Thais's mouth moved. "I remember that you would not say the word 'child.' For two years you would not say it. If a customer's child came into the kitchen you would leave the room. If a woman on the street was carrying a baby you would cross the road. And then one day you said it — we were counting the wine and you said, 'The child of the wine merchant is sick,' and you went on counting — and after that you were all right, or you were better, or you were a different kind of not all right."

"What kind?"

"The kind that builds a house," Thais said, "and fills it with people, and never has a child of her own."

The lamp guttered. Cassia reached out and turned the wick up, and the light steadied.

"Go to bed," she said. "Tomorrow I want the strongbox brought up, and the black book, and I want you to write down everything you know about Felix. Every debt, every woman, every bribe. Every man he has ever ruined. I want it all."

"For what?"

"Because a man who has ruined other men has enemies, and enemies are cheaper than friends." Cassia opened a drawer and took out a small box of cedarwood, and set it on the desk, and did not open it. "And because I am going to make him choose between the money and his life, and I need to know which one he loves more."

Thais rose. At the door she said, "And the boy? In Puteoli. The fuller's boy. Chrysis's brother."

"What about him?"

"Two thousand sesterces is not a great deal of money."

"No," Cassia said. "It is not."

"You could buy him and give him to her, and she would be yours forever."

"I do not want her to be mine forever. I want her to be free and to know it." Cassia looked up. "There is a difference, Thais. I have spent my whole life learning it."

Thais went out. Cassia sat alone for a long time with the cedar box in front of her. Then she opened it.

There was nothing in it but a letter. The papyrus was brown at the edges and had cracked along two of the folds, and the ink was faded to the colour of rust, and the handwriting was the careful, upright handwriting of a young man of twenty who had been taught to write by a Greek tutor with a stick. She did not read it. She had read it so many times that she could have recited it in the dark, and had done, on bad nights, in Puteoli, when the clients were gone and the house was quiet and the only sound was the sea.

She held it for a while. Then she put it back in the box and closed the lid, and put the box back in the drawer, and locked the drawer, and put the key in the fold of her belt where it had lived for twenty-four years.

Sabina came in with the evening lesson, which was letters. She had learned her alphabet from Thais and her numbers from Hermeros and her manners from everyone, and she could now read a line of Virgil slowly and with great seriousness, as though each word were a coin she had been asked to test with her teeth. Cassia sat her at the little table with the wax tablet and the stylus and heard her read.

"The arms and the man I sing," Sabina read. "Who first from the shores of Troy..." She stopped. "Domina, why does it begin with arms? Is it a song about weapons?"

"It is a song about a man who carries them."

"But it says the arms and the man. As though the arms were the important part."

Cassia looked at her. Sabina was twelve, and thin, and her hair was tied back with a strip of leather, and she had the flat, direct gaze of a child who has not yet learned to be embarrassed by her own intelligence.

"Perhaps the poet thought they were," Cassia said. "The arms are what the man is for. The man is what the arms are used by. A line like that is a way of saying that you cannot separate the two."

"That is a sad thing to begin a song with."

"It is a Roman thing to begin a song with."

Sabina considered this, and then wrote the line again on her tablet, in careful letters, and Cassia watched her do it and felt the old, strange, painful thing that she had felt for twenty-four years whenever a child did something well.

"Domina," Sabina said, without looking up, "did you have a mother?"

The room was very quiet. The lamp hissed.

"I did," Cassia said. "She died when I was small."

"What was she like?"

"She smelled of lavender. She used to sing when she was working. She had a bad temper and a good laugh, and she could not sew, and she used to tell me that the gods were not in the temples, they were in the kitchen, because that is where the food was." Cassia's voice was steady. "I do not remember her face. I remember the smell of lavender and the singing and the bad temper. That is all."

"That is a lot," Sabina said.

"Yes," Cassia said. "It is."

"Will you teach me to write a letter?"

"Who would you write to?"

"Nobody," Sabina said. "I would just like to be able to."

Cassia put her hand on the girl's head for a moment, and then took it away, because tenderness in this house was a thing that had to be measured out in small doses, like medicine.

Ione came in as Sabina was leaving. She had been out, and she was still in her cloak, and her cheeks were pink with the cold, and she stood in the doorway and watched the child go with an expression that Cassia could not read.

"She is getting clever," Ione said.

"She is getting educated. It is not the same. Cleverness is what she was born with."

"You are fond of her."

"Yes."

"More than you are of the rest of us."

Cassia looked at her. "I am fond of all of you. It is not a competition."

"Everything is a competition." Ione came in and sat down, uninvited, in the chair by the desk. "I have been thinking about what you said at the Saturnalia. That you were not a customary woman. That you had built something worth keeping."

"I did not say that."

"You said something like it. It does not matter. What matters is that I have been in this house for nine years, and I have no house of my own, and no money of my own, and no name. I have a room, and a share of the gifts, and a future that depends on your health and your temper and the goodwill of men who think of me as furniture."

"You are not furniture."

"I know. That is why I am talking to you." Ione leaned forward. "I want to buy my freedom."

Cassia was silent.

"I have saved," Ione said. "Not enough. I have a man who will lend me the rest. He is a wine merchant in the Vicus Longus, and he is fat and he is fifty and he has a wife, and he wants me to keep a house for him, and I do not want to keep a house for him, but I want to be free more than I want anything else." Her voice did not shake. "I am telling you because I would rather buy my freedom from you than run away from you, and because I think you would rather have the money than the scandal."

"How much do you have?"

"Forty thousand."

"And the man will lend you forty more."

"Yes."

"And what will he want for it?"

Ione's mouth twisted. "What do men always want?"

Cassia looked at her for a long moment. Then she said, "I will not sell you to him."

"You will not—"

"I will free you," Cassia said. "For nothing."

Ione stared at her. "Why?"

"Because you asked me a fair question and because you have been honest with me for nine years, which is longer than most wives manage, and because if I keep you here against your will, you will poison the house, and I like the house." Cassia picked up her pen. "There will be conditions. You will stay until the spring, and you will train the girl who takes your place, and you will not take any of my clients with you, and you will not tell anyone what you know about me. And you will not take the wine merchant's money, because a woman who takes a loan from a man like that is not free, whatever the paper says."

Ione's eyes were bright. "I do not know what to say."

"Say nothing. It is not a favour. It is a transaction, and I am getting the better of it." Cassia looked down at her accounts. "Go and tell Thais. She will draw up the papers, and she will be unpleasant about it, and then she will cry in the kitchen, and you will pretend not to notice."

Ione rose. At the door she said, "You are not what they say you are."

"I am exactly what they say I am," Cassia said. "I am also other things. Most people are."

There was a knock at the door of the house, and Bathyllus's voice, and then Hermeros's, and then a third voice, young and high and frightened. Cassia went out into the atrium. A boy stood there with his cloak wet from the street and his hair plastered to his forehead, and in his hand he had a wax tablet with the seal broken.

"From whom?" Cassia said.

"From the senator Corvinus," the boy said. "He said to give it to you and no one else and to wait for an answer."

She took the tablet and turned it to the lamp.

*They have found the physician's assistant. He is in the house of Pactus and he is talking. F.*

No greeting. No closing. The same four lines, the same terrible economy, twenty-four years later.

Cassia looked at the boy, who was shivering, and at Bathyllus, who was watching her, and at Hermeros, who was pretending to be interested in the accounts, and beyond them at the dark garden where the roses were black sticks and the fountain played its patient music in the cold.

"Tell him," she said, "that I will come to him. Tomorrow, at the ninth hour, at the house of my friend Tertulla, which is on the Caelian and has two doors. Tell him to come alone and to come by the garden door. Tell him that if he brings a lictor, or a friend, or a slave who can read, I will have him thrown out of the house and he can explain to the Senate why he was there."

The boy nodded and ran.

"Domina," Hermeros said, when he had gone, "is that wise?"

"No," Cassia said. "It is necessary. Wisdom is what you do when you have a choice."
