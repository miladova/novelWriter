The Kalends of January opened grey and cold, with a wind off the Tiber that smelled of silt and smoke, and Rome put on its best face anyway. The city had been preparing for a month. The shopkeepers had washed their awnings and hung them with laurel; the wine sellers had chalked the prices of the good Caecuban on their boards; the priests of half a hundred temples had been up since dark, killing birds and reading their livers and arguing about the omens in the way that priests argue when the omens are good but not perfect. The emperor was to dedicate his forum. The emperor was to take the consulship for the sixth time. The emperor was to give games, and a distribution, and a speech that everyone would pretend to have heard and no one would remember.

Antonia Cassia had been invited to none of it. She went anyway.

They left the house before the third hour, while the streets were still full of carts and the vigiles were still arguing with the carters about the load limits on the Via Lata. Bathyllus walked in front with a staff and the expression of a man who had been told to keep the peace and was looking forward to failing. Behind him came Cassia in a plain litter, with Tertulla beside her and Ione on the seat opposite, dressed in a blue that was too expensive for the morning and exactly right for the afternoon. Hermeros brought up the rear with a purse and a face of mild disapproval. The purse was for bribes. The face was for the account books.

They went down the Clivus Suburanus into the old city, where the streets narrowed and the insulae leaned out over the road as though trying to hear the news, and the crowd thickened with every step. Porters with baskets. A man selling hot sausages from a brazier. Two women with a child between them, arguing about whether the child had been cured or had only stopped crying. A group of young men in matching cloaks, drunk already, singing a song about the emperor's bridge over the Danube that had never been finished and had cost a great deal. A beggar with no legs, who knew Cassia's litter by its curtains and called out to her by name, and to whom she gave a silver piece without stopping.

"You are too generous," Tertulla said.

"I am not generous. I am superstitious. He has been at that corner for eleven years, and he has outlived three of my enemies."

Tertulla laughed. Vibidia Tertulla was thirty-nine and had buried two husbands and had the kind of money that made her unmarriageable and the kind of wit that made her unwelcome at half the dinner tables in Rome. She had been Cassia's friend for six years, and her lover for two of them, off and on, in the way of two women who had both decided that the world had taken enough from them and they would keep this one thing for themselves. She was not beautiful, exactly, but she had a face that improved when she was interested, and she was interested in almost everything.

The crowd at the forum was already enormous. The new complex stood where the old slums had been, and the men who had lived in those slums had been moved somewhere else, and the men who had built the new complex had been paid, and the men who were now looking at it had been told that it was the greatest thing ever built by human hands, which, on that morning, in that light, with the incense and the garlands and the noise, it almost was.

They had a place in the portico of the Basilica Ulpia, arranged for them by Comptus, who had arranged it for a great many people and had been paid in favours by all of them. The basilica was vast. Its columns were grey granite from the quarries of Mons Claudianus, each one dragged across Egypt and floated across the sea and hauled up the Tiber and set upright by men who would never see the finished building. The roof was gilded bronze, and the floor was marble in a pattern that made the eye want to follow it, and the apse at the end held a statue of the emperor that was larger than the emperor and, Cassia thought, better looking.

Beyond the basilica stood the two libraries, one Greek and one Latin, facing each other across a small courtyard with a fountain. Beyond them, the column was still in its scaffolding, a great wrapped thing with workmen crawling on it, the spiral of carved soldiers and ships and bridges emerging from the top down as the masons worked. And in the middle of the new square, on a bronze horse, the emperor sat with his hand raised, and the horse had its mouth open, and the pigeons had already found it.

"Look at it," Tertulla said. "They have taken the heart out of the city and put a monument in its place."

"They have taken the slums out of the city," Cassia said. "It is not the same."

"It is the same to the people who lived in them."

"People who live in slums are used to being moved. It is the ones who live in houses who are surprised." Cassia watched the workmen on the column. "My father—" She stopped.

Tertulla looked at her. "Your father?"

"I have no father. I was thinking of someone else's." She turned away. "You are not afraid," Ione said, from the other side of the litter, "of being seen."

"I am afraid of being seen in the wrong company. It is not the same."

"You are a freedwoman in a city where the emperor's grandmother sold fish. There is no wrong company, only the wrong witnesses."

Cassia looked at her. "That is either the wisest thing you have said or the most dangerous."

"Can it not be both?" Ione turned her face to the light. "I want to see the libraries. I have heard there is a copy of Sappho there that cost more than a house."

"There is a copy of Sappho there that is kept in a box and shown to no one. It is the most Roman thing about the building."

"I should like to see it anyway."

"Then you will have to marry a senator, or become one. I do not recommend either." Cassia drew her mantle closer. "Do you know why I came today, Ione?"

"Because you were not invited."

"Because I was not invited." Cassia watched the standards go by. "A woman in my position is always being told what she is not. She is not respectable. She is not senatorial. She is not the sort of woman one invites to the dedication of a forum. If she stays at home, she agrees with them. If she goes, she makes them say it to her face, and a man who says a thing to your face is a man you can answer."

"And if he does not say it to your face?"

"Then he says it behind your back, and you find out from someone like Felix, and you charge him for the information." Cassia smiled, without warmth. "I have been in this city for twenty years. I have learned that the only thing more expensive than being seen is being invisible."

Ione was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "And the senator Corvinus? Is he here because you were not invited?"

Cassia did not look at her. "The senator Corvinus is here because it is the Kalends, and because he is an augur, and because a man like that would come to the opening of a door if there were a crowd to see him do it."

"That is not what I asked."

"No," Cassia said. "It is not."

"Where is Comptus?"

"With the grain men. He will be here. He is always here, and always where he should not be, and always sorry."

A trumpet sounded from the direction of the old forum, and the crowd turned like a field in the wind. The procession was coming. First the lictors, then the priests, then the colleges of the augurs and the pontiffs in their striped robes, then the consuls, then the senate, a long river of white wool and purple borders, and above it all the standards and the eagles and the smoke of the incense. The people cheered. Some cheered because they loved the emperor. Some cheered because they were being paid to. Most cheered because the man beside them was cheering, and because a crowd in Rome is a thing that runs on its own momentum until it hits something.

Cassia did not cheer. She stood with her hands folded and watched the faces go by, and she read them the way she read the room at a dinner: the ambitious, the frightened, the bored, the drunk. There was Proculus, sweating under his toga, his son beside him looking as though he had been dressed by a committee. There was Pactus, tall and lean and smiling, walking with the ease of a man who had never had to ask permission for anything. There was the young Publius Sulpicius Corvinus, twenty-one and trying to look older, his hair combed forward in the fashion, his shoes too new.

And there, behind the consuls, in the place of honour that his augurate gave him, was his father.

Lucius Sulpicius Corvinus walked with his head up and his hands at his sides. He was forty-five and looked it in the way that men who have spent their lives outdoors and in the Senate look it: weathered, straight, the grey at his temples cut short. His toga was white as a Vestal's, and his shoes were old and good. He had the face of a man who had been handsome at twenty and had never quite forgiven the world for noticing that he was handsome still. He did not look at the crowd. He looked at the middle distance, where men of his kind looked, and the crowd looked at him, and Cassia looked at him, and for a moment the noise of the forum seemed to her like the noise of water over stones.

Then his head turned. It was not a large movement. It was the smallest movement in the world, the movement of a man who has been told there is someone behind him and has decided to be certain before he looks. His eyes found her across the width of the basilica, through the crowd, through the smoke and the standards and the twenty-four years.

He looked away.

Cassia's hands were cold. She put them in her sleeves and kept her face as still as a painted wall.

"You know him," Tertulla said, quietly.

"Everyone knows him."

"That is not what I asked."

"No," Cassia said. "It is not."

The ceremony went on for a long time. There was a sacrifice, and a reading of the decree, and a speech by the consul, and another speech by a man from the treasury who had been told to keep it short and had not. The emperor spoke last. He was a big man, broad and grey and careful, and he spoke in the voice of a soldier who had learned to speak in public because it was part of the job. He spoke of the city, and of the generations who had built it, and of the duty of a man to leave behind him something better than he had found. The crowd cheered at the right moments. Cassia watched the faces of the senators, who were watching the crowd, and understood that every word the emperor said was being weighed by a hundred men who were each calculating what it would be worth to them.

When it was over, the crowd began to move, and the movement was the dangerous part. The porticoes filled with people looking for their friends, their patrons, their wives. A child was separated from its mother and began to scream. A litter was overturned and its occupant, a fat man in a green cloak, was helped up by three strangers and robbed by a fourth. The vigiles appeared, and then the Praetorian guard, and the crowd opened for the guard in the way that water opens for a keel.

Cassia kept her party together by main force, her hand on Ione's wrist and Bathyllus's shoulder in front of her. They were in the portico of the library now, where the light was dimmer and the noise was muffled by the shelves of scrolls beyond the wall, and the smell of cedar and dust and old papyrus was so strong that it seemed to be a kind of silence.

"Wait here," Cassia said. "Bathyllus, the door."

She had intended to go to Comptus. She had intended to find out what he had learned from the grain office, and to be seen finding it out, because a woman who is seen to be informed is a woman whom men think twice about crossing. Instead she found herself standing before a bust of the divine Augustus, looking at a face that had been dead for a hundred years, and hearing footsteps behind her that she had been hearing, in one form or another, since she was eighteen.

"Domina."

She turned.

He was alone. That was the first thing she noticed; a man of his rank was never alone in public, and the fact that he had made himself alone was a statement. The second thing she noticed was the smell of him. Wool and cedar and the faint, clean smell of the oil his mother used in the house. She had not smelled it in twenty-four years, and it went through her like a blade.

"Senator."

"You are well."

"I am rich. It is often confused."

He did not smile. His face had the closed, careful look of a man who had spent his life in rooms where every word was recorded. "I sent a letter."

"I received it."

"You did not answer."

"I answered by receiving. It is the custom."

"You are not a customary woman."

"No," Cassia said. "I am not."

But before Corvinus could say more, a woman came into the portico with two slaves behind her and the particular stillness of a person who has decided to be noticed without appearing to want it. She was thirty-seven or so, with dark hair dressed high and a face that had been beautiful and had settled into something more useful. Her stola was the colour of cream, and her jewels were old and heavy and worth more than the house Cassia lived in. She stopped a few paces away and looked at Cassia with an expression that was not hostility and was not welcome.

"Domina," Corvinus said, and there was something in his voice that had not been there before. "My wife."

Aemilia Celsa inclined her head. "I have been looking for you," she said to her husband. "The consul's wife is asking after the games. She has a scheme for a pantomime that will require a great deal of money and a great many virgins, and I have been sent to find out which of the two is in shorter supply."

"Tell her the virgins," Cassia said.

Aemilia's eyes came to her. They were grey, and steady, and not at all surprised. "You are the woman my mother-in-law prays about."

"I am honoured. I did not know she prayed."

"She prays for the destruction of her enemies. It is a form of religion." Aemilia's mouth moved, almost a smile. "I have never been inside your house. I am told it is beautiful."

"It is comfortable. Beauty is a word men use when they want to pay less."

"And what word do you use?"

"Profitable."

Aemilia laughed, a short, dry sound, and for a moment she looked younger and much more tired. "I have heard a great deal about you," she said. "Some of it from my husband, most of it from women who have never met you. I find that the women who have never met you are the ones who know the most."

"They are the ones with the most to say."

"Yes." Aemilia looked at Corvinus, and then back at Cassia, and something passed between the two women that had no name. "I am told you receive senators."

"I receive everyone. It is the business."

"Do you receive their wives?"

The question hung in the air. Corvinus said, "Aemilia," in a voice that was almost a warning, and she ignored him.

"I receive anyone who comes with good manners and a reason to be there," Cassia said. "Wives are rarer than senators. They are also usually better company."

"Then perhaps I will come," Aemilia said. "One day. When I have a reason." She turned to her husband. "The consul's wife is waiting. You should come and be seen to be bored. It is what you are for."

She went out, her slaves behind her, and the portico seemed larger and emptier for her going.

"She does not know," Corvinus said.

"She knows everything," Cassia said. "She is a senator's wife. It is her profession."

A group of senators came past the end of the portico, talking loudly about the sacrifice, and Corvinus waited until they were gone. When he spoke again, his voice was lower.

"Felix came to me. He came to me before he came to you. He wanted three hundred thousand from me and he told me he had already spoken to you, and that you had agreed to help him."

"Then he lied to you."

"Did he?"

"Which part?" She looked at him. "I did not agree to help him. I agreed to hear him. There is a difference, and it is the difference between a whore and a hostess."

His jaw tightened. "You always did know how to put a knife in a compliment."

"I learned from the best." She said it before she could stop herself, and saw the blow land. He took it without moving, the way he had always taken things, and she hated him for it, and hated herself more.

"The accusation is being prepared," he said. "It will be laid before the consul within the month. It concerns my father."

"I heard."

"From Felix."

"From a senator who had drunk too much. Felix merely confirmed it." She held his eyes. "You have nothing to fear from me, Lucius. I have kept your secret for twenty-four years. I have kept it through the fire and the plague and the year I had nothing to eat but bread and olives. I am not going to give it away now, when I have finally built something worth keeping."

"Something worth keeping," he repeated, and looked around the library, the shelves, the scrolls, the marble, the whole vast monument of it. "You have done well."

"I have done what I could."

"You always did." He said it without warmth. "My mother is ill."

"I am sorry to hear it."

"No, you are not."

"No," Cassia said. "I am not. But I am polite, and I am sorry to hear it in the way that one is sorry to hear of a storm at sea. It is a great deal of weather and none of it mine."

He looked at her for a long moment. In the dim light of the library, with the dust moving in the shafts of sun, he looked younger than he had in the procession, and tired, and something else that she did not want to name.

"I need to know what you will say," he said. "If they come to you. If they ask you about Tibur."

"They will not come to me."

"They will come to your household."

That stopped her. She had thought of Felix, and of the Senate, and of the slow machinery of the law, but she had not thought of the household. The household was hers. The household was the one thing in the world that was entirely hers, bought and paid for and built out of people no one else had wanted.

"On what charge?" she said, and her voice was steady.

"Parricide."

The word hung in the air between them. A man who killed his father was sewn into a sack with a dog and a cock and a viper and thrown into the Tiber. The law had been old when the Republic was young. It was not often used. It was never used against a man who could afford a good lawyer, unless someone with power wanted it used.

"They cannot touch me," Cassia said. "I am free. I have been free for sixteen years. Free women are not put to the question."

"No," he said. "But your slaves are."

She looked past him, through the doorway, to where Bathyllus stood with his back to them, and beyond him to the crowd, and beyond that to the house on the Esquiline where Sabina was learning her letters and Chrysis was waiting for news of her brother and Thais was counting the linens and Celer was shouting at a boy about a fish.

"Then you had better hope," she said, "that your secret is worth more to me than they are."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only answer I have." She stepped closer to him, close enough that no one else could have heard, close enough that she could smell the cedar and the wool and the faint, clean oil. "Listen to me, Lucius. I have spent twenty-four years making sure that what happened at Tibur stayed at Tibur. I have paid for it. I have paid in ways you cannot imagine, and in some ways you can. I am not going to destroy my house to save yours. But I am not going to destroy yours to save mine, either, unless I have to. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Then we have an understanding."

"We have a truce."

"Call it what you like." She turned to go.

"Cassia."

She stopped. He had not used her name in twenty-four years. He had called her domina, and then he had called her nothing at all, and now he said her name like a man setting down a heavy thing he had been carrying for a long time.

"There is something else," he said.

"There is always something else."

Before she could answer, a shadow fell across the doorway. Decimus Iunius Pactus stood there with a cup in his hand and a smile that had been polished by years of public life. He was forty-nine, tall and lean, with the sort of weathered good looks that made men trust him and women regret it. He was a new man, which in Rome meant a man whose grandfather had not been a senator, and he had spent his whole career making the old families forget it and the new ones remember it.

"Corvinus," he said. "I did not know you were a patron of the libraries."

"I am a patron of nothing," Corvinus said, "except my own household."

"A harsh doctrine. The gods are patrons of everything, and look how well it has worked for them." Pactus's eyes slid to Cassia, and his smile changed, became more real and more careful. "Domina. We have not met. I am Pactus."

"I know who you are."

"Then I am flattered, and also slightly afraid."

"Men who say they are afraid are usually the least afraid men in the room."

"Not I. I am afraid of all sorts of things. Failure. Debt. My wife's cook." He sipped his wine. "I am told you keep the best table on the Esquiline."

"I keep a table. The rest is gossip."

"Gossip is the only currency that has never been debased." He glanced at Corvinus, and then back at her. "I hope you will permit me to call on you. I have a proposition that requires discretion, and I am told you are the soul of it."

"Come," Cassia said. "Bring your proposition. I will decide whether it is worth the salt."

"I would expect nothing less." He bowed, and went out, and the portico was quiet again.

"That man," Corvinus said, "is the reason the Asian command is not yet decided."

"I know."

"He will use you."

"Everyone uses me. I am a house. That is what houses are for." She looked at him. "The difference is that I decide what I charge."

Corvinus looked at the doorway where Pactus had stood, and then at her, and his face closed like a hand.

"The child," he said.

The library was very quiet. Somewhere beyond the wall, a man was laughing, and a cart went past on the stones, and the fountain in the courtyard played its endless, patient music.

"There was no child," Cassia said.

"Cassia—"

"There was no child. There was a girl who was told there was a child, and there was a liar who told her, and there was a grave with nothing in it, and there was a woman who has spent her life not thinking about it. That is all there is. If you have come to me for absolution, you have come to the wrong house. I sell pleasure. I do not sell that."

She walked away from him, past the bust of Augustus, past the shelves of scrolls, past Bathyllus, into the noise and the light. She did not look back. She had learned long ago that a woman who looks back is a woman who is followed.

They came out of the forum into the late afternoon, when the light had gone the colour of old bronze and the city was beginning to think about dinner. The crowd had thinned. The workmen on the column had gone home, and the scaffolding stood against the sky like a cage for a bird that had not yet been caught.

Cassia walked. She had sent the litter ahead and told the others to go with it, and Tertulla, who knew her, had not argued. She walked up the Via Lata with her veil over her hair and her hands in her sleeves, and the city closed around her the way it always did, indifferent and alive. A baker was taking bread out of an oven. A woman was shouting at a man on a balcony. A dog was eating something in the gutter that had once been a pigeon. Two boys were playing at gladiators with sticks, and one of them had tied a rag around his head like a retiarius, and the other was pretending to be the emperor.

She stopped at a fountain to drink and to let an old woman fill her jar first, and while she stood there she saw, across the street, a girl in a brown cloak buying figs from a stall. The girl was fifteen or sixteen, with a plain face and a sulky mouth and the unmistakable bearing of a house that had money. She was not alone. She had a slave with her, a thin woman with a basket, and the slave was looking around in the way of a slave who has been told to be somewhere she should not be.

The girl turned, and Cassia saw her eyes. They were her father's eyes. They were also, in the way that these things happen, the eyes of the portrait in the atrium of the house on the Palatine that Cassia had never seen, and the eyes of a man who had once stood in a garden at Tibur with his hands full of roses.

The girl saw Cassia looking and did not look away. For a moment they regarded each other across the street, the freedwoman and the senator's daughter, and the noise of the city went on around them, and neither of them moved.

Then the slave touched the girl's arm, and the girl turned, and they went off toward the Palatine, and Cassia stood with her cup of water and watched them go.

"Domina," Bathyllus said, behind her. "The house."

"Yes," she said. "The house."

She walked on. The water was cold in her stomach, and the memory of cedar was in her throat, and somewhere behind her, in the new forum, a workman was hammering at the column, fixing another piece of the emperor's glory into place. It would stand for a thousand years, she thought. It would outlast her, and Corvinus, and the Senate, and the whole squabbling city. It would outlast the secret. That was the thing about stone. It did not care what it was built on.

At the corner of the Clivus Suburanus, a man was waiting for her. He was a small man in a brown tunic, with the look of a clerk, and he stepped out of a doorway as she passed and said, quietly, "The lady is to know that the midwife is alive."

Bathyllus moved faster than a man his size should have. His hand closed on the man's collar, and the man did not resist. He looked at Cassia with the flat, patient eyes of a man who had been paid to deliver a message and had been paid enough to be calm about it.

"What midwife?" Cassia said.

"The one from Tibur," the man said. "The one who was at the birth. She is old, and she is not well, and she has been asking for the lady. She says she has something to give her."

"Who sent you?"

"No one sent me. She paid me with a ring." The man held out his hand. In his palm was a small bronze ring, worn thin, with a pattern of two snakes twined around a staff. Cassia had seen it before. She had seen it on the hand of a woman who had held her down on a bed in a cell at Tibur and told her to push, and had told her afterward that the child had never breathed.

She took the ring. It was cold, and lighter than it looked.

"Where is she?"

"At Tibur. In the village below the hill. The house with the fig tree." The man pulled free of Bathyllus's hand and straightened his tunic. "She says the lady should come before the spring. She says the lady will want to hear what she has to say."

He went away down the street, and Bathyllus looked at Cassia, and Cassia looked at the ring in her hand.

"Domina?"

"Go home," she said. "Tell Hermeros to bring the strongbox to my room. Tell Thais to come to me. And tell no one else anything."

She began to walk again, faster now, the ring closed in her fist. The city went on around her, buying and selling and eating and dying, and above the rooftops the new forum of the emperor shone in the last of the light like something that had been built by gods who had never had a mother, and never been told a lie.
