They left Rome before dawn on the day after the funeral, in two carriages and a cart, with Bathyllus riding ahead and two of Tertulla's men behind. The sky was grey and the streets were empty and the city smelled of wet ash and the river. Cassia sat in the first carriage with the wooden box on the seat beside her and her hand on the lid, and she did not look back at the house as they passed the end of the street, because she had learned a long time ago that a woman who looks back is a woman who is followed.

Tertulla sat opposite her with a basket of bread and cheese and a flask of wine and the expression of a woman who has decided to be practical in the face of other people's grief.

"Eat something," she said.

"I am not hungry."

"You will be. The road to Tibur is eight hours and the inn is bad." Tertulla cut a piece of cheese and handed it across. "Eat it, or I will tell the driver to stop at every milestone and I will make you walk."

Cassia ate the cheese. It tasted of nothing.

They went out through the Porta Tiburtina and onto the Via Tiburtina, and the city fell away behind them in stages: first the tombs and the market gardens, then the villas of the rich with their walls and their cypresses, then the plain, wide and green and full of larks. The sun came up. The road was crowded with carts and mules and people walking with bundles, and once they passed a procession taking a body out to a family tomb, and the mourning women were wailing in the way that is half grief and half performance, and Cassia thought about a boy in a grave outside the Esquiline gate.

They stopped at an inn at the ninth milestone to water the horses, and the innkeeper's wife brought out a jug of wine and a plate of figs and told them that the road ahead was clear and that there had been soldiers on it the day before, going north.

"Soldiers?" Tertulla said.

"Two. Not in uniform. They had good horses and they did not stop to eat." The woman wiped her hands on her apron. "They asked about a house at Tibur. The one with the fig tree."

Cassia set down her cup.

"Did they say why?"

"No. They paid for their wine and they went on." The woman looked at her curiously. "Are you going to Tibur, domina?"

"Yes."

"Then you will want to be careful. There are men on that road who are not what they seem."

At the ninth milestone there was a shrine to the springs, a small stone altar under a roof, with a cup and a jug and a great many rags tied to the branches of a tree. Travellers left offerings there for safe passage, and the rags were the clothes of sick children, and the place smelled of old water and dust.

Tertulla went to the altar and put down a coin and stood for a moment with her head bowed. Cassia stayed by the carriage.

"You should make an offering," Tertulla said, when she came back.

"To whom? The springs?"

"To whatever is listening." Tertulla climbed into the carriage. "I do not believe in the gods. I believe in the usefulness of pretending to, in front of the servants." She settled her skirts. "What are you going to do when you have the truth?"

"I do not know."

"You must have thought about it."

"I have thought about it for twenty-four years. I have thought about it so much that I have worn a groove in it." Cassia looked out at the road. "When I was in Puteoli I used to imagine finding him. I was twenty and I was nothing and I imagined walking into a house and saying, 'I am your mother,' and having him run to me. When I was thirty I imagined finding him and finding that he was dead, and I imagined standing at the grave and saying all the things I had never been able to say. When I was forty I stopped imagining it, because I had discovered that imagination is a kind of debt, and that the interest is very high." She turned. "And now I am forty-two and I am on a road to Tibur with a box in my lap, and I find that I have no imagination left at all. Only arithmetic."

"What does the arithmetic say?"

"It says that I have a son who is alive, and a household that depends on me, and an accusation that will destroy the man I love if I cannot stop it, and an enemy who has already burned my house and killed a child." Cassia's voice was steady. "It says that I cannot afford to be a mother. Not this year. This year I have to be a woman with a knife."

Tertulla was silent for a moment. Then she said, "You are not a woman with a knife."

"I am a woman with a ledger and a house full of secrets and a great deal of money. It is the same thing, in Rome."

They drove on. The sun went higher and the day grew warm and the plain gave way to the hills, and the hills were green and wooded and full of the sound of water. Cassia looked out of the window at the road and did not see it. She was thinking about a summer twenty-four years before, and a garden, and a boy who came to the kitchen for bread, and the way the light had fallen through the vines.

The inn at Tibur was a long, low building with a stable and a yard and a fig tree by the gate, and they came into it in the late afternoon with the light going gold and the hills turning blue behind them. Bathyllus had been there before and had spoken to the innkeeper, and there were rooms ready, and a boy to take the horses, and a woman in the kitchen making something that smelled of onions and thyme.

Cassia did not go into the inn. She stood in the yard with her hand on the wooden box and looked up the hill.

The villa was still there. She could see the roofline above the trees, and a corner of the portico, and the terrace where she had stood with the cushions in her arms and looked down at a young man with dust on his boots. It was a different colour now, and there was a new wing at the east end, and the vineyard had been replanted, but it was the same house, and the sight of it went through her like a spear.

"Domina," Bathyllus said. "It is owned by a wine merchant now. He is away. The steward has been told that a lady from Rome may come to see the garden, and he has agreed, for a price."

"Not today."

"No."

"Tomorrow. I will go tomorrow." She turned away. "Take me to the village."

***

She went up to the villa in the last hour of the afternoon, with Bathyllus and the steward and no one else.

The steward was a thin, nervous man named Faustillus who had been told that a lady from Rome wished to see the garden, and who had decided that a fee was a fee and that it was not his business to ask why. He walked ahead of her up the hill and talked about the vineyard and the new press and the difficulty of getting good labour, and Cassia walked behind him and did not hear a word.

The gate was the same gate. The hinge still squealed in the same place. The portico was the same portico, and the vine on it was a different vine, and the mosaic of dolphins in the bathhouse had been repaired again, badly, by someone who did not understand dolphins. The garden was smaller than she remembered, and the box hedges were leggy, and the fountain was dry.

"The mistress of the house is in Rome," Faustillus said. "She comes in the summer. The master is in Campania. The house is kept ready."

"Leave me," Cassia said.

"Domina—"

"Leave me. I will not steal the silver. I want to stand in the garden."

He went, reluctantly, and Cassia stood in the middle of the garden and turned around slowly, once, and the whole of it came back to her at once: the smell of the rosemary, the sound of the water from the falls, the way the light lay on the terrace in the late afternoon, the boy coming up from the vineyard with his boots dusty and his hair wet from the fountain.

She walked around the house. The kitchen door was the same. The bench by the door was the same bench, or a bench in the same place, and she stood and looked at it and remembered sitting on it with a book of poems in her lap and a young man beside her saying, *read me the one about the sparrow.*

The door to the corridor was open. She went in.

The cell at the back of the villa was a storeroom now. It had shelves in it and sacks of grain and a smell of mice. The small high window was still there. The whitewash had been redone, and the fish that a child had scratched into the plaster was gone, covered over by a later coat, and Cassia stood in the middle of the floor and looked at the wall where it had been and put her hand flat against the plaster.

She had been eighteen years old in this room. She had been in labour in this room. She had heard her son cry in this room.

"Domina?" Faustillus, in the corridor, nervous. "Domina, is everything well?"

"Yes," Cassia said. "Everything is well. I have seen what I came to see."

She came out of the room and walked back through the garden and out of the gate, and at the gate she stopped and looked back at the house and said, to no one in particular, "It is a good house. Tell your master that a lady from Rome says it is a good house, and that he should fix the fountain."

She went down the hill to the village with the light going and the first lamps being lit in the cottages, and she did not look back again.

***

The house with the fig tree stood at the end of a lane below the villaThe house with the fig tree stood at the end of a lane below the villa, where the ground flattened out and the stream ran between stone walls. It was small and old and had a tiled roof and a door painted a faded blue, and there was a chicken in the yard and a boy of six sitting on the step with a stick.

Marcella came out when she heard the carriage. She had walked back from Rome with Bathyllus and had been at the house for two days, and she looked tired and frightened and relieved all at once.

"She has been asking for you," she said. "She has not slept. She sits up in the chair by the fire and she will not go to bed."

"Take me in."

The room was low and dark and warm, and the smell of it was the smell of old age and woodsmoke and herbs. Psyche sat in a chair by the hearth with a blanket over her knees. She was smaller than Cassia remembered — much smaller, as though she had been left out in the sun too long and had shrunk — and her hands were twisted with the disease that old women get, and one eye was clouded. But the other eye was sharp and grey and it found Cassia the moment she came through the door.

"Domina," she said.

Cassia stood in the middle of the room and looked at her. Twenty-four years. She had imagined this meeting a thousand times, and in every version she had been eloquent, and in every version the old woman had wept, and in every version she had said the things that had been in her mouth for twenty-four years.

What she said was, "You told me he did not breathe."

The old woman's face worked. "I did."

"You told me he was buried under the fig tree."

"I did."

"You let them put me on a cart and send me to Puteoli, and you let me believe that my son was dead, and you have known for twenty-four years that he was alive." Cassia's voice was steady. "Why?"

"Because I was afraid," Psyche said.

"Of what?"

"Of her." The old woman's hands tightened on the blanket. "You do not know what she was like, domina. You were a girl in the kitchen. You saw her fan and her medicine box and her cold voice. I saw her in that room at night with the lamp in her hand and the child in her arms, and I saw her face when she told me what to do, and I have never in my life been so frightened of anyone." She looked at the fire. "She said that if I told you, she would have me killed. She said that if I told anyone, she would have my daughter sold and my granddaughter sold and my son-in-law put in a mine. She said that she had done worse things than that for less, and I believed her, because I had been in that house for twenty years and I knew what she was."

Cassia sat down on the stool by the fire. Her legs would not hold her.

"I have something else," Psyche said. "Something I did not put in the box."

She reached inside her dress and brought out a small folded cloth, and opened it on her knee. Inside was a curl of hair, fine and dark, tied with a thread.

"I cut it off him before she took him," she said. "I do not know why. I have never known why. I put it in my dress and I forgot about it, and I found it three days later when I was washing, and I have kept it ever since." She held it out. "It is all I have of him. I thought — I thought if I ever met you, I would give it to you. And then I thought you would kill me, and I did not know which I was more afraid of."

Cassia took the curl of hair. It was so light that she could barely feel it in her palm, and it was dark, dark as her own, not the honey colour of the young man in the yard on the Via Ostiensis. She closed her hand around it.

"His hair is lighter now," she said. "I saw him. I stood in the street and looked at him."

Psyche's face changed. "You have seen him?"

"Yes."

"Is he — is he well?"

"He is a potter. He has clay on his arms and a father who calls him in to supper and a laugh that I could hear from the street." Cassia put the curl of hair carefully into the box with the receipt. "He is alive, and he is well, and he does not know that I exist, and I am going to have to decide what to do about that."

"Tell him," Psyche said. "Tell him everything. He has a right to know."

"Does he?" Cassia closed the box. "He has a mother who died giving birth to him, as far as he knows. He has a father who raised him. If I go to him and tell him that his mother is a woman who runs a house of pleasure on the Esquiline, and that his grandmother is a murderer, and that his father is a senator who has spent twenty-four years believing him dead — what does he gain? A name? He has a name. A fortune? He has a trade. A family? He has one." She looked at the fire. "What he gains is the truth. What he loses is the life he has."

"That is not for you to decide," Psyche said.

"No," Cassia said. "It is not. But it is for me to offer, and I have been in this world long enough to know that a truth offered to a person who has not asked for it is not always a gift. Sometimes it is a stone thrown into a well." She put the box in her sleeve. "I will tell him. But I will tell him in my own time, and in my own way, and not because a frightened old woman in a chair thinks it would make her feel better."

"Tell me," she said. "From the beginning. Tell me everything."

And Psyche told her.

***

The night of the birth, the mistress had come to the room three times. The first time she had brought the cup. The second time she had stood in the doorway while the pains came and had said nothing at all. The third time she had come in with the steward behind her and had waited until the child was born.

"He was a fine boy," Psyche said. "Big. He came out yelling. I have delivered a hundred babies and I know a healthy one when I see it. I put him on your belly and he yelled, and you put your hand on him, and then she said, 'Give him to me.'"

"And you gave him to her."

"I gave him to her." The old woman's voice cracked. "I have thought about it every day for twenty-four years. I should have run. I should have taken him and run out of the door and down the hill. But there was a steward and there were two men in the yard and I was sixty years old and I had a daughter at home. So I gave him to her, and she wrapped him in a cloth, and she said, 'The girl is not to know. She is to be told it was stillborn. In the morning you will tell her, and you will tell her the same thing every day for the rest of your life, and if you ever tell her anything else I will know.'"

"And then?"

"And then she took him out of the room, and I gave you the poppy, and you slept. And in the morning she sent for me and she had a man with her — a dealer, a man who bought and sold children, a man named Caepio — and she gave the child to him and he gave her money and a paper, and she gave the paper to me."

"Why you?"

"Because she did not want it in the house." Psyche looked at the fire. "She said a paper in a house is a thing that can be found. She said I was to keep it, and that if I ever spoke of it she would have me killed, and that if I kept it and said nothing I would be paid a little every year, and that if I lost it she would have me killed anyway." She laughed, a dry, terrible sound. "So I kept it. I kept it under my bed for twenty-four years, and I have looked at it every year on his birthday, and I have prayed for him every day, and I have never once had the courage to go to Rome and find him."

Cassia sat very still. The fire moved. The boy outside said something to the chicken.

"Where is the grave?" she said.

"There is no grave."

"What do you mean, there is no grave?"

"There is a place under the fig tree where I dug a hole, and there is nothing in it. I put a stone there and I told the household that the child was buried, and your mistress told the same story, and the household believed it because they wanted to." Psyche looked at her. "There is no grave, domina. There never was. Your son is not in the garden at Tibur. He is in Rome."

Cassia stood up. She walked to the window and stood with her back to the room and looked out at the yard, at the chicken, at the boy on the step, at the lane and the wall and the hill beyond.

"The paper," she said.

Psyche nodded at Marcella, who went to a chest in the corner and took out a small wooden box and brought it to Cassia. It was not the box that Bathyllus had brought to Rome. That box had come from under the old woman's bed and had been sealed with a leather strap and had contained, Cassia now understood, nothing but the things a frightened old woman keeps to remind herself that she is not mad.

This box was smaller. It had a lock. Marcella gave Cassia the key, and Cassia opened it.

Inside there was a receipt.

It was a single sheet of papyrus, folded twice, brown with age, and the ink was faded but legible. It said, in a clerk's careful hand:

*Received from the household of M. Sulpicius Corvinus, for one male infant, aged one day, of good health and no defect, the sum of four hundred sesterces. The infant is delivered to Gaius Vibidius, potter, of the Via Ostiensis, outside the Porta Trigemina, in the city of Rome, to be raised as his own. No claim upon the child shall be made by the seller or by any person on the seller's behalf. Witnessed by L. Caepio.*

And below, in a different hand, a line that had been added afterward:

*The child is called Gaius Vibidius Nestor.*

Cassia read it three times. Then she folded it and put it back in the box and closed the lid and held the box against her chest with both hands, and stood in the middle of the room with the fire behind her and the light going out of the sky, and she did not cry, because she had done her crying in a street in Rome with Tertulla's arms around her, and because there was no crying left in her now, only a hard, clear, cold thing that she recognized because she had felt it once before, on a cart on the road to Puteoli.

"Marcella," she said.

"Domina."

"Go to the inn and tell the lady Tertulla to have the notary brought from the town. Tell her I want a statement drawn up and witnessed and sealed, and I want it done tonight, before the old woman sleeps. Every word she has said. Every name. The cup, the child, the dealer, the money, the paper." She turned. "And tell Bathyllus to bring the carriage to the door at first light. We are going back to Rome tomorrow."

Psyche looked up at her. "You are not going to kill me."

"No."

"You should."

"I know." Cassia looked at her. "I have thought about it for twenty-four years. I have imagined it in a hundred ways. And now I am standing in your house with your fire at my back and your granddaughter in the next room, and I find that I cannot do it, because you are an old woman with a blanket over your knees who has been afraid for twenty-four years, and because I have spent my whole life trying not to become the sort of person who kills old women in their chairs." She put the box in her sleeve. "But I will not forgive you. Do not mistake me. I will put your statement before the Senate and I will let them decide what to do with you, and if they decide to punish you, I will not lift a finger. That is not mercy. It is arithmetic."

Psyche nodded slowly. "It is more than I deserve."

"Yes," Cassia said. "It is."

***

The notary came at dusk with his tablets and his clerk and his seal, and the statement took two hours, and Psyche gave it in a steady voice with her hand on the arm of the chair and her eyes on the fire. When it was done she made her mark, and Marcella witnessed it, and Cassia witnessed it, and the notary sealed it and gave it to her with the air of a man who has just been paid a great deal of money and would like to be paid a great deal more.

They went back to the inn in the dark. Cassia did not sleep. She sat in the room with the statement on the table and the box beside it, and at midnight there was a knock at the door, and Bathyllus came in with his face set.

"Domina. There are men in the village."

"Felix's men?"

"Two of them. They came to the house with the fig tree an hour ago and asked for the old woman. Marcella told them she was asleep and would not be disturbed, and they went away, but they are at the inn at the crossroads and they have been asking about a carriage with a woman in it." He paused. "They have horses. Ours are tired. If we wait for the morning, they will follow us."

"Then we do not wait."

"Domina, the road at night is—"

"I have been on worse roads," Cassia said. "Wake the household. We leave in a quarter of an hour."

They left at midnight with the lamps lit and the horses fresh enough, and they went down the hill and onto the Via Tiburtina with the dark on either side and the stars overhead, and Bathyllus rode behind with a sword under his cloak and did not look back. They were three miles out of Tibur when the men came after them.

There were two of them, on good horses, and they came fast, and one of them shouted something that was lost in the wind. Bathyllus turned his horse and drew across the road. There was a scuffle in the dark, and the sound of hooves, and a cry, and then the carriage lurched and Tertulla caught Cassia by the arm and said, "Do not look. Do not look."

They did not stop. They went on at a gallop for a mile and then the horses were blown and they had to walk them, and Bathyllus caught them up at the milestone with blood on his face and a cut across his forearm and a riderless horse on a leading rein.

"One of them is in the ditch," he said. "The other has gone back for him. They will not follow tonight."

"You are hurt."

"It is a scratch." He wiped his face. "Domina. They were not trying to kill us. They were trying to stop the carriage. They wanted the old woman, or the paper, or both."

"They wanted the paper." Cassia put her hand on the box in her sleeve. "They will not have it."

They came into Rome at dawn, through the Porta Tiburtina, with the city waking around them and the smoke of a thousand fires going up into the grey air, and Cassia sat in the carriage with the box in her hands and the statement in her sleeve and looked at the walls of the city and thought: I have it. I have the paper, and the woman, and the words. I have the truth in my hands for the first time in twenty-four years.

And then she thought: and it is not enough.

Because the truth, in Rome, was not a thing that set you free. It was a thing that had to be proved, in public, before men who had reasons of their own, and the men who had reasons of their own had money and horses and men in the palace and a fire that could be set in the night.

She came into the house on the Esquiline at the third hour, and the house was half black and half standing, and the household came out into the yard to meet her, and Thais said, "Domina, there is a letter from the Palatine."

Cassia took it. It was sealed with a raven, and the hand was Corvinus's, and it said:

*My mother is dying. She has asked for you. Come tonight. Bring what you have.*

She stood in the burnt yard with the letter in her hand and the ash under her feet and the morning light coming over the roof, and she said, "Tell the household to rest. Tell Hermeros to bring me the black book. And tell Bathyllus to have the horses ready at dusk."

"Where are we going?" Tertulla said.

"To the Palatine," Cassia said. "To see a woman who has been waiting twenty-four years to tell me the truth, and who is going to tell it to me whether she wants to or not."
