The trial was held in the Senate house on the sixth day before the Kalends of August, and the city had been waiting for it for a month.

They had begun to gather before dawn. By the first hour the Forum was full, and by the third the crowd had overflowed into the basilicas and the porticoes and the steps of the temples, and the shopkeepers had put up their shutters and the wine sellers had brought out their barrels and the pickpockets had come down from the Subura in force. There were food sellers and water sellers and a man with a performing monkey and a woman selling little clay figures of the principals — Corvinus with a sword, Pactus with a scroll, Cassia with a purse — and the whole thing had the air of a festival that everyone was slightly ashamed of enjoying.

Cassia came at the fourth hour with Bathyllus and Tertulla and the leather satchel, and she was not stopped at the door this time. The lictor stepped aside. The clerk looked at her summons and nodded. And she walked up the aisle of the Curia with her head up and her hands steady, and the whole chamber turned to look at her.

Aemilia was in the gallery.

Women were not admitted to the floor, but the gallery above the benches had been opened for the families of the accused, and she was there with young Sulpicia beside her and a slave behind them, and she was dressed in black for her mother-in-law and she had the composure of a woman who had decided that she would not be seen to be afraid.

Cassia looked up at her as she came in, and Aemilia inclined her head, once, and young Sulpicia did the same, and the small movement went through the chamber like a rumour.

He is not alone, it said. Whatever else is true, he is not alone.

The benches were full. The consuls sat on their curule chairs. The emperor's secretary sat on a chair to one side with a clerk and a tablet, and he was a thin, grey, careful man who looked as though he had spent his life reading documents and had never once been surprised by one. The senators sat in their ranks. The prosecutor's bench was full of lawyers. The defence bench had Corvinus and two advocates and a row of empty seats where his supporters should have been, and the emptiness was a statement in itself.

Pactus was there, in his white toga, at the front of the prosecutor's bench. He looked rested and calm and entirely at ease.

The consul called the house to order. The clerk read the charge. And then the prosecution rose.

---

Crispus spoke for an hour and a half.

It was a good speech. It was better than good; it was the speech of a man who had been paid a great deal of money and had spent a month polishing every sentence. He spoke about the sanctity of the family, and the duty of a son to his father, and the terrible crime of parricide, which the law punished not with a fine or an exile but with the sack and the river and the viper and the dog. He spoke about the evidence: the physician's certificate, the assistant's testimony, the steward's statement, the midwife's account of the night. He spoke about the quarrel between the accused and his father, and the threats that had been overheard, and the haste with which the accused had left for Syria within the week. And then he spoke about the woman.

He did not name her. He did not need to. He spoke about a freedwoman who kept a house on the Esquiline, and who had made a fortune out of the appetites of senators, and who had been for twenty years the intimate of the accused; and he said that it was she who had corrupted the household at Tibur, she who had seduced the young man, she who had been present at the death, and she who had spent twenty-four years buying silence and influence and the friendship of men who should have known better.

"Conscript fathers," he said, "this is not a trial about a poisoned cup. It is a trial about a family that was destroyed from within by appetite and by money. The cup was the means. The woman was the cause."

He sat down. There was a murmur of approval from the benches, and a rumble of something else from the galleries, and the consul struck the floor.

The defence rose. Corvinus's advocate was a young man named Sura, who had been chosen because he was clever and because he was cheap and because he was the only advocate in Rome who had agreed to take the case.

Sura was good. He was better than good; he was brilliant, in the way that young men are brilliant when they have nothing to lose. He took Crispus's speech apart piece by piece: the physician's certificate was signed by a man who had been paid; the assistant had been paid; the steward had been paid; the midwife had been paid; every witness in the prosecution's case had been paid by somebody, and the somebody was a banker on the Via Lata who held the mortgages on the estate. He spoke about the fire on the Esquiline, and the boy who had died in it, and the arsonist who had been paid by a steward, and he said that the men who had burned a house and killed a child were the same men who had brought this accusation, and that the Senate should ask itself why.

There was a roar from the galleries. The consul struck the floor three times.

Then Corvinus rose to speak for himself.

He had not been required to. His advocate had advised him against it; a defendant who speaks is a defendant who can be trapped. But he rose anyway, and the chamber went quiet in the way that a chamber goes quiet when a man who has been silent for a long time stands up.

"Conscript fathers," he said. "I am not going to make a speech. I have made a great many speeches in this room and most of them were designed to make men vote for something, and this one is not. This one is a statement of fact." He paused. "I did not kill my father. I was in Syria when he died. I received the news at Antioch and I wept, and I have the letter I wrote to my mother in reply, and it is in the archive of this house, and any man who wishes may read it."

He looked at the benches.

"But I did something worse than kill him, and I want the house to know it, because the house is going to hear it from the prosecution before the day is out. When my father told me that he intended to sell the girl I loved and dispose of her child, I did nothing. I did not go to the governor. I did not go to the courts. I did not take her away in the night, though I could have, and though she would have come. I stood in a room and I listened to a man tell me what he was going to do, and I said nothing, and I got on a ship, and I did not come back for two years." He looked at Cassia. "That is the truth about me. It is worse than the charge. And I have carried it for twenty-four years."

The chamber was silent.

"The prosecution will say that I am a man who covered up a death to keep his inheritance. I am a man who covered up a life — the life of a woman who was sold, and the life of a child who was given away — because I was twenty years old and I was a coward, and because I believed that if I said nothing, the world would let me keep the things I had." He paused. "It did not. It never does. The world has a way of collecting."

He sat down.

In the gallery, young Sulpicia had gone very still.

She had been told, at home, in the careful words that families use, that her father had done nothing wrong and that the trial was politics. She had believed it, or had decided to believe it, which is not the same thing. And now she was sitting in a gallery above the Senate house and listening to her father say that he had stood in a room and let a man sell a girl and give away a child, and that it was worse than the charge, and she was sixteen years old and the floor of the world had moved.

Aemilia put her hand over her daughter's hand. She did not say anything. There was nothing to say that would have helped, and she was wise enough to know it.

Crispus rose at once. "The accused has made a very moving speech. But the house will observe that it is not evidence. It is an admission of moral failure, not a denial of the charge. The fact that a man is a coward does not mean he is not a murderer."

"No," Corvinus said. "But the fact that a man is a coward does not mean he is a murderer either. And the house will have to decide which of us it believes: a delator who has been convicted of extortion, a steward who has been paid, a physician's assistant who has been paid, and a prosecutor whose banker holds my mortgage — or a woman who has lost everything she had and who has no reason to lie."

The consul struck the floor. "The house will hear the witnesses."

Then the witnesses were called.

---

The midwife Psyche was called first, and she gave her evidence in a closed session, with the galleries cleared and the doors shut and the clerks sworn to silence.

She told the story again, as she had told it in June: the cup, the night, the child, the cry, the mistress with the lamp, the dealer, the receipt, the twenty-four years. She was older and weaker than she had been, and she had been brought from Tibur in a litter, and she gave her evidence sitting down, which was against the rules and which the consul allowed because she was seventy and could not stand.

When she had finished, the receipt was produced and read aloud. The clerk read it slowly, in the flat voice of a man reading a shopping list, and the chamber listened in absolute silence.

*Received from the household of M. Sulpicius Corvinus, for one male infant, aged one day, of good health and no defect, the sum of four hundred sesterces. The infant is delivered to Gaius Vibidius, potter, of the Via Ostiensis, outside the Porta Trigemina, in the city of Rome, to be raised as his own. No claim upon the child shall be made by the seller or by any person on the seller's behalf. Witnessed by L. Caepio.*

*The child is called Gaius Vibidius Nestor.*

The clerk stopped. The chamber was silent. Then the consul said, "The name of the child and the name of the potter are to be struck from the record and sealed. They are not to be spoken outside this chamber. Any man who speaks them will answer to the house for contempt."

The clerk struck them out. The receipt was sealed and put in the archive. And the Senate of Rome sat in a silence that was, for a moment, almost decent.

Then Crispus rose.

"The house has heard the midwife," he said. "The house has heard the receipt. And the house will observe that the receipt proves nothing about the death of Marcus Sulpicius. It proves that a child was born and sold. It proves that the household at Tibur was a place of vice and secrecy. It does not prove that the accused did not kill his father. In fact, conscript fathers, it proves the opposite: it proves that there was a secret in that house worth killing for."

He called the physician's assistant, Faustus. He called the old steward, who was brought in on a chair and who gave his evidence in a whisper and contradicted himself twice. He called a slave from the Tibur household who had been bought by the prosecution and who said that he had heard the young master threaten his father. He called them one by one, and each of them added a small stone to the wall he was building, and Cassia sat in her seat and watched the wall go up.

The prosecution's witnesses did not do as well as Crispus had hoped.

Faustus, the physician's assistant, was a nervous man of forty with a twitch in his left eye, and he gave his evidence in a voice that was too loud. He said that his master had told him that the death was not natural. He said that his master had been paid to sign the certificate. He said that he had been paid to leave Rome and had come back when the money ran out.

"Who paid you?" Cassia asked.

"The knight Felix."

"Out of whose purse?"

"I do not know."

"You do not know, or you do not wish to say?"

"I do not know." Faustus wiped his face. "The money came from a steward. I never met the man who paid for it."

"Was the steward's name Anthus?"

"I do not know his name."

"Was he a thin man, fifty years old, with grey hair and a mole on his cheek?"

Faustus stared at her. "Yes."

"Then you do know his name," Cassia said. "It is Anthus, and he is the steward of the senator Pactus, and he has this morning admitted in this chamber to paying for the burning of my house. The house will draw its own conclusions about the reliability of a witness who has been paid by a man who pays for fires."

There was a rumble of laughter. Crispus objected. The consul allowed the question and the chamber to draw its own conclusions.

The old steward was worse. He was eighty and he had a bad chest and he had been brought from Ficulea in a litter, and he gave his evidence in a whisper that the clerk had to repeat. He said that he had been in the house on the night of the death. He said that he had seen the mistress go into the master's room with a cup. He said that he had been paid to keep silent, and he named the man who had paid him — not Pactus, not Sittius, but a clerk in the palace whose name he did not know.

Then Cassia produced the statement of the forger.

It was a single sheet of papyrus, taken down by Junia's son in the presence of two witnesses, and it said that a man named Secundus had forged a letter in the year of the consulship of Silanus at the request of the knight Felix, and that the letter had been used to destroy the family of Lucius Junius, praetor, and that he was dying of a disease of the lungs and wished to clear his conscience before he met his maker.

The clerk read it. The chamber listened. And Crispus, for the first time that day, looked at Pactus.

Pactus did not look back.

Then the consul called a recess, and the chamber emptied into the porticoes to eat and drink and tell each other what they had heard.

Cassia went out into the colonnade with Tertulla and stood in the shade with a cup of water she did not drink. The crowd moved around her. Nobody spoke to her. A senator she had dined with four times that year went past with his eyes on the columns. A woman she had lent money to at a rate that would have made a Carthaginian blush looked at her and then looked away. Two young men in the doorway turned their backs.

"You see," Tertulla said.

"I see."

"Does it surprise you?"

"No." Cassia watched the crowd. "It surprises me a little that it does not hurt more. I have known these people for sixteen years. I have fed them and lent them money and found husbands for their daughters. And this morning I am a whore who has been bought by a banker, and by this evening I will be something worse."

"You will be the woman who told the truth to the Senate," Tertulla said. "Some of them will hate you for it, and some of them will be ashamed for the rest of their lives, and one or two will remember that you were the only person in this whole affair who did not lie." She paused. "And when it is over, half of them will come back to your table as though nothing had happened, and you will let them, because you are a businesswoman, and because you have a house to run."

"And I will let them," Cassia said.

"Yes."

They stood in the shade for a while.

At the far end of the colonnade, Pactus's wife was standing with her women. She was a small, handsome, tired woman of about forty who had been married to Pactus for twenty years and had borne him six children, and she had never once spoken to Cassia in all that time. She looked across the colonnade now, and she looked at Cassia for a long moment, and then she inclined her head — the smallest possible movement, and not a friendly one, and not an unfriendly one either. It was the nod of a woman who had heard her husband lie in a public place and had already known that he would.

"She knows," Tertulla said.

"She has known for years." Cassia set the cup down on the parapet. "That is the thing nobody tells you about the wives. They always know. They know before the mistress does."

The bell rang for the afternoon session.

Then it was her turn.

She stood in the middle of the floor with the satchel at her side and the whole chamber looking at her, and she answered the questions.

Yes, she had been a slave in the household. Yes, she had been the mistress's maid. Yes, she had been pregnant by the son. Yes, she had been sold to Puteoli. Yes, she had bought her freedom and built a house and a business. Yes, the senator had come to her house. Yes, she had received him. Yes, she had received half the men in that chamber, and if the house wished to pursue that line of inquiry she would be happy to provide a list, and she was sure the Senate would find it instructive.

There was laughter. The consul did not strike the floor.

Crispus rose again. "The witness has admitted that she is the accused's mistress."

"I have admitted that I am the accused's friend," Cassia said. "There is a difference, and it is the difference between a woman who is paid and a woman who is trusted. I have been paid by a great many men in this city. The senator Corvinus is not one of them."

"You have admitted that you were present at the death."

"I have admitted that I was present at a death in a house where I was a slave, and that I was given a cup by the mistress of that house, and that I woke to be told that my son was dead and that I was to be sold." Cassia's voice was steady. "If the house wishes to know who killed Marcus Sulpicius, I suggest it ask the woman who gave me the cup. But she is dead, and her son is on trial, and it seems to me that the prosecution has chosen the easier target."

The chamber stirred. Crispus's face tightened.

"The witness is hostile," he said. "The prosecution asks that her household be questioned again —"

"The household has been questioned," the consul said. "The questioning produced nothing. The prefect's report is before the house."

"The prefect's report is incomplete."

"Then it is incomplete," the consul said. "The house will not order a second questioning on the word of a prosecutor who has already had one. Proceed."

Crispus's mouth was a thin line. He turned back to Cassia.

"You have a document," he said. "A statement, signed by the knight Felix, who is now convicted of extortion and calumny, and whose word is worthless. You have a statement from an arsonist, who is a criminal. You have a letter that you claim was written by a clerk in the palace, and which you have not produced." He stepped closer. "You have nothing, domina. You have a story, and a ruined reputation, and a great deal of money. And you are standing in the Senate of Rome asking the Senate to believe that a banker and a senator and a clerk in the palace conspired to destroy a consular family in order to steal a house."

"Yes," Cassia said. "That is exactly what I am asking."

"Then produce your proof."

Cassia opened the satchel.

---

She produced the statement of the arsonist first.

It was read aloud by the clerk, and it named Anthus, the steward of the senator Pactus, and it said that Anthus had paid Verecundus four thousand sesterces to burn a house on the Esquiline on the night of the eleventh of March, and that he had been told the house would be empty, and that he had not known about the boy in the passage.

There was uproar. Pactus rose to his feet. The consul struck the floor.

"This is a fabrication," Pactus said. "The man is a criminal and a liar, and he has been paid by the woman to say my name."

"He has not been paid by me," Cassia said. "He has been paid by your steward. And your steward is in this chamber."

The chamber went silent.

"He is standing at the back, by the door," Cassia said. "I saw him when I came in. He has been here all morning, listening to the evidence, and he has been getting paler and paler for an hour." She turned. "Anthus. Stand up."

There was a long, terrible pause. Then, at the back of the chamber, a man in a plain tunic stood up.

He was fifty, thin, grey, with the look of a man who had spent his life in corridors, and he stood with his hands at his sides and his face the colour of clay.

"Anthus," the consul said. "Is this true?"

Anthus looked at Pactus. Pactus looked back at him, and his face was calm, and his eyes were not.

"Anthus," Pactus said. "Tell the house that you have never seen this man. Tell the house that you have never paid anyone to burn anything. Tell the house the truth."

Anthus opened his mouth. Then he closed it. Then he said, in a voice that could barely be heard, "I paid him."

The chamber erupted.

The consul struck the floor again and again, and the lictors moved in, and above the noise the emperor's secretary rose from his seat and said, in a voice that was not loud but that carried, "The house will be silent."

The chamber went silent. Every man in it turned to look at the thin grey man with the tablet.

"The emperor's household has an interest in this matter," the secretary said. "Not in the guilt or innocence of the senator Corvinus, which is for this house to decide. In the conduct of a steward who has admitted in open session to paying for the destruction of a Roman house and the death of a Roman slave. That is a crime against the peace of the city, and it will be tried, and it will be tried before this house, with the emperor's secretary present." He looked at Pactus. "The senator Pactus will be examined tomorrow on his knowledge of his steward's actions. He will not leave the city until he has been examined."

Pactus's face did not change. But he bowed, and said, "I am at the service of the house," and sat down.

The chamber erupted.

---

The consul adjourned the trial to the following day. The guards took Anthus into custody. Pactus was escorted from the chamber by two lictors, and he walked out with his head up and his toga straight and his face entirely composed, and the crowd outside, when they saw him, began to shout.

Cassia came out of the Curia into the late afternoon with Tertulla on one side and Bathyllus on the other, and the crowd pressed in on her, and this time it did not shout. It made a sound she had never heard before, a low, rising, hungry sound, and it took her a moment to understand that it was cheering.

"Say something," Tertulla said.

"No."

"Say something. They will remember it."

Cassia stopped on the steps. The crowd went quiet, or as quiet as a crowd of ten thousand people can go.

"I am not going to make a speech," she said. "I am a freedwoman and I have been on trial in this city for six months, and I am tired, and I want to go home." She paused. "But I will say one thing. A boy died in my house in March. His name was Nolus. He was eleven years old and he fed the pigeons and he was in a passage with a bolted door. The men who paid for that fire are going to be tried tomorrow, and I am going to be in that chamber, and I am going to watch." She looked at the crowd. "If you want to cheer for something, cheer for him. He is the only person in this whole affair who did nothing wrong."

Before she went out, she found Corvinus in the corridor behind the chamber, alone but for one lictor, with his back against the wall.

He looked exhausted. His toga was crumpled and his hair was out of place and there was a mark on his cheek where he had been pressing his hand.

"You spoke well," she said.

"I spoke the truth. It is easier than speaking well." He looked at her. "Tomorrow they will come for me with everything they have. Pactus will take the prosecution himself; he cannot afford not to. He will say that I killed my father and that you are my whore and that the child was a fiction invented to win sympathy."

"Then we will answer him."

"With what?"

"With the truth," Cassia said, "and with the arsonist, and with the steward, and with a letter from a clerk in the palace that I have not yet produced." She looked at him. "I am saving it. A man like Pactus will hang himself if you give him enough rope, and tomorrow he will have a great deal of rope."

Corvinus looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed — a tired, surprised laugh — and said, "You are enjoying this."

"No," Cassia said. "I am not enjoying it. I am good at it. Those are different things, and it has taken me forty-two years to learn the difference."

She went down the steps and into the crowd, and the crowd opened for her, and the sound that followed her was not cheering any more. It was something older and stranger, and it followed her all the way to the edge of the Forum.

---

At home, the house was in an uproar. The household had heard the news before she got there — the city was like that — and they were in the atrium, all of them, and Thais was crying, and Celer was shouting, and Sabina was standing on the impluvium with her arms out.

Cassia stood in the doorway and looked at them.

"Tomorrow," she said, "is the second day. It will be worse than today. There will be more shouting, and more lies, and at some point a man will say something about me that is true and that I would rather not have said in public. And then it will be over, and we will come home, and we will eat something, and we will sleep." She paused. "And then, the day after, we are going to have a proper funeral for Nolus. Not the one we had in March. A proper one, with a stone with his name on it and a priest and a feast, and everyone in this house will be there."

Thais wiped her face. "Domina. The stone will cost money."

"Let it cost money," Cassia said. "We have a great deal of money, and we have just discovered what it is for."

She went into the tablinum and sat down and put her hands on the desk and closed her eyes. And then she opened them and took out a clean sheet of paper and began to write the speech she would make the next day, because she had learned a long time ago that the only way to survive a thing like this was to prepare for it as though it were an account.
