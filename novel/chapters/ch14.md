The games were given in April, in the new amphitheatre in the Campus Martius, and they were given by the emperor in honour of the dedication of the forum, which had been dedicated in January, and which no one in the crowd remembered by April, because a crowd remembers only the last spectacle and the next one.

Cassia had a seat in the second tier, on the women's side, with Tertulla and Thais and a cushion she had brought from home because the stone was cold in the morning. She had been to the games a hundred times. She had watched men kill each other in the morning and beasts kill each other at noon and men kill beasts in the afternoon, and she had learned, over the years, to watch the crowd instead of the sand, because the crowd was the real spectacle, and because a woman who understands a crowd can predict a great deal about a city.

The Sulpicii had a box on the senatorial side, near the front, with the other consular families. Corvinus was there in his white toga with the purple border, and Aemilia beside him in a dress the colour of wine, and Sulpicia between them, and Publius behind, and the whole box was a picture of a family that had nothing to hide. Cassia looked at it once and then looked away, and did not look at it again for a long time, because she had discovered that looking at a thing you cannot have is a way of torturing yourself, and she had had enough of that.

Pactus was three boxes along, with his wife and his brother and a group of clients, and he was laughing at something, and his laugh carried across the stone.

"He is very cheerful," Tertulla said.

"He has reason to be."

"Has he?"

"Felix was arrested this morning."

Tertulla turned her head. "What?"

"On a charge of calumny. The urban prefect's men took him from his house at dawn and sealed the door. His wife and children are with his sister on the Aventine." Cassia watched the sand. "Sittius's lawyer laid the information. They are calling it an extortion, a false accusation laid for money, and they have a witness who will swear that Felix demanded three hundred thousand sesterces from the senator Corvinus and threatened him with a prosecution if he did not pay."

"That is true."

"Yes. It is entirely true. That is the beauty of it." Cassia turned her cup in her hands. "A true thing can be used to destroy a man just as easily as a false one. They are not prosecuting him for the accusation. They are prosecuting him for the extortion. And once he is convicted of extortion, his evidence against them is worthless, because a convicted delator cannot be a witness, and the whole case against Corvinus falls apart — and with it, everything he knows about Sittius and Pactus and the money."

Tertulla was silent for a moment. "Then we have lost."

"We have lost our witness. We have not lost the case." Cassia looked at the sand. "I have the midwife's statement, and the receipt, and the notary's seal. I have the banker's own words, though I cannot prove them. And I have something else."

"What?"

"The truth," Cassia said. "It is not worth much in a courtroom. But it is worth something in a city."

The trumpets sounded. The crowd rose. The emperor came into his box with his guard behind him, and the whole amphitheatre stood and cheered, and the sound was like a wave breaking over stones, and Cassia stood with the rest and cheered too, because she was a woman who had learned that a person who does not cheer in a crowd is a person whom the crowd remembers.

"They have announced the betrothal," Tertulla said, quietly.

Cassia did not look at the box. "To whom?"

"Aulus Proculus. The herald read it out before the first fight, with the other notices. The marriage will be in June, and the dowry is the vineyard in Etruria, which is mortgaged to Sittius, and the bridegroom's father is to pay the debts." Tertulla's mouth twisted. "It is a very good match for a family with no money and a scandal coming. It is a very bad match for a girl of fifteen with a mind of her own."

Cassia looked at the box then, and at the girl in the wine-coloured dress sitting between her parents with her hands folded and her face perfectly composed, and thought about a wine shop in the Subura and a girl in a blue cloak who had wanted to know what she was marrying into.

"She knows," Tertulla said. "Look at her. She is sitting there like a Vestal at a funeral, and she is fifteen, and she knows exactly what her grandmother has sold her for."

"Then she will survive it."

"Will she?"

"Yes," Cassia said. "She has the hardness. You can see it in the way she holds her shoulders." She looked away. "It is not a good thing to be hard at fifteen. But it is better than the alternative."

The games began. In the morning there were the hunters, men with spears and nets against lions and bears, and a bull that went mad and had to be killed by six men with spears, and a leopard that escaped its handlers and ran around the arena with the crowd screaming with laughter until it was caught in a net. At noon there was an execution: three men condemned for treason, thrown to the beasts without weapons, and the crowd watched with the particular attention it reserved for death without skill. One of the men fought; the other two stood and waited, and the beasts took them quickly, and the sand was raked over, and the crowd called for the afternoon.

At the midday break a woman came down the steps of the women's gallery and sat beside Cassia without asking permission.

She was fifty, dressed in good but not expensive wool, with a plain face and red eyes and hands that would not keep still in her lap. Cassia knew her by sight; everyone in the second tier knew her by sight, because she was the wife of a man who had been a magistrate and had lost everything in a prosecution fifteen years before, and because she had spent the years since then doing penance in public.

"Domina," the woman said. "I am Junia. My husband was Lucius Junius, who was praetor in the year of the consulship of Silanus."

"I remember."

"Then you know what happened to us."

"I know that your husband was accused and that he lost his property and that he died the year after." Cassia turned. "What do you want?"

"I want to tell you something." Junia's hands twisted. "My husband was accused by Felix. Felix built the case out of nothing — a letter, a clerk, a slave who was promised his freedom. My husband was innocent. He was acquitted of everything but a technicality, and the technicality ruined him, and the state took the house, and the man who bought the house at a fraction of its value was a banker on the Via Lata." She looked at Cassia. "The same banker, domina. I have been watching him for fifteen years. I have been watching all of them."

Cassia was very still.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because my son is a clerk in the office of the urban prefect, and he told me last night that Felix is to be charged with extortion, and that the charge was laid by the same banker's lawyer, and that the whole thing is a trap." Junia's eyes were bright and hard. "And because I have hated that man for fifteen years and I have never had the power to do anything about it, and now there is a woman in this city who does."

"What can you give me?"

"Names. Dates. The deeds of the house he bought. The testimony of a clerk who is still alive and still frightened." Junia leaned closer. "And one thing more. Fifteen years ago, when Felix built his case against my husband, he used a forger. A man named Secundus, who lives now in a village near Fidenae, and who is dying of a disease of the lungs, and who told me last year, when he thought he was dying, that he had done it, and that he was sorry, and that he would swear to it in a court if anyone would listen."

Cassia looked at the woman beside her, and at the crowd, and at the sand where the beasts had died, and she felt the shape of the whole thing come together in her mind like a key turning in a lock.

"Write it down," she said. "Everything. Names, dates, the deed, the forger. Bring it to my house tonight, by the garden door, and I will pay you for your trouble, and your son will not be named in any of it."

Junia's face worked. "I do not want money."

"Everyone wants money. It is the only thing in Rome that everyone wants." Cassia put her hand on the woman's wrist. "Take the money. It will make you feel less like a suppliant, and it will make me feel less like a benefactor, and we will both be able to look at each other afterwards."

In the afternoon there were the gladiators.

Cassia had seen a thousand gladiators. She had seen the famous ones and the forgettable ones and the ones who died badly and the ones who died well. She had seen the crowd turn on a man who refused to die and the crowd turn on a man who killed too quickly, and she had learned that a crowd at the games is not a crowd of murderers; it is a crowd of people who have been told that this is the place where death is allowed, and who are discovering, together, what they are capable of.

The day's favourite was a retiarius named Bato, a big man with a net and a trident and a following of women who screamed his name, and he fought a murmillo called Niger who had been brought from Gaul and had a helmet with a fish on it. They fought for a long time. Bato was faster. Niger was stronger. The crowd began to shout, and the shouting turned into a rhythm, and the rhythm turned into a single voice, and the voice wanted blood.

Bato caught Niger in the net and knocked him down and stood over him with the trident at his throat, and the crowd screamed, and the referee looked to the emperor, and the emperor, who was tired, made the gesture that meant mercy, and the crowd groaned, and Bato lifted the trident and stepped back and offered his hand, and Niger, who was bleeding from the shoulder and the leg, took it, and the two men walked off the sand together to a storm of shouting and a great deal of thrown money.

"Mercy," Tertulla said. "There is a novelty."

"The emperor is in a good mood," Cassia said. "He has a forum and a bridge and a war coming in the East. He can afford to be merciful in April."

"He will not be merciful in May."

The fourth fight was between two men who had been condemned to the arena for crimes and had been given swords and no training, and it was the kind of fight that the crowd loved and that Cassia could not watch. One of them was killed within a minute. The other stood over the body with the sword in his hand and his face turned up to the crowd, and the crowd screamed at him, and he did not move, and after a long moment the referee took him by the arm and led him off the sand, and he went like a man in a dream.

"He will not live the year," Tertulla said.

"Who?"

"The one who won. They always kill themselves, the ones who are not gladiators. They cannot go back to whatever they were, and they cannot stay in the arena, and there is nothing in between." She looked at the sand. "It is a very Roman thing, to give a man a sword and then take away every reason to use it."

Cassia said nothing. She was thinking about a boy on the Via Ostiensis with clay on his arms, and about the fact that he had a trade and a father and a laugh that carried into the street, and about how thin the line was between a life like that and a life like the man on the sand.

"No," Cassia said. "He will not."

---

Between the third and fourth fights there was an interval, and the crowd stood and stretched and went to the food sellers and the latrines and the fountains, and Cassia went out into the corridor behind the women's gallery to get away from the noise.

Pactus was there.

He was standing by a column with a cup of wine in his hand and two of his clients at a distance, and he was looking at her as though he had known she would come out into that corridor at that moment. He probably had. He was a man who arranged things.

"Domina," he said. "A word."

"I have no words for you."

"Then listen to mine." He came closer. "Felix is in a cell under the prefect's house, and tomorrow he will be charged, and in a month he will be convicted, and after that he will be a man with a brand on his record and no one will ever believe a word he says again. Your witness is gone. Your case is gone. The only thing you have left is a midwife and a piece of paper, and a midwife can be discredited and a piece of paper can be called a forgery." He drank his wine. "I am offering you a way out."

"There is no way out of a hole you dug."

"I did not dig it. Your senator's father dug it, twenty-four years ago, when he put a child in a slave and his wife put a cup in his wine." Pactus's face was pleasant. "I know about the cup, domina. I have known for a month. I have a statement from the physician's assistant that says the old man's death was not natural, and I have another from the steward that says the wife was in the room, and I have been saving them, because a good prosecutor does not show his hand until the last moment."

Cassia's face did not change. "Then why are you offering me a way out?"

"Because I do not want to destroy the Sulpicii. I want the Asian command. There is a difference." He set his cup down on the ledge. "Withdraw the midwife. Seal the receipt. Let the accusation die for lack of evidence, and I will let the senator retire from public life with his name and his house and his family, and I will take the province, and everyone will be satisfied. The alternative is a trial. And in a trial, everything comes out. The cup. The child. The brothel. The boy on the Via Ostiensis." He smiled. "I have not been to see him. I have been saving him, as your friend Felix was saving him. But I will go, if I have to. And when I have finished with him, he will not be a potter any more. He will be the bastard of a senator and a whore, and every man in Rome will know it, and he will have to leave the city to escape it."

Cassia looked at him. She was aware, in a distant way, that her hands were cold and that her heart was going very fast and that the noise of the crowd in the corridor was like the noise of the sea.

"Say his name," she said.

"What?"

"Say his name. You have been talking about him for a minute. Say his name."

Pactus's smile did not change. "I do not know his name."

"Then you do not know him, and you cannot destroy him, and you have just told me that you are bluffing." Cassia stepped closer to him. "You are a new man, Pactus. You have spent your whole life being afraid that someone would find out that your grandfather was a farmer and your father was a tax collector. You have borrowed money you cannot repay and made promises you cannot keep, and you have hired a delator and a banker and an arsonist, and now you are standing in a corridor at the games telling a woman that you will destroy a boy you have never met." She smiled. "I have known men like you all my life. You are not dangerous. You are frightened. And frightened men make mistakes."

Pactus's face changed. It was a small change, a tightening around the mouth, and it was the first honest thing she had seen on it.

"You will regret this," he said.

"I have regretted worse," Cassia said, and walked away.

---

She came back to her seat and the games went on, and she did not see any of it. She sat with her hands in her lap and her eyes on the sand and her mind on the boy, and after a while Tertulla leaned over and said, "You are pale. What did he say?"

"He threatened the boy."

"Then we go to the boy."

"We cannot. If we go to him, we make him visible. If we are seen at that shop, every man in Rome will know who he is by the Ides." Cassia looked at the sand. "We need another way."

"What way?"

"I do not know yet." She stood. "I am going home. Tell Thais to meet me there. Tell Hermeros to bring the black book and the household accounts and every letter I have ever received from a man in the Senate. And tell Bathyllus to find the boy's father — the potter, Gaius Vibidius — and bring him to the house tonight, by the garden door, and tell him nothing except that a lady wishes to speak to him about his son."

"His son?"

"His son," Cassia said. "The boy is twenty-four years old and his father is a potter who loves him, and if anyone is going to protect him from the men who want to use him, it will not be me. It will be the man who raised him."

She went out through the dark corridor and down the stairs into the street, and the roar of the crowd followed her out into the daylight, and behind her, in the amphitheatre, a man was dying on the sand for the pleasure of fifty thousand people who would forget him by supper.

---

Felix's wife came at the seventh hour.

She came to the garden door with her two children and a bundle and no slave, and Bathyllus came to the tablinum with a face like a man who has been asked to choose between two duties.

"Domina. There is a woman at the door. She says she is the wife of the man Felix."

Cassia put down her pen. "Bring her in."

She was younger than Cassia had expected — thirty, perhaps, with the worn prettiness of a woman who has been pretty and has had no time for it lately — and she came into the tablinum with a child on either side and her chin up and her eyes red.

"You are the woman my husband made the agreement with," she said.

"I am."

"He is in a cell under the prefect's house. They will not let me see him. They have sealed the door of our house and taken everything, and my sister will not have us any longer than a week, and I have no money and no one to turn to." Her voice was steady. "He told me, before they took him, that if anything happened I was to come to you. He said you were the only person in Rome who had ever kept a promise to him. He said you had signed a paper with your seal on it, and that the paper said his children would never want."

Cassia looked at the two children. The boy was nine, with his father's sharp face and his mother's eyes. The girl was six, and she was holding her mother's hand and staring at the three Graces on the wall.

"Show me the paper," Cassia said.

The woman took it from her sleeve. It was the agreement, sealed with Cassia's ring, and it was creased and soft from being carried against the body.

Cassia read it, and then she folded it and gave it back.

"Thais," she said. "Take the lady and the children to the room at the top of the stairs. The one with the window over the garden. Have Narcissus look at them. Have Celer make something hot. They are to stay until I say otherwise, and no one is to know they are here."

The woman stared at her. "You are giving us the room at the top of the stairs?"

"Yes."

"I thought —" She stopped. "I thought you would give us money and send us away."

"I thought so too," Cassia said. "It seems I am not the woman I was."

The potter came at the ninth hour, alone, with clay still under his fingernails and the look of a man who has been told that a lady wishes to speak to him about his son and has spent the afternoon imagining the worst.

Gaius Vibidius was fifty and broad and grey and entirely without pretension. He had a good, plain face, the face of a man who works with his hands and sleeps well, and he stood in the tablinum of the House of the Graces with his cap in his hands and did not look at the silver or the paintings or the three Graces in the alcove.

"Domina," he said. "What has my boy done?"

"Nothing," Cassia said. "Sit down. There is wine, and there is a great deal to tell you, and none of it is his fault."

He sat. He did not drink the wine. And Cassia told him.

She told him about the villa at Tibur, and the girl in the kitchen, and the son who was born in a cell, and the woman who took him out of the room. She told him about the receipt, and the four hundred sesterces, and the dealer named Caepio, and the twenty-four years. She did not tell him everything. She did not tell him about the cup, or the trial, or the men who wanted to use the boy to destroy a senator. She told him enough.

The potter listened without moving. When she had finished, he sat for a long time with his hands on his knees.

"I bought him," he said at last. "From a man in the Forum. My wife could not have children, and we had prayed, and we had gone to the temple, and a man told us there was a child to be had, and we paid for him, and we took him home." He looked up. "I did not know he was stolen. I want you to understand that. I thought he was a foundling, or the child of a woman who could not keep him. I did not know he was taken from his mother."

"He was not stolen from me," Cassia said. "He was taken. There is a difference, and I do not hold you responsible for it."

"He is my son," the potter said.

"I know."

"I have raised him for twenty-four years. I taught him the wheel and the kiln and the price of clay. He calls me father." His voice was steady, and his hands were not. "I am not going to give him up because a lady in a big house has decided that she wants him back."

"I am not asking you to give him up." Cassia leaned forward. "I am asking you to protect him. There are men in this city who want to use him to destroy a senator, and if they cannot use him they may hurt him, and I cannot protect him from the inside of my own house because I am the woman they are trying to destroy. You can. You are his father. You can take him out of the city for a month, to the country, to a workshop somewhere, and you can keep him safe until this is over."

The potter looked at her. "And after it is over?"

"After it is over," Cassia said, "he can decide for himself whether he wants to know me. I will not come to your shop. I will not send for him. I will not tell him who I am. That is his to decide, when he is ready, and if he is never ready, then I will live with it." She paused. "But I would like to know that he is alive. That is all. A message, once a year, from you, to say that he is alive."

Gaius Vibidius looked at her for a long time. Then he picked up the cup of wine and drank it off, and set it down, and said, "You are not what I expected."

"What did you expect?"

"I do not know. A lady." He wiped his mouth. "He looks like you, you know. Around the eyes. I have seen it for years and I never knew what it was, and now I know." He stood. "I will take him to my brother's workshop at Praeneste. He will not like it; he has a girl in Ostia and he will complain about the clay. But he will go, because he is a good boy, and because I will tell him that the workshop needs him." He put on his cap. "And domina. When this is over. If he wants to know you. I will not stand in his way."

Cassia stood. "Thank you."

"Do not thank me. I have not done anything yet." He went to the door. "But I will tell you one thing. If you had come to my shop twenty-four years ago and asked for him, I would have given him to you. I would have hated it. But I would have given him to you, because he was yours before he was mine." He went out.

Cassia stood in the tablinum for a long time. Then she sat down and put her head in her hands, and Thais came in and stood beside her, and the house was quiet.

"The hearing is set," Thais said. "The consul's clerk sent word. The sixth day before the Kalends of July."

Cassia looked up. "Then we have three weeks."

"Three weeks," Thais said, "and one witness in a cell, and a banker with a lawyer, and a senator with a dying mother and a burnt house and a boy he has never met."

"Yes," Cassia said. "Three weeks."

There was a letter from the Palatine on the table, in Corvinus's hand.

*The notary has been again. My mother has given the second paper. She dictated it in the morning and signed it in the afternoon and then she slept for six hours, which the physician says is a bad sign. She has asked for you. She says she has something else to tell you and that she will not tell it to me. I do not know what else there is. I am beginning to think that I have never known my mother at all.*

*The hearing is set for the sixth day before the Kalends of July. Pactus has been seen at the palace twice. Sittius has left the city for his villa at Baiae, which means he intends to be far away when it happens, and that he expects it to happen.*

*I am not afraid of the trial. I am afraid of what will be left of us afterwards. I have spent my whole life being a Sulpicius. I do not know how to be anything else.*

*L.*

Cassia read it twice. Then she folded it and put it in the drawer with the other letter, the one that had been in the cedar box for twenty-four years, and she sat for a moment with her hand on the drawer.

Then she took out a clean sheet of paper and wrote:

*You will learn. It is not as difficult as it looks. You begin by putting one foot in front of the other and doing the next necessary thing, and you do it again the next day, and after a while you find that you have become someone else, and that the someone else is not so bad.*

*I will come to the Palatine tomorrow. Tell your mother to save whatever she has to say. I have a great deal to do and very little time, and I am not going to spend it listening to an old woman's riddles.*

*C.*

She sanded the letter and sealed it and gave it to Bathyllus, and then she turned back to the accounts and worked until the lamps burned low.
