The palace on the Palatine was not one building. It was a hill covered with buildings, added to by every emperor for a hundred years, and by the reign of Trajan it had become a city inside the city: halls and courtyards and gardens and bathhouses and barracks and offices, connected by corridors and stairways and passages so complicated that men who had lived there all their lives still got lost in it.

Cassia had never been inside it. She had stood outside the gates on festival days, and she had seen the crowds go in and out, and she had known a dozen men who came and went through those doors every week, and she had never once been inside, because there was no reason for a woman like her to be there, and because a woman like her did not go where she was not wanted.

She went in through the gate on the Via Sacra with the empress's letter in her hand and a steward in front of her and two guards behind, and she walked through corridors of marble and porphyry and gilt with her heart going like a drum and her face as still as a painted wall.

The palace smelled of incense and wax and hot stone. There were slaves everywhere, silent, moving in and out of doorways with trays and papers and messages. There were soldiers of the guard at every turning, standing in their scale armour with their hands on their spears. There were clerks in little rooms off the corridors, bent over desks, and the sound of their pens was like the sound of insects. And there were works of art, more of them than Cassia had ever seen in one place: statues and paintings and mosaics and tables of coloured marble, and ceilings of gilded bronze, and a fountain in a courtyard that was made of a single block of porphyry and had been brought from Egypt at a cost that could not be counted.

She thought, walking through it: I have spent twenty-four years building a house that would make men lower their voices, and this is what the men who own the world live in. And then she thought: and they are just as frightened as I am. Every one of them. You can see it in their faces.

The steward stopped at a door at the end of a long corridor and said, "The empress will see you in the garden room. You will not speak until you are spoken to. You will not sit until you are told to sit. You will not touch anything. You will keep your eyes down." He looked at her. "And domina. She is not like the other one. She does not want flattery and she does not want cleverness. She wants the truth, and she has very little patience with people who take a long time to get to it."

"Then we will get on very well," Cassia said.

She waited for an hour in a room off the corridor with a bench, a fountain, and a view of a courtyard where the guard was drilling.

There was one other person waiting: a Greek of about seventy, in a clean but threadbare cloak, with a scroll case under his arm and the patient, faintly amused expression of a man who had been waiting in palaces for fifty years.

"You are here to see the empress," he said.

"I am."

"Then you will wait an hour. She is reading in the garden and she does not like to be interrupted before the fourth hour." He shifted on the bench. "I am here to see the emperor's secretary. I have been here every day for a month. I am a teacher of philosophy, and I have written a book about the education of princes, and I have been told by three different freedmen that it is excellent and by none of them that it will be read."

"I am sorry," Cassia said.

"Do not be. It is an old story." The philosopher looked at her. "You are the woman with the house on the Esquiline."

"I am."

"I have heard of you. I have heard that you keep a good table and a dangerous book and that half the Senate owes you money." He smiled. "I have also heard that you are being destroyed by a delator, and that the whole city is watching to see whether you will survive it. It is the most interesting thing in Rome this month. More interesting than the games."

"And what do you think will happen?"

"I think you will survive," the philosopher said. "You have the look of a survivor. I have seen it in soldiers and in slaves and in one or two philosophers. It is not courage. It is a kind of stubbornness that has been beaten into a person until it becomes a virtue." He stood, because a steward had appeared at the end of the corridor. "When this is over, domina, and you are rich and safe and bored, send for me. I will come and dine at your table and tell you about the education of princes, and you will tell me about the education of a woman in Puteoli, and we will both learn something."

"What is your name?"

"Epictetus," he said, and went out.

The garden room was small, by the standards of the palace, and it was open on one side to a courtyard with a fountain and a fig tree and a great many roses. The empress Pompeia Plotina was sitting in a chair by the open side with a book on her knee and a slave behind her with a fan. She was perhaps sixty, with grey hair drawn back plainly, and a long, grave, intelligent face, and she was dressed in a plain stola of undyed wool with no jewellery at all.

She looked up when Cassia came in and looked at her for a long moment without saying anything.

"Domina," Cassia said, and bowed her head.

"Sit down," the empress said. "You have been standing in corridors for an hour and you are not as young as you were."

Cassia sat. The empress closed her book and put it on the table beside her.

"You have brought the letter," she said.

"I have."

"Give it to me."

Cassia took the letter from her sleeve and handed it over. The empress unfolded it and read it, slowly, once, and then again. Her face did not change. When she had finished she folded it and put it on the table and sat for a moment with her hands in her lap.

"This is my husband's money," she said. "This is the grain of Egypt and the taxes of Gaul and the rents of the imperial estates. It has been stolen by a clerk and shared with a senator and hidden in a bank on the Via Lata, and the clerk is now the man who counts the money for the man who owns the world." She looked up. "Do you know what happens to a woman like me who brings this to the emperor?"

"I do not."

"He will be angry. He will be angry with the clerk, and he will be angry with the senator, and he will be angry with me for telling him, because I will have made him look a fool, and a man who has been made to look a fool does not thank the person who showed him the mirror." Plotina's mouth moved. "I have been married to him for thirty years. I know exactly how angry he will be, and I know exactly how long it will last, and I know that it will be worth it."

"Then you will help me."

"I will help you," the empress said, "but first I will tell you something, because you are going to hear it from someone else if I do not, and because I would rather you heard it from me." She set the letter down. "The emperor is not well. He has not been well since the Dacian war. His feet swell and his breath is short and he cannot sleep, and the physicians say it is the dropsy and the physicians say a great many other things that they do not say in front of him. He is fifty-nine years old. He has been emperor for fifteen years, and he has spent them in the saddle, and there is not much of him left."

Cassia said nothing.

"When he dies," Plotina said, "there will be a question about who follows him. There is a man in the East, a general, who has been winning battles and making friends, and there is a boy in the palace who is clever and charming and who has been waiting for twenty years for someone to die, and there is the Senate, which would like to choose one of its own and knows that it will not be allowed to." She looked at Cassia. "In the year after the emperor dies, every man who has a secret will be in danger. Every clerk, every senator, every freedman, every woman with a house and a book. The men who survive will be the ones who have made themselves useful to the right people and dangerous to the wrong ones, and who have not made a spectacle of themselves."

"Then you are telling me to be quiet."

"I am telling you to be careful," the empress said. "A trial in the Senate with the whole city watching is a very loud thing. It will make you famous, and fame in this city is a kind of debt, and the interest is collected by whoever is in power when it comes due." She leaned forward. "I can protect you for a year. I cannot protect you for ten. So take the trial, and win it, and then go home and be dull and rich and keep your household safe, and let the men in the palace destroy each other in their own time."

Cassia looked at her. In the courtyard the fountain played, and the roses moved, and somewhere beyond the wall a soldier was shouting a command.

"You are telling me to survive," she said.

"Yes."

"Everyone tells me to survive. My steward tells me to survive. My friend tells me to survive. The old woman who sold my son tells me that she did it so that I would survive." Cassia shook her head. "I have survived for twenty-four years. I have survived Puteoli and the fire and the questioning and the whole of it, and I am beginning to think that survival is a very small ambition."

The empress looked at her for a long moment.

"What would you have instead?"

"I would have justice," Cassia said. "Not for me. For the gardener who died in a cell because a banker wanted a house. For the child who was put on a rope because a delator wanted a fee. For the boy on the Via Ostiensis who has been hidden and hunted and used as a threat for six months, and who does not even know why." She stopped. "I have spent my whole life being sensible. You are asking me to be sensible again, and I will do it, because you are right and because I have a household and a house and a business and I cannot afford to be a hero. But I want you to know what you are asking."

The empress was silent. Then she said, "I know exactly what I am asking. I have been asking it of myself for thirty years." She picked up her book again. "You will have your arsonists. You will have your trial. And when it is over, you will send me a list of the men who burned your house and killed your boy, and I will make sure that they are not forgotten in whatever quiet arrangement is made about the rest." She looked up. "That is the most I can do. It is more than most women in this palace get."

"Then you will help me."

"I will help you," the empress said, "because it is my husband's money and because I do not like thieves, and because a man who has been robbing the emperor for twenty years is a danger to the empire, and because I am bored." She picked up the letter again. "But I will not do it your way. You want a trial in the Senate, with the whole city watching and the whole story told. That is a woman's idea of justice. It is not how these things are done."

"Then how are they done?"

"Quietly." Plotina set the letter down. "The clerk will be removed from his office. He will be given a house in the country and a pension and a very clear understanding that if he ever returns to Rome he will be prosecuted for fraud. The senator Pactus will be told that his patron has fallen and that he would be wise to withdraw his accusation and retire to his estates. The banker Sittius will be investigated by the prefect of the grain supply and will pay a fine that will ruin him. And your senator will be acquitted, and his household will be released, and the boy will be left alone, and no one will ever know why."

"And the truth?"

"The truth will be known to the people who need to know it." The empress looked at her. "You are a woman who has spent her life in a house where men tell you things they would not tell their wives. You know that the truth is not a thing that is announced. It is a thing that is used."

Cassia sat very still. The fountain played in the courtyard. The roses moved in the warm wind.

"And if I refuse?" she said. "If I insist on the trial?"

"Then you will have your trial," Plotina said, "and you will probably win, because you have a letter and a receipt and a midwife and a dead gardener, and because the Senate loves nothing more than the destruction of a man who has risen too fast. And when you have won, the clerk will be executed, and the senator Pactus will be exiled, and the banker will hang himself in his bath, and the emperor will be humiliated in front of the whole city, and he will remember that it was a freedwoman with a grudge who did it to him." She paused. "And then, in a year or two, when the scandal has been forgotten, he will find a reason to destroy you. He will not do it himself. He will find a magistrate who owes him a favour, and there will be a charge, and your house will be seized, and your household will be sold, and the boy on the Via Ostiensis will be a slave before he is thirty. That is what happens to people who win too publicly in Rome."

Cassia looked at the empress. She had spent her whole life believing that the truth was a weapon, and here was a woman who had spent her whole life in the palace telling her that the truth was a tool, and that the difference between the two was the difference between a soldier and a surgeon.

"What do you want me to do?" she said.

"I want you to give the trial to the Senate," Plotina said, "with the emperor's secretary in the chamber and the emperor's name kept out of it. I want the clerk Primus to be tried and condemned for fraud, and I want it done in public, because a public condemnation is a warning to every other clerk in the palace. And I want the senator Pactus to be ruined by his own accusation, in front of the men he has spent his life trying to impress." She leaned forward. "I do not want a quiet little arrangement in a back room. I want a spectacle. But I want it to be a spectacle about the theft of the emperor's money, and not about the private life of a senator and a freedwoman and a boy on the Via Ostiensis."

Cassia understood. She understood entirely, and she felt the shape of the bargain close around her like a hand.

"You will give me the trial," she said, "and in exchange I will keep the boy out of it."

"Yes."

"And the midwife?"

"The midwife will testify to the child, because the child is the foundation of the accusation, and without the child there is no case. But she will testify in a closed session, and her evidence will be sealed, and the receipt will be read to the Senate and then sealed and put in the archive, and the name of the potter and the name of the boy will not be spoken aloud." Plotina picked up her book. "That is the bargain. It is not perfect. It is the best that can be done in this city by a woman in your position, and I am offering it to you because I have been in this palace for thirty years and I have never once been able to help a woman like you, and because I am tired of watching clever women destroyed by stupid men."

Cassia sat for a moment. Then she said, "I have one condition."

"Name it."

"The men who burned my house and killed a child will not be part of the quiet arrangement. The arsonist has given a statement, and the steward who paid him has been named. I want them tried and punished, in public, before the trial of the senator. I want the city to know that a boy died in my house and that the men who did it were not important enough to be protected."

The empress looked at her for a long moment. Then she said, "That is a very Roman condition."

"I am a very Roman woman."

"Yes," Plotina said. "I can see that." She stood. "You will have your arsonists. And you will have your trial. And now you will go home, domina, and you will tell no one where you have been, and you will wait for a message from my steward. It will come within three days."

Cassia stood. At the door she turned and said, "Domina. May I ask you something?"

"You may."

"Why are you doing this? Not the fraud. The other thing. The boy. You have no reason to care about a potter on the Via Ostiensis."

The empress looked at her. In the light from the courtyard, with the roses behind her and the book in her hand, she looked like a woman who had been beautiful once and had put it away, and who had spent thirty years learning to be useful instead.

"I had a child," she said. "A girl. She lived three days. It was a long time ago, and the emperor does not speak of it, and neither do I." She opened her book. "I have spent my life in this palace being sensible. It is a great comfort to be unreasonable once."

---

She was halfway down the corridor when a man stepped out of a doorway in front of her.

He was sixty, small, neat, with the hands of a clerk and the face of a man who had not been refused anything in twenty years. He wore a plain tunic of excellent wool and no jewellery, and he stood in the middle of the corridor with his hands clasped behind his back and said, "Domina."

Cassia stopped. The steward behind her had stopped too, and had gone very still.

"You are Primus," she said.

"I am." He inclined his head. "I have been watching your career with great interest."

"And I have been watching yours."

"I doubt that. No one watches a clerk." He smiled. "I have a proposal for you."

"I have had a great many proposals this month."

"This one is simple. You have a letter. I have a great deal of money. You give me the letter, and I give you three million sesterces and a house in Campania and a guarantee that no one in your household will ever be questioned again." He said it in the tone of a man offering a seat on a bench. "It is a good offer. It is more money than you will make in ten years of senators."

"It is a good offer," Cassia said. "But I have just spent an hour with the empress, and she has offered me something better."

The clerk's face did not change, but something behind it did — a small movement, like a fish under ice.

"The empress," he said, "is a woman with a book and no power."

"The empress," Cassia said, "is the wife of the man whose money you have been stealing for twenty years, and she is very bored, and she has just decided to be unreasonable." She stepped around him. "You should have taken the house in the country when you had the chance, Primus. It is a very pleasant house. I have seen it from the road."

She walked on down the corridor. Behind her, the clerk stood without moving, and the steward followed her, and the guards followed the steward, and the whole palace went on around them with its incense and its marble and its clerks and its silence.

At the gate on the Via Sacra, Bathyllus was waiting with his face grey.

"Domina," he said. "The household has been released. All of them. They came back an hour ago."

Cassia stopped. "All of them?"

"All of them. The prefect's clerk came himself with a paper and a cart, and he would not say why, and he would not look at anyone." Bathyllus took her arm. "And domina. There is a message from the Palatine. The old woman is dying. The senator says to come at once."

Cassia stood in the street with the sun on her face and the letter in her sleeve and the smell of the palace still in her clothes.

"Take me home first," she said. "I want to see them."

"Domina—"

"Take me home, Bathyllus. I have been in the palace for an hour and I have not seen my household in nine days and I am going to see them before I go anywhere else."

On the way home Bathyllus told her what had happened at the prefect's gate.

"The clerk came out himself," he said. "He had a paper in his hand and he read it twice, and then he told the guards to open the cells, and they brought them out one by one. He would not say who had sent the order. He would not say anything at all. He looked like a man who had been told that the ground under his feet was not as solid as he had thought."

"Was there a man with him? A small man, neat, in a good cloak?"

"There was a man in the courtyard who did not come forward. He stood by the fig tree with his hands behind his back and watched them come out, and when they were all out he turned and walked away." Bathyllus paused. "I have seen him before, domina. He was at the Curia on the day of the hearing. He was standing behind Pactus."

Cassia looked out of the litter at the streets going past.

"He was counting them," she said. "He was making sure that all twelve came out. He wanted to know that the order had been obeyed."

"Eleven," Bathyllus said. "Eleven came out."

"Yes," Cassia said. "Eleven."

He took her home. They were all in the atrium, all eleven of them, with Thais in the middle and Sabina holding her hand, and when Cassia came through the door they did not cheer and they did not weep; they stood in a line and looked at her, and Thais said, "There you are," and Cassia said, "Here I am," and then she went around the line and put her hand on each of their heads, one by one, like a woman counting her stock.

Then she went to the Palatine.

---

The house on the Palatine was full of lamps and quiet people.

They had put the sedan chair down in the street and Cassia had walked up the hill on foot, because the litter could not get through the crowd of servants and clients and neighbours who had gathered outside the door in the way that Romans gather outside a house where someone is dying, and by the time she reached the atrium she had been recognized three times and had said nothing to anyone.

Aemilia met her at the foot of the stairs. She was in a plain dress with her hair down and no jewellery, and she looked as though she had been awake for two days.

"She has had a turn," she said. "The physician says it is the end. He says she may last the night or she may last an hour, and he says there is nothing to be done but keep her warm and give her the poppy when the pain is bad." She looked at Cassia. "She has been asking for you since noon. She will not have anyone else in the room. Not me, not the children, not her son. She says she has something to say to you and that she will not say it to anyone else."

"Where is Corvinus?"

"In the garden. He has been walking up and down for an hour." Aemilia's mouth moved. "He is afraid. He has spent his whole life being afraid of that woman and now he is afraid of losing her, and he does not know what to do with either."

Cassia looked up the stairs. The corridor was lit with lamps, and the door at the end of it was open, and she could hear, faintly, the sound of a woman breathing.

"Does she know about the empress?" Cassia said.

"No one knows about the empress. You told me not to tell anyone, and I have not told anyone, not even my husband." Aemilia paused. "Did it work?"

"It worked."

Aemilia closed her eyes for a moment. When she opened them they were wet.

"Then go up," she said. "She has been waiting for you, and she is dying, and whatever there is between you, she is dying, and I have discovered in the last two days that I do not want to hate a dying woman. It is a waste of the little time I have left with her."

Cassia went up the stairs.

At the door of the room she stopped. The brazier was lit, and the lamps were low, and the old woman was propped up on her pillows with her eyes closed, and her face was the colour of the wax on the masks in the atrium below. The physician sat on a stool in the corner with his bag at his feet, and the slave who had been with her for thirty years sat by the bed with a cloth in her hand.

Cassia went in and stood at the foot of the bed.

Sulpicia Varia opened her eyes.

"You came," she said.

"I came."

"Good." The old woman's hand moved on the coverlet, a small, impatient movement. "Send them out. All of them. I have something to tell you, and I do not want it heard by a physician or a slave or my daughter-in-law, because they will all tell someone, and the someone will tell the city, and the city will tell the Senate, and by the Ides the whole of Rome will know what I did."

Cassia looked at the physician. He rose and went out without a word, and the slave followed, and the door closed, and the room was quiet except for the brazier and the old woman's breathing.

"Sit down," Sulpicia Varia said. "This will take a while."
