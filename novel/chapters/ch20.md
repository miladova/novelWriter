The old woman talked for two hours.

She talked slowly, in a voice that was barely above a whisper, and Cassia sat on the stool by the bed with her hands in her lap and listened, and the brazier ticked, and the lamps burned down, and outside the window the night went on over the Palatine.

She had begun to plan it in the spring. Not the poison — the poison had been in her mind for years, in the way that a woman who has been humiliated for twenty-six years keeps a thing in her mind and takes it out sometimes and looks at it. But the plan, the actual plan, had begun in the spring, when her husband had come back from Rome in a rage about a debt and had struck her across the face in front of a slave.

"He had done it before," she said. "He had done it a hundred times. But that time there was a slave in the room, and the slave looked at me, and I saw myself in her face. I saw what I looked like. And I thought: I am fifty-three years old and I have been married to this man for twenty-six years and he will do this to me until one of us dies, and it is not going to be him."

She had a physician who was in debt. She had a steward who was in debt. She had a cook who could be frightened. She had a knowledge of herbs that she had learned from her own mother, who had learned it from hers, and she had a garden at Tibur with a bed of poppies and a bed of hellebore and a corner where the monkshood grew.

She had not used the monkshood. She had used something slower and kinder and easier to explain: a little in his wine at dinner, a little more in the posset he took before bed, a third dose in the morning when he complained of a headache. His heart was bad. The physician had told him two years before that he must not excite himself, and he had ignored it, as he ignored everything that did not suit him.

"I did not know whether it would kill him," she said. "I knew that it might. I knew that if it did not, he would beat me within an inch of my life and I would have to try again. And I lay in my bed that night and listened to him breathing in the next room and I thought: please. Please let it be tonight." She closed her eyes. "And it was."

Cassia sat very still.

"Your mother," the old woman said. "Daphne. I have not thought about her in years, and now I cannot stop."

Cassia went very still.

"She was a good servant and a bad laundress and she sang when she worked," Sulpicia Varia said. "She had a temper and a laugh and she could not sew. She died in the winter of the year I was thirty-five, of a fever, in the room over the laundry, and I paid for the funeral and I kept you in the household because she had asked me to and because I do not like to be in debt to a dead woman." Her eyes were closed. "She asked me to look after you. She said, 'Domina, she is clever, she will be worth something to you.' I said I would. And I did, in my way. I taught you to read, I put you in my own rooms, I let you sit at the foot of my chair and listen to the poets." She paused. "And then I sold you to a brothel and told you your child was dead. I have thought about that too. I have spent twenty-four years thinking about the difference between what I promised a dying woman and what I did."

Cassia said nothing. She was thinking about lavender and a song about a boat and hands that were red and cracked and always gentle, and about the fact that the old woman in the bed had known her mother better than she did.

"Did she suffer?" she said.

"No. The fever took her in four days. She was not in pain at the end; she was delirious, and she talked about a boat." The old woman's mouth moved. "She called for you. She called for you for two days, and I sat with her, and I told her that you were asleep, and I sent for the physician twice, and there was nothing to be done."

The brazier ticked. The lamp burned. Cassia sat on the stool with her hands in her lap and did not move for a long time.

"Thank you," she said at last. "For sitting with her."

"It was the least I could do."

"Yes," Cassia said. "It was."

"And the cup you gave me?"

"Poppy and honey and a little wine, and three drops of bitter almond for the taste, the way a cook puts it in a cake. Nothing more than that. I wanted you asleep, and I wanted you quiet, and I wanted the birth to happen without a great deal of noise." The old woman's mouth moved. "It was not cruelty. It was the opposite. If you had been awake, you would have fought, and they would have held you down, and you would have remembered it for the rest of your life. I gave you the only mercy I had left to give."

"And then you took my son."

"And then I took your son." Sulpicia Varia opened her eyes. "I had planned that too. Not the child — the child was a complication. But when the midwife told me that you were in labour, I understood that the child was the answer to everything. A living child would have been a scandal and a weapon. A dead child was a grief. A child given to a potter in Rome was a debt that could be paid." She looked at Cassia. "I chose the potter myself. I sent the steward to the market to find a man with a good trade and no children and a wife who could not conceive. It took him three weeks. He found Gaius Vibidius, who was known in the trade as an honest man, and who had been to the temple of Juno Lucina six times in five years and had gone home every time with nothing."

"You chose him," Cassia said, "because he was kind."

"I chose him because he was kind," the old woman said. "I want you to know that. Whatever else I have done, I did not give your son to a cruel man. I chose the kindest man I could find, and I paid the dealer to forget the address, and I have spent twenty-four years paying him to keep his mouth shut, and I have prayed every day that the boy would have a good life." She stopped. "That is not an excuse. I know it is not an excuse. It is the truth."

Cassia looked at her. In the low light the old woman's face was the colour of ash, and her hands were like birds on the coverlet, and there was nothing left of the woman who had terrified a household for fifty years except the voice and the eyes.

"Why did you not tell me?" she said. "After he was dead. After you had done it. Why did you let me go to Puteoli believing that my son was dead, when you could have told me the truth and given me money and sent me to Rome?"

"Because I was afraid," Sulpicia Varia said. "Not of you. Of the world. If you had known, you would have come back, and you would have wanted the boy, and the boy would have been a scandal, and the scandal would have destroyed my son. I had just killed my husband to save this family. I was not going to let a slave girl destroy it the week after." She paused. "And because I did not think you would survive. I thought you would be dead within the year. I thought Puteoli would kill you, or a client would, or the pox, and that the boy would be safe and the secret would die with you." Her eyes moved over Cassia's face. "You did not die. You bought the house and you bought the business and you came back to Rome and you built a fortune, and I have watched you for sixteen years, and every year I have thought: she is going to find out. She is going to come for me. And you never did, and I began to think that the gods had decided to be merciful, and then my son stood for the Asian command, and the clerk in the palace decided that we were a liability, and it all came apart."

"The clerk," Cassia said. "Primus. He knew about the child?"

"He knew about the death. He did not know about the child. He knew that my husband had died in suspicious circumstances and that the physician had signed a certificate and that the steward had been paid. He has been holding it over me for twenty-four years, the way he holds everything over everyone. When he decided to destroy the family, he gave the information to Pactus, and Pactus gave it to a delator, and the delator found the midwife." She closed her eyes. "It was not the boy they wanted. It was the death. The boy was a gift they found on the way."

Cassia sat with that for a moment.

"Tell me what he looks like," the old woman said.

Cassia looked at her.

"Please," Sulpicia Varia said. "I have never seen him. I have thought about him for twenty-four years and I have never seen him. Tell me what he looks like."

Cassia was silent for a moment. Then she said, "He is tall. Taller than his father, I think, or he will be. He has the family nose and the family jaw, and he has his hair, which is the colour of dark honey, and he has a laugh that carries." She stopped. "He works with clay. His arms are grey with it to the elbow. He has a father who calls him in to supper and cuffs him on the head, and a shop on the Via Ostiensis with a kiln in the yard, and when I saw him he was carrying a board of cups on his shoulder and laughing at something in the doorway."

The old woman's eyes were closed. Two tears ran down her face and into the pillow.

"He is happy," she said.

"He is happy."

"Then it was worth it."

"No," Cassia said. "It was not worth it. But he is happy, and that is a thing that happened, and I am not going to pretend it did not." She stood up. "You do not get to decide that it was worth it. You do not get to make this a story in which you were right. You made a choice. It cost me twenty-four years. And it gave him a life. Both of those things are true, and you will have to die with both of them, and so will I."

"What do you want from me?" she said.

Sulpicia Varia opened her eyes. They were wet.

"I want you to tell him," she said. "The boy. When this is over, when he knows who you are, and who his father is, and what was done — I want you to tell him that his grandmother chose a kind man to raise him. I want you to tell him that she was a monster and that she was sorry, and that she thought about him every day for twenty-four years, and that she hoped he was happy." Her voice cracked. "I know you will not forgive me. I am not asking for that. I am asking you to carry one true thing about me to a boy I will never meet."

Cassia looked at her for a long time. The brazier ticked. The lamp guttered.

"I will tell him," she said. "I will tell him all of it. Not the version that makes you a saint. The version that makes you a woman who did something terrible and knew it, and who chose the kindest man she could find, and who paid for it for twenty-four years." She stood. "And I will tell him that you asked me to tell him you were sorry. Because you did ask. And because it is true."

The old woman closed her eyes. Her hand moved on the coverlet, and Cassia, after a moment, took it.

"Thank you," Sulpicia Varia said.

"Do not thank me. I am not doing it for you."

"I know," the old woman said. "But I am thanking you anyway. It is the last thing I have left to do."

---

She died at dawn, with the whole family in the room.

They had come in one by one in the last hour: Aemilia first, because she was the mistress of the house now and because she had decided that she would not be kept out; then Publius, who stood at the foot of the bed with his hands behind his back and his face white; then young Sulpicia, who had come from her husband's house in the middle of the night and who sat on the floor by the bed and held her grandmother's other hand; then Corvinus, who came in last and stood by the door and did not move.

The old woman did not speak again. She lay with her eyes closed and her breathing getting smaller and smaller, and the physician came and felt her pulse and shook his head and went out, and the slave woman lit the last lamp, and the sky outside the window went from grey to rose.

At the end she opened her eyes and looked around the room, at all of them, and her mouth moved, and Cassia, who was closest, leaned down and heard her say, "The masks. Polish the masks."

Then she died.

There was a silence in the room. Then Aemilia began to cry, quietly, with her hand over her mouth, and Publius went out, and young Sulpicia put her head down on the bed and sobbed, and Corvinus stood by the door with his face in his hands.

Cassia stood up. She looked at the dead woman for a moment, and then she went to the window and opened it, and the morning came in, and the city was waking below the hill, and somewhere a cart went past on the stones.

"The masks," she said. "Someone had better polish the masks."

---

The funeral was three days later, and it was the largest funeral on the Palatine that year.

The old woman had left instructions, and the instructions were precise. The procession was to go from the house to the tomb on the Via Appia, and the ancestors were to be represented by actors wearing the wax masks, and the actors were to be hired from the best troupe in the city, and the eulogy was to be delivered by her son and not by a hired orator, and there was to be no display of grief by the hired mourners beyond what was customary, because she had always thought the professional wailing was vulgar.

The whole of the Palatine came. The whole of the Senate came. The consul came, and the prefect, and a dozen men who had been her husband's friends and had not spoken to her in twenty years, and a hundred clients who owed their fortunes to the family, and a crowd of the poor who came for the bread that was distributed in her name.

Cassia stood at the back of the procession with Tertulla and watched the masks go by: the Sulpicii, four hundred years of them, carried on the shoulders of actors who had been paid to look like them, and behind them the bier with the old woman's body, and behind that the family in black.

Corvinus walked behind the bier with his son on one side and his daughter on the other, and his face was the face of a man who had been asked to carry something too heavy for him, and who was carrying it anyway.

"He is going to be all right," Tertulla said.

"How do you know?"

"Because he is still walking."

At the tomb, the eulogy was spoken. Corvinus spoke for a quarter of an hour, and it was a good speech, and it was, Cassia thought, entirely honest: he spoke about his mother's courage and her hardness and her devotion to the family, and he did not pretend that she had been kind, and he did not pretend that she had been easy, and at the end he said, "She did what she thought was necessary, and she paid for it, and she did not ask for anyone's forgiveness. I ask it for her, because she cannot."

There was a murmur of approval. The old men nodded. The women wept. The body was put in the tomb with the ashes of her husband and the ashes of four generations of Sulpicii, and the stone was sealed, and the family went home.

Pactus was there.

He stood at the edge of the crowd in a black cloak with his clients behind him, and he listened to the eulogy with his head bowed and his hands clasped, and when it was over he came forward and put a handful of earth on the tomb and said something to Corvinus that Cassia could not hear.

"What did he say?" she asked Tertulla.

"I could not hear it either. But look at his face."

Corvinus's face was white. He said something back, short and hard, and Pactus inclined his head and moved away, and the crowd closed around him.

"He is not here for the funeral," Tertulla said. "He is here to be seen at the funeral. There is a difference. A man who is seen at the funeral of his enemy's mother is a man who is saying to the city: I am not afraid of him, and I am not his enemy, and when I destroy him it will be the law that does it and not me."

"It is a good performance."

"It is a very good performance. He has been rehearsing it for a month." Tertulla took her arm. "Come. The family is going back to the house, and there will be food, and you are not invited, and we are going to your house to eat bread and cheese and drink too much wine and talk about something other than death."

They went. But before they did, Cassia looked back at the tomb, and at the masks on their stands, and at the crowd dispersing into the Via Appia, and she thought about an old woman who had spent her life keeping a house and a name and a family together, and who had died asking for the masks to be polished.

---

The will was read that evening in the tablinum, with the family and the freedmen and the chief clients present, and the lawyer read it in the flat, careful voice of a man who has read a great many wills and has learned not to be surprised by any of them.

The house and the estate went to Corvinus, with a life interest to Aemilia. The money in the strongbox was divided among the children and the grandchildren. The vineyard in Etruria, which was mortgaged to Sittius, was to be sold and the proceeds used to pay the debts, and if there was anything left, it was to go to the poor of the Palatine district.

The slaves of the household were to be freed. All of them. Every one, with a sum of money for each, and a letter of recommendation, and the freedom of any children they might have in the future.

There was a bequest to the midwife Psyche of Tibur, of a hundred thousand sesterces, "for services rendered and silence kept."

And there was a bequest to Antonia Cassia, freedwoman, of a small wooden chest, "which is in my room and which is to be given to her unopened."

The lawyer read it out and looked up. "That is the whole of the will," he said. "There is a codicil, which is sealed, and which is to be opened only if the legatee requests it."

"I request it," Cassia said.

The lawyer broke the seal. He read the codicil to himself first, and his face did something complicated, and then he read it aloud.

*To Antonia Cassia. I have watched you for sixteen years and I have never once spoken to you. You have done what I could not do, and you have done it without a husband and without a name, and I have envied you every day. The chest contains the jewellery of my marriage, which my husband gave me and which I have not worn since the year he died. It is worth a great deal. Sell it and give the money to the boy, and tell him it is from his grandmother, and tell him that she hopes he has a good life and that he does not have to be afraid of anything.*

*I am not asking for your forgiveness. I am asking you to be better than me. You will be. You have already been.*

The lawyer folded the codicil. The room was silent. Corvinus was looking at the table. Aemilia was looking at Cassia.

Aemilia came to her as the room was emptying.

"The jewellery," she said. "It is worth a great deal. It should have gone to my daughter."

"It was hers to give."

"It should have gone to my daughter," Aemilia said again, and her voice was not angry; it was tired. "I have spent nineteen years in that house, and I have kept it running while she sat in her chair and gave orders, and I have borne her cruelty and her coldness and her contempt, and at the end she left the only thing of value to a woman she met once in a garden." She looked at Cassia. "Do you understand what that means?"

"I understand that she was trying to make amends."

"To you."

"To the boy," Cassia said. "Not to me. She could not make amends to me. She knew that. This was the only amends she could make."

Aemilia was silent. Then she said, "Sell it. Give the money to the boy. I do not want it." She turned to go, and then stopped. "But Cassia. My daughter is fifteen years old and she has been married for a month to a man she does not love, and she has just watched her grandmother die, and she is going to hear the whole story in the Senate in twelve days. When this is over, she will need someone who is not her mother and not her father and not her husband. She will need you."

"I know."

"Then do not let her down."

Cassia stood.

"Where is the chest?" she said.

---

She took it home that night and opened it in the tablinum with the lamps lit and the household around her.

It was a chest of cedarwood with a bronze lock, and inside it was every piece of jewellery the old woman had ever owned: the gold necklace with the cameo of a woman's head, the emerald earrings, the bracelet of twisted gold, the rings, the brooch with the brooch that had pinned the bridal veil at four generations of weddings. It was worth a fortune. It was worth more than the house.

Cassia looked at it for a long time. Then she closed the lid and locked it and said, "Thais. Put it in the strongroom. We will sell it in the autumn, when the trial is over and the city has stopped talking, and the money will go to the boy." She paused. "And when he is ready to hear it, I will tell him that his grandmother left it to him, and that she hoped he would not be afraid of anything."

Thais took the chest. At the door she said, "Domina. The trial is in twelve days."

"I know."

"The whole city is talking about the funeral. They are saying that the old woman's death is a judgment on the family, and that the senator will not survive the trial, and that the whole house will fall." Thais paused. "And they are saying that you were in the palace last week, and that the empress received you, and that the emperor's secretary has been asking questions about the grain accounts."

Cassia looked up.

"Who is saying that?"

"Everyone," Thais said. "It is in the wine shops. It is in the baths. It is in the market. Someone is spreading it, and it is not us."

Cassia sat for a moment. Then she laughed — a short, hard, entirely unexpected laugh.

"Primus," she said. "He is trying to make me look like the emperor's woman. He is trying to make the Senate think that the trial is a palace plot, so that the senators will vote for his man out of spite." She stood. "It is a good move. It is exactly the move I would have made."

"What do we do?"

"We do nothing," Cassia said. "We let him spread it, and we let the city talk, and in twelve days we go into that chamber with the receipt and the statements and the arsonist and the midwife, and we tell the truth in front of the whole Senate." She looked at the locked chest in Thais's arms. "And we do not mention the empress once. Not a single time. Because a woman who is accused of being the emperor's creature is a woman who is finished in this city, but a woman who says nothing and produces the documents is a woman they have to listen to."

She went to bed.

---

On the ninth day after the old woman's death, four days before the trial, the freed slaves of the Palatine house came out to the tomb for the rites, and there were forty of them, and they stood in a rough half-circle in the road and said the words and poured the wine and went away again.

Cassia went out to them. She had not intended to; she had come to the tomb alone, early, before the family, with a jar of wine and a handful of flowers, and she found them already there, cooks and porters and a woman who had been the old woman's dresser for thirty years, and a boy of about fourteen who held the dresser's hand.

"You are the lady from the Esquiline," the dresser said. "The one who came to the house at the end."

"I am."

"She talked about you." The dresser looked at the tomb. "Not to us. She talked to the steward, and we heard it through the door, the way we always heard everything through that door. She said you were the only person in Rome who had never asked her for anything, and she did not know what to do with you."

Cassia looked at the flowers in her hand.

"She gave me a great deal," she said.

"She gave everyone a great deal. That was the trouble with her." The dresser turned. "We are free, then. All of us. She put it in the will, and the lawyer has read it, and my son is free and I am free and we have money for the first time in our lives." She paused. "And I do not know what to feel. I have been in that house since I was sixteen. I have hated her and I have loved her and I have washed her and fed her and lied for her, and now she is dead and I am free and I am standing in a road outside a tomb with a boy who does not know how to be free either."

"You will learn," Cassia said.

"How?"

"The way I did. Badly, and slowly, and with a great deal of help from people who had no reason to give it." Cassia put the flowers down. "Come to my house on the Esquiline. Not today. In a month, when the noise has died down. I will find you work if you want it and I will leave you alone if you do not, and there will be no debt."

The dresser looked at her for a long moment. Then she said, "Why?"

"Because someone did it for me once," Cassia said, "and I have been trying to pay it back for sixteen years, and I am nowhere near finished."

In the morning she began, very carefully, to prepare for the trial, and she did not come out of the tablinum for three days.

---

The dresser came to the house on the Esquiline in September, when the trial was over and the city had found other things to talk about, and Cassia gave her the linen room and a wage and a contract, and she stayed eleven years.
