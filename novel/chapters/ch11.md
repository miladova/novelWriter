The fire began a little after the third hour of the morning, in the storeroom behind the kitchen, where the oil jars were kept.

Cassia woke because the dog was barking. There was a dog in the alley behind the house that belonged to the bakery and barked at everything, so at first she did not move; she lay in the dark and listened to it and thought about the hearing and the box and the face of the man in the Senate. Then she smelled smoke, and she was out of bed and at the door before she had decided to move.

The corridor was full of it. Grey, thin, moving along the ceiling like water. She pulled her mantle over her mouth and went down the stairs, and the house was full of noise now: doors, voices, the crackle that is not like any other sound in the world.

"Fire!" Bathyllus's voice, from the service yard. "Bucket line! Domina — the storeroom!"

She got to the bottom of the stairs and the atrium was full of people. Thais with her hair down, carrying the strongbox. Sabina in her nightgown, standing still in the middle of the floor with her hands over her ears. The two kitchen boys dragging the water barrel. Celer in his apron, his face black with smoke, shouting at a slave who was trying to carry a chest out through the front door.

"Leave the chest!" Cassia said. "Get everyone into the street. Thais, count them. Bathyllus, the strongroom."

She went through the passage to the back of the house and the heat hit her like a wall. The storeroom door was open, and inside it was a furnace: the oil jars had gone, and the flames were running along the shelves, and the ceiling above was black and moving. The yard beyond was full of men passing buckets, and the water hissed and did nothing.

"The door," she said. "Shut the door. Cut off the air."

They shut it. The fire roared behind it. Bathyllus came through the smoke with the key to the strongroom and they went together into the tablinum, and the room was already hazy, and Cassia's hands were steady as she opened the panel and the lock and pulled out the strongbox and the black book and the wooden box.

"The linen," she said. "Get the linen out. And the wills. Leave the rest."

They got the strongbox and the book and the box and three bundles of documents out into the street, and by then the vigiles had come, too late as always, with their hooks and their buckets and their axes. The neighbours were in the street in their nightclothes. The baker was there with his sons. The flower seller. The old woman from the insula opposite. Somebody had brought a ladder. Somebody was shouting that the whole block would go.

It did not go. The fire had been confined to the storeroom and the passage behind the kitchen and part of the yard, and the vigiles pulled down the shed and soaked the wall, and by dawn it was out, and the house stood, blackened and stinking, with a hole in its side where the storeroom had been.

The fire had a sound that Cassia would remember for the rest of her life: not a roar, as she had expected, but a low, busy, eating noise, like a hundred people tearing paper. It filled the passage and the yard, and the smoke came off it in waves, and the water thrown on it went up in steam and did nothing.

She carried the strongbox out herself. It was heavier than she expected; she had never carried it before, and she staggered with it through the atrium and out into the street, and Hermeros took it from her and put it in the baker's cart, and she went back in.

"Domina!" Bathyllus, in the doorway, his face black. "You cannot—"

"The papers. The wills. There are three wills in that room and if they burn, three families lose everything." She pushed past him. "The linen can burn. The silver can burn. The wills cannot."

The tablinum was full of smoke. She went in with her mantle over her face and her eyes streaming, and she found the strongroom by touch, and the key would not go in the lock because her hands were shaking, and she stopped, and made herself breathe, and put the key in, and turned it.

The black book was on the shelf where she had left it. The wooden box was beside it. She took them both, and three rolls of documents, and a bag of coin, and she went out through the smoke with her arms full and her lungs burning, and in the atrium she fell against the impluvium and lay there coughing while the household shouted her name.

"Domina. Domina." Thais, with water. "Drink. Drink."

"I am all right." She was not all right. She sat on the edge of the impluvium with the black book in her lap and the wooden box under her arm and the smoke rolling over her head, and she laughed, once, and it turned into a cough that went on for a long time.

The neighbours came. The baker's wife brought a blanket and put it around her shoulders. The flower seller stood in the street with her hands clasped and her lips moving, praying to whatever god looked after houses. The old woman from the insula opposite, who had not spoken to Cassia in six years because of a dispute about a drain, came across the street with a jug of water and stood beside her without saying anything, which was, in its way, an apology.

By dawn the street was full of people and the house was a black shell with a hole in its side, and the sun came up over the Esquiline and made the wet ash shine.

"Domina," Bathyllus said, from the passage.

She went.

That was when they found the boy.

Nolus was eleven years old. He had come to the house two years before, from the slave market, and he had been given the job of feeding the pigeons and sweeping the yard, and he slept in the passage behind the kitchen because it was warm there. He had not come out when the others came out. They found him at the far end of the passage, by the door to the yard, which had been bolted on the outside.

Cassia stood in the street and looked at the small, black, curled thing that had been a boy, and she did not look away, and she did not cry, and she thought: someone bolted that door. Someone bolted that door from the outside, and the boy went to it, and he could not get out.

"Domina," Bathyllus said, at her shoulder. "You should not be here."

"Who bolted the door?"

He was silent.

"Who bolted the door, Bathyllus?"

"It was bolted when I got there. The bolt was hot. I could not touch it." His voice was flat. "The yard door is bolted every night. It is the rule. The boy should not have been in the passage. He was supposed to sleep in the kitchen with the others."

"Then someone put him in the passage."

"Domina—"

"Someone put him in the passage and bolted the door and set the fire in the storeroom, and they did it knowing that the boy would be there, and that the house would burn, and that everyone in it would be asleep." Cassia turned. "That is not an accident. That is a message."

***

The household was taken to Tertulla's house on the Caelian, all of them, in the grey light, with what they could carry. Cassia stayed. She stood in the blackened yard with the vigiles and the smell of wet ash and looked at the storeroom, and the vigiles' captain, a thick man named Pudens who had been in the service for twenty years, came and stood beside her and said, "It was oil. There was a lamp broken on the floor and there was oil poured on the shelves. The fire started in three places. That is not an accident."

"I know."

"Someone wanted this house burned."

"Yes."

"Who?"

"If I knew that," Cassia said, "I would not need you."

Pudens looked at her. He was a man who had spent his life dealing with the small disasters of the city, and he had the flat, patient face of a man who had learned not to be surprised. "I will write it down as an accident. That is what I will write, unless you pay me to write something else, and I do not think you will, because you are not a fool." He spat. "If I write arson, there will be an inquiry, and the inquiry will be run by a man who is friends with a man who wants you ruined, and you will spend the next six months in a courtroom instead of in this house. If I write accident, the insurance will pay, and you will bury your boy, and you will find out who did it yourself. Which is what you are going to do anyway."

"You are a philosopher, Pudens."

"I am a captain of the vigiles. It is the same thing, with worse hours." He turned to go. "Bury the boy today. Do not wait. This heat will turn him by tomorrow, and the whole street will smell of it, and then people will start asking questions that you do not want asked."

They buried Nolus that afternoon, in a grave outside the Porta Esquilina, in the ground where the poor were buried and the slaves were buried and the people no one claimed were buried. Cassia paid for a stone. It was a small stone, and it said his name and his age and the words *SERVUS FAMILIAE*, and she stood at the grave with the household around her and the smell of the fire still in her hair, and she thought about a boy in a passage with a bolted door, and about a cry in the night twenty-four years before.

Thais stood beside her. "It was not your fault."

"Yes it was."

"Domina—"

"I am the mistress of that house. A boy died in it because someone wanted to hurt me, and they knew that the way to hurt me was to burn what is mine." Cassia's voice was very quiet. "I have spent my life making sure that no one could touch me. I built walls and bought favours and made myself useful to men who could destroy me. And a boy who could not have been more than eleven years old is in the ground because a man with a lamp wanted to send me a message." She looked at the small stone. "That is the price of my house. I have been paying it for twenty-four years and I did not know it."

Tertulla came out to the Caelian in the afternoon and took charge of the household as though she had been born for it. She had the women put in the upstairs rooms and the men in the stables and the strongbox put in her own strongroom behind a curtain, and she had Celer brought into the kitchen and told to make something for twenty-three people, and Celer, who had been standing in the street in his apron for six hours without speaking, looked at her and said, "I burned the fish at the Saturnalia. I have never burned a fish in my life. I have been thinking about it all night, and I cannot remember how I did it."

"Celer," Tertulla said. "It was a fish. It does not matter."

"It matters," Celer said. "If I had not burned the fish, the mistress would not have sent me to the market on the second day, and I would have been in the storeroom when it happened, and I would have smelled the oil."

"You would have died."

"Perhaps," Celer said. "And the boy would not have been alone."

Tertulla took him by the shoulders and sat him down on a stool and gave him a cup of wine and stood over him until he drank it. Then she went out into the garden and found Cassia sitting on the bench by the rosemary with the wooden box in her lap.

"The boy is asking about you," she said. "Sabina. She has not spoken since the fire. She sat in the corner all morning with her hands over her ears. Thais says she will not eat."

"I will go to her."

"In a minute." Tertulla sat down beside her. "First tell me what you are going to do."

"About what?"

"About the fire. About the accusation. About the fact that a child is dead and a man paid for it." Tertulla's voice was steady and hard. "I have known you for six years. I have never seen you like this. You are sitting in a garden with a box in your lap and your house half burned and your face is the face of a woman who has already decided something and is waiting for the courage to do it."

Cassia looked at the box.

"He is alive," she said. "The midwife says he is alive. He is a potter's son on the Via Ostiensis, and he is twenty-four years old, and he does not know that I exist." She put her hand on the lid. "And someone has been asking about him. Someone who is not Felix and not Sittius and not Pactus. Someone who knew where to send men to Tibur to ask about a boy."

"Who?"

"I do not know. But there is a patron in the palace, and Pactus is frightened of him, and a man who is frightened does things he would not otherwise do." Cassia stood. "I am going to see my son."

Tertulla looked at her. "Now?"

"Now. Before someone else does."

"Cassia—"

"I am not going to speak to him. I am going to look at him. I am going to stand in the street and look at him and see what kind of man he is, and then I am going to come home and do what has to be done." She picked up the box. "I have waited twenty-four years. I can wait another month. But I cannot go another day without knowing what he looks like."

Sabina was in the small room upstairs, sitting on the bed with her knees drawn up, in a clean tunic that someone had put on her. She had not spoken since the fire. She looked up when Cassia came in and then looked down again.

Cassia sat on the bed beside her and did not touch her.

"When I was a girl," she said, "I saw a man killed in the street. He was a baker, and a cart went over him, and I was standing on the other side of the road with a basket of linen. I was nine years old. I could not speak for two days. My mother thought I had lost my wits." She looked at the wall. "I have thought about it for thirty-three years. Not every day. Not even every year. But sometimes, when I am tired, I see the cart and the man's arm and the bread rolling in the dust."

Sabina said nothing.

"There is no good way to see a thing like that," Cassia said. "There is no trick to it. It goes into you and it stays, and you carry it, and in time you find that you can carry it and do other things at the same time. That is all I can tell you. It does not get smaller. You get stronger."

The child's shoulders began to shake. Cassia put her arms around her and held her, and Sabina cried the way children cry, with her whole body, and Cassia held her and did not say anything else for a long time.

"Was it my fault?" Sabina said, at last.

"No."

"I was awake. I heard the dog. I did not get up."

"You are twelve years old. You could not have done anything. If you had got up, you would have been in the passage with Nolus." Cassia's voice was steady. "The fault is not yours. It is not Celer's, and it is not Thais's, and it is not the household's. It is mine, because I am the mistress of this house, and because I have enemies, and because I did not think they would go this far."

Sabina pulled back and looked at her. Her face was wet and her eyes were red and she was, in that moment, entirely a child and entirely a person.

"Are we going to be sold?" she said.

"No."

"Are you going to be killed?"

"No."

"How do you know?"

"Because I am going to make sure of it," Cassia said. "That is what I do. I make sure of things. It is not a kind of magic. It is arithmetic." She wiped the child's face with her thumb. "Now. You are going to eat something, and then you are going to sleep, and tomorrow you are going to help Thais with the linen, because there is a great deal of linen and it will not fold itself."

Sabina nodded. At the door she said, "Domina. Who is going to feed the pigeons?"

Cassia stood in the corridor for a moment with her hand on the doorframe.

"I will," she said. "Until we find someone else."

Tertulla rose. "I am coming with you."

"No."

"I am coming with you, and I am going to stand in the street beside you, and when you cannot speak I am going to hold your hand, and when you want to go in I am going to hold you back." She took Cassia's arm. "That is what friends are for. It is not a very exalted thing. It is mostly standing in streets."

She turned and walked back to the city.

***

Sittius the banker kept an office behind the Basilica Julia, in a narrow building with a green door and a step worn into a curve by the feet of two generations of debtors. Cassia went there in the afternoon, alone but for Bathyllus, and she was kept waiting for a quarter of an hour in a room with a bench and a jug of water and no window, and then shown into the office.

Gaius Sittius was sixty and neat and entirely without warmth. He had a face like a closed account book and hands that did not move when he talked, and he had been a banker for forty years and had never in that time been known to lose his temper, raise his voice, or forgive a debt.

"Domina," he said. "This is an unexpected pleasure."

"Is it?"

"It is unexpected." He gestured to a chair. "Sit. You have come about the fire."

"I have come about the fire."

"I am sorry for your loss. I heard about the boy. It is a terrible thing." He said it in the tone of a man reading a weather report. "I hope you do not think I had anything to do with it."

"I think you paid for it."

Sittius looked at her without expression. "That is a serious accusation."

"It is not an accusation. It is a statement of fact, and you know it, and I know it, and the only question is what I am going to do about it." Cassia sat down. "You hold the mortgage on the Sulpicius house and the vineyard in Etruria and a warehouse on the Tiber. If the senator is ruined, the estate comes to you at a fraction of its value. You have been buying his debts for years, quietly, through intermediaries. You paid a delator to assemble a case, and you paid a man to burn my house, and you did it because you are a man who has spent his life acquiring things and has never once considered what it costs other people to be acquired."

Sittius's hands did not move. His face did not change. But something behind his eyes shifted, the way a fish moves under ice.

"You have no proof," he said.

"I have a delator who will swear to the payment. I have a copy of the petition with your lawyer's name on it. I have the statement of a clerk in the annona office who can show that your brother-in-law has been cheating the grain supply for two years." Cassia leaned back. "And I have a house full of people who have just watched a child burn to death, and who will say anything I ask them to say, because they are frightened and because they are angry and because they loved him."

Sittius was silent for a long moment. Then he said, "What do you want?"

"I want you to withdraw the accusation."

"I cannot withdraw it. It is not mine. It was laid before the consul by a Roman knight."

"You can tell your lawyer to lose the papers. You can tell your witnesses to forget. You can tell Pactus that the case is over." Cassia leaned forward. "And in exchange, I will not destroy you. I will not go to the prefect of the grain supply. I will not go to the emperor's freedman with a file of documents that would put your brother-in-law in a mine and you in a small house in the country with a very long walk to the nearest town."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I will destroy you," Cassia said, "and I will do it slowly, and I will start with the people you love, and you will watch."

Sittius looked at her for a long moment. Then he smiled, and it was the first expression she had seen on his face, and it was worse than the blankness.

"You are a remarkable woman," he said. "I have been in this business for forty years and I have never met anyone who could do arithmetic and speak of love in the same breath." He stood. "I will consider your offer. It is a good offer. It is, in fact, the offer I would make in your position." He walked to the window. "But I did not burn your house, domina. I want you to understand that. I am a banker. Bankers do not burn houses. We foreclose on them, which is cheaper and legal and far more satisfying." He turned. "You have an enemy who is not me. And if I were you, I would find out who it is before you spend all your ammunition on a man who has no interest in killing you."

"Who?"

"I do not know. But I know that Pactus came to me a month ago with a proposal, and I know that he was more frightened than a man with a plan should be, and I know that he has a patron in the palace." Sittius smiled again. "That is the trouble with new men, domina. They always have a patron. And a patron is a man who can be blamed."

Cassia's hand tightened on the edge of the desk. "You are telling me that Pactus has a patron in the palace."

"I am telling you that Pactus has a patron, and that the patron has a reason to want the Sulpicii damaged, and that the reason is not the Asian command." Sittius's mouth moved. "The Sulpicii have a claim on a piece of land near the new forum. It was left to them by a man who died in the reign of Domitian, and the claim was contested, and the case has been in the courts for fifteen years. If the family is destroyed, the claim is worthless, and the land can be bought for a fraction of its value by a man who knows what it will be worth when the emperor extends the forum." He looked at her. "That man is not me. I am a banker. I lend money against land. I do not buy land that has a claim on it."

"Who buys land that has a claim on it?"

"A man who has been told that the claim will not survive the year." Sittius turned back to his window. "I have told you more than I should. I am telling you because I would like you to remember, when this is over, that I was useful to you, and that I did not burn your house."

Cassia rose. At the door she said, "If you did not burn my house, then who did?"

"I told you. I do not know." Sittius turned back to his window. "But I will tell you something for nothing, because you are the most interesting thing that has happened to me this year. The men who came to Tibur looking for your midwife were not mine. I sent one man, a clerk, to look at a ledger. He came back and told me that there were two other men there, and that they were not asking about a will. They were asking about a boy."

The room was very quiet. Cassia's hands were cold.

"What boy?"

"You know what boy." Sittius did not turn around. "If I were you, domina, I would go and see him before someone else does. And I would take money. A great deal of money. The kind of money that makes a man forget that he ever had another name."

She went out into the Forum with her heart going like a drum and the sun in her eyes and the smell of smoke still in her hair, and she stood on the steps of the basilica and looked across the crowded square, and for a moment the whole city seemed to her a machine made of appetite and debt and fear, grinding on and on, and she was inside it, and so was a boy on the Via Ostiensis who did not know that he had a mother.

***

They went out through the Porta Trigemina at dusk, in a plain litter, with Bathyllus walking ahead and Tertulla beside her, and the streets beyond the gate were the streets of the workshops: tanners and rope-makers and potters and men who worked in leather and men who worked in clay. The smell was of smoke and wet earth and the river. The lamps were being lit. A dog barked somewhere. A woman was singing in a yard.

The potter's shop was on the corner of the Via Ostiensis and a narrow lane, with a tiled roof and a wide door and a yard behind it where the kiln stood. There was a lamp burning in the window, and the door was open, and as they came past, a young man came out into the yard with a board of cups on his shoulder and set it down on the trestle.

He was tall and broad-shouldered and had his sleeves rolled up over his forearms, and his hands and his forearms and the front of his tunic were grey with clay. His hair was the colour of dark honey, and he had the family's nose and the family's jaw, and he moved with the unhurried competence of a man who has done the same work every day since he was a boy.

He turned to say something to someone inside the shop, and he laughed, and the laugh was a young man's laugh, surprised out of him, and it changed his face entirely.

Cassia stood in the street with her hand on Tertulla's arm and looked at her son.

She had imagined it a thousand times. She had imagined him dead, and she had imagined him as a baby, and she had imagined him as a young man in a senator's house with a senator's manners. She had not imagined this: a potter with clay on his arms, laughing at something in a doorway, in a street that smelled of the river.

She did not move. Tertulla's hand closed on hers.

"Cassia," she said, very quietly. "Do you want to go in?"

"No," Cassia said.

"Then we will stand here until you do."

They stood for a long time. The young man went back inside, and the lamp in the window moved, and someone inside the shop said something that made him laugh again. A small boy came out and chased a chicken around the yard. An older man came to the door and called the young man in to supper, and the young man said something over his shoulder, and the older man cuffed him lightly on the back of the head, and they went in together.

Cassia watched the door close. Then she turned away, and Tertulla turned with her, and they walked back up the Via Ostiensis in the dark, and Cassia did not say anything at all until they were through the gate and into the streets of the city again.

"He is happy," she said.

"Yes."

"He has a father who cuffs him on the head and calls him in to supper."

"Yes."

"I am glad," Cassia said, and then she stopped walking, and stood in the middle of the street with her hands over her face, and cried for the first time in twenty-four years, in the dark, in the street, with the noise of the city going on around her and Tertulla's arms around her, holding her up.
