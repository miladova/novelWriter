The wedding was set for the ninth day of June, and the whole of the Palatine knew it, and by the fifth of June the house of the Sulpicii had begun to look like a house under siege.

There were deliveries at every hour: baskets of flowers, bolts of cloth, jars of wine, a new couch for the bridal chamber, a chest of linen that had been ordered in March and arrived late. There were slaves in the atrium with ladders and garlands, and the steward in the tablinum with the accounts, and Aemilia in the garden with the cook, and Sulpicia Varia in her room, dying, giving orders.

The bride herself came to the House of the Graces on the morning of the seventh, before the sun was high, alone but for one slave, by the garden door.

Cassia was in the tablinum with the black book open and the accounts in front of her, and she looked up when Bathyllus brought the girl in and did not immediately understand who she was, because the girl was in a plain cloak with her hair tied back and no paint on her face, and she looked younger than fifteen and much more tired.

"Domina," Sulpicia said. "I have come to ask you for something."

"Sit down."

"I would rather stand."

"Then stand." Cassia closed the book. "What do you want?"

The girl's chin came up. "I am to be married in two days. My grandmother has arranged it, my father has agreed to it, my mother has told me that it is a good match and that I will learn to be happy. I have met the man twice. He is not stupid and he is not cruel and he has a great deal of money, and when he looks at me he sees a girl who will run his house and give him sons and make him look settled." She stopped. "I do not want to marry him."

"Then do not."

"You know that I cannot—"

"I know that you can." Cassia stood. "You are fifteen years old and the daughter of a consular and you have been told all your life that your marriage is a matter for your family. That is true. It is also true that women in this city have refused marriages before, and that the ones who refuse them pay a price, and that the price is sometimes worth paying." She came around the desk. "But you have not come here to ask me whether you should refuse. You have come here to ask me something else. What is it?"

The girl looked at her. Her eyes were her father's eyes, and they were frightened, and they were steady.

"How do I survive it?" she said.

Cassia looked at her for a long moment. Then she took her arm and led her to the garden, where the roses were in flower and the fountain was playing and the morning light was coming through the vines.

"You survive it by keeping something," Cassia said. "Not your freedom; you will not have that. Not your money; that will be his. Something else. A room in your house that is yours and no one else's. A friend you do not tell him about. A skill you learn that he does not know you have. A purse that no one knows about, hidden where he will not look, with enough in it to buy a passage out of the city if you ever need one." She looked at the girl. "I have known women who survived forty years of marriage on nothing but a purse and a friend. I have known women who had everything and survived nothing. The difference is not love. It is not beauty. It is the knowledge that there is one thing in the world that is yours."

"And if he beats me?"

"Then you come to me."

"And if he beats me and I cannot come to you?"

"Then you put a knife in your sleeve and you learn how to use it." Cassia said it without emphasis. "I am not going to tell you that it will not happen. I am going to tell you that you are not a child, and that you are not a fool, and that a woman who knows how to use a knife is a woman who is beaten less often than a woman who does not."

The girl looked at her. Then she laughed — a short, shocked, entirely genuine laugh — and put her hand over her mouth.

"My mother would faint," she said.

"Your mother is a better woman than I am and a worse strategist." Cassia reached into her sleeve and took out a small purse and put it into the girl's hand. "This is yours. It is not your dowry and it is not your father's money and it is no one's business but yours. There are two hundred gold pieces in it. Do not tell your husband. Do not tell your mother. Do not tell your brother, because your brother cannot keep a secret, and do not tell your grandmother, because your grandmother will take it off you and put it in the family strongbox where she thinks it belongs." She closed the girl's fingers around the purse. "And if you ever need to leave, there is a house on the Esquiline with a garden door, and the woman who lives in it does not judge."

Sulpicia looked at the purse in her hand. Her eyes were bright.

"Why are you doing this?" she said.

"Because a woman gave me nothing when I was fifteen," Cassia said, "and I have spent my whole life making up for it."

---

The bride's toilette began at the second hour in the small room off the garden, with the shutters open and the morning sun coming in, and it was, in its way, the most political hour of the whole wedding.

Aemilia directed it. She had been up since before dawn and she was dressed in the wine-coloured stola she had worn at the games, and she had the keys at her belt and the household lists in her head and a face that gave nothing away. The women were hers: the two aunts from the country, the cousin from Ostia, the old nurse who had come out of retirement for the day, the hairdresser from the Subura who had been paid four times her usual fee to keep her mouth shut about the shape of the bride's head.

The bride sat on a stool in the middle of the room in her tunic with her hair loose and her hands in her lap, and let them work.

There was a ritual to it, and the ritual had been old when Rome was young. The hair was divided into six locks with the point of a spear — a spear that was kept in the house for this purpose and had been used at every Sulpician wedding for two hundred years — and each lock was coiled and pinned, and the whole was bound with woollen fillets, and the orange veil was laid over it and pinned with a brooch that had come down through four generations. The bride's tunic was woven in the old way, on an upright loom, and her shoes were the same red as the veil, and her girdle was tied with the knot of Hercules, which the bridegroom was to untie, and which the women of the household had tied, that morning, with a great deal of laughing and a great many suggestions about what the bridegroom would do when he got to it.

Aemilia watched from the corner. She had done this herself, nineteen years before, in this same room, with a different set of women and a different brooch and a great deal of hope.

"Hold still," the hairdresser said, for the fourth time.

"I am holding still."

"You are not. You are breathing."

"People do."

"Not on their wedding day. On their wedding day they hold their breath and they faint, and then we have to start again." The hairdresser put a pin in with a good deal of force. "There. Now the veil."

The old nurse, who was ninety and had the voice of a woman who has never once been contradicted, came forward with the veil and laid it over the girl's head and said, "There. You look like your grandmother."

"Thank you," Sulpicia said.

"It was not a compliment," the old nurse said. "She was a difficult woman and she made everyone around her miserable, and you have her chin."

There was a silence. Then one of the aunts laughed, and then the whole room laughed, and the bride laughed too, and for a moment she was fifteen and not a marriage portion, and Aemilia, standing in the corner, felt something turn over in her chest.

When the others had gone to see about the flowers, she came and stood in front of her daughter.

"You will not have an easy time of it," she said. "The Proculi are not cruel people, but they are vain, and vanity is harder to live with than cruelty. You will be expected to be pretty and agreeable and to produce a son in the first year, and if you do not, they will be disappointed, and they will tell you so in a hundred small ways." She straightened the veil. "You will want to be liked. Do not be liked. Be necessary. A woman who is liked is a woman who can be replaced by someone prettier. A woman who is necessary cannot be replaced at all."

"Grandmother said the same thing."

"Your grandmother was right about a great many things and wrong about the only one that mattered." Aemilia took her daughter's hands. "She was right that the family is worth everything. She was wrong that a person is worth nothing. You are not nothing, Sulpicia. You are my daughter, and you are clever, and you are going to be a great deal more than a wife in a house on the Palatine." She kissed her forehead, under the veil. "Now. Do not cry. It will ruin the paint."

The girl did not cry. She looked at her mother with her father's eyes and her grandmother's chin, and she said, "Mother. Did you ever think of leaving?"

Aemilia looked at her for a long moment. The sun was high, and the house was full of the noise of the wedding, and somewhere in the garden the flute players were tuning up.

"Every day," she said. "And every day I found a reason not to." She let go of her daughter's hands. "That is the difference between you and me. You have a purse in your sleeve and you know how to use it. I had nothing but a reason not to, and I made it my whole life." She turned to the door. "Come. They are waiting."

The wedding was at the house of the Sulpicii, in the old atrium with the masks, and Cassia was not invited, and she went anyway.

She did not go in. She stood in the street outside with the crowd, in a plain cloak with her hair covered, and watched the guests arrive: the Proculi in their litters, the senators and their wives, the clients with their gifts, the flute players, the boys with the torches. The door of the house was hung with garlands and the whole street smelled of flowers and smoke.

Tertulla stood beside her. "We are going to be seen."

"Let them see."

"You said we were not to be seen together until the trial."

"I said I would not be seen with your husband," Cassia said. "You are not my husband. You are my friend, and I am standing in the street at a wedding, and there is nothing in the law against it."

Tertulla laughed. "You have an answer for everything."

"I have had a great deal of practice."

The bride came out of the house at noon, and the crowd pressed forward. Sulpicia was in the traditional dress — the white tunic, the orange veil, the elaborate arrangement of hair with the spear in it — and she looked small and stiff and very young, and she was holding her father's arm so tightly that Cassia could see the knuckles of her hand go white.

Corvinus walked her through the crowd to the house of Aulus Proculus, and Aemilia walked behind with the bridesmaids, and Publius carried the torch, and the flute players played the wedding song, and the crowd sang the bawdy verses that were traditional and that were, that year, more than usually obscene.

Cassia watched the girl go. When she passed, for a moment, their eyes met, and Sulpicia's chin came up, and her hand went to her sleeve — to the purse that was hidden there — and she nodded, once, very slightly, and went on.

"She will be all right," Tertulla said.

"I know."

"How do you know?"

"Because she has something in her sleeve," Cassia said, "and because she knows it."

The procession went up the hill through the narrow streets, and the crowd grew as it went, because a wedding procession in Rome is a thing that gathers people the way a fire gathers people, and by the time it reached the top of the Palatine there were perhaps three hundred of them.

The flute players went first, and then the torchbearers, and then the bride with her father, and then the women, and then the clients and the neighbours and the children and the dogs. The songs were the old songs, the ones that were sung at every wedding in the city, and they were obscene in the way that the old songs are obscene, and the crowd sang them with the particular delight of people who have been given permission to be rude in public.

Cassia watched from the edge of it. She watched the torchlight on the cobbles and the garlands on the doorposts and the faces in the crowd, and she thought about all the weddings she had seen and all the ones she had not been invited to, and about the fact that she had never had one.

She had been married once, in a way. Antonius Felix had freed her in his will and left her his name and his fortune, and there had been no ceremony, no veil, no songs, no torch. There had been a lawyer and a tablet and an old man in a chair, and he had put his hand on her head and said, "You are a free woman now. Do not waste it." And she had gone out into the street with the tablet in her hand and the sun in her eyes and discovered that freedom was a thing that had to be paid for every day.

Corvinus came past with his daughter on his arm, and he did not look at Cassia, and Cassia did not look at him, and the whole exchange took a fraction of a second and contained a lifetime.

Publius came next, carrying the torch, and he did look at her — a quick, frightened, determined look — and then he turned his face back to the front and walked on.

Behind the bride, Aemilia walked with the married women, and she looked straight ahead, and her face was composed, and when she passed Cassia, she said, without turning her head, in a voice that only Cassia could hear, "The purse. Thank you."

Cassia said nothing. The procession went on up the hill.

At the door of the bridegroom's house the procession stopped, and the bridegroom came out, and the girl was lifted over the threshold amid a great deal of shouting, and the door closed behind them, and the crowd began to disperse.

Cassia turned away.

"Domina." Bathyllus, at her elbow. "There is a slave from the Palatine. He says the old woman is asking for you."

---

The house on the Palatine was quiet in the way that a house is quiet when everyone in it is somewhere else. The atrium was empty. The masks looked out of their cupboards. The garlands from the wedding were still hanging, and the floor was still scattered with flowers, and the whole place smelled of crushed petals and wine.

Cassia went up the stairs alone. The door of the old woman's room was open, and the room was dark except for a single lamp, and Sulpicia Varia was lying propped up on her pillows with her eyes open and her hands folded.

"You did not go to the wedding," she said.

"I was in the street."

"Of course you were." The old woman's voice was a whisper now. "I have been listening to it from up here. The songs. The flute. The shouting. It sounds like a funeral, a little. The songs are the same, you know. The bawdy ones. At a funeral they sing the same ones, and everyone laughs, and then everyone cries." She turned her head on the pillow. "I have been waiting for you. I have something else to give you."

"I have the two statements."

"This is not a statement." Sulpicia Varia's hand moved under the pillow and came out with a letter, folded and sealed and brown with age. "This is a letter. It was written to my husband in the year of the consulship of Domitian and Silanus, by a man named Primus, who was then a clerk in the office of the treasury and is now the emperor's freedman, the a rationibus, the man who counts the emperor's money."

Cassia took the letter. The seal was broken. The hand was small and neat and careful.

"What does it say?"

"It says that a shipment of grain was diverted, and that the money from the sale was shared among three men, and that my husband's share was paid into the account of a banker on the Via Lata. It says that the clerk will keep his part of the bargain and expects my husband to keep his. It says that if anything happens to the clerk, he has taken precautions." The old woman's eyes moved to Cassia's face. "Does it say that? I cannot read anymore. My eyes are bad. It said that when I read it, twenty-four years ago."

Cassia read the letter. Then she read it again.

"Yes," she said. "That is what it says."

"Then you understand."

"I understand that your husband was a thief."

"My husband was a great many things. He was a thief and a bully and a man who could not bear to be crossed. But he was not the only one." Sulpicia Varia's voice was very quiet. "The clerk Primus is now one of the most powerful men in Rome. He controls the emperor's purse and the emperor's accounts and a great deal of the emperor's time. He has been paying me for twenty-four years not to publish this letter. And now my son is standing for the Asian command, and the clerk has decided that the Sulpicii have become a liability, and he has found a new man — your Pactus — who can destroy the family and get the letter at the same time."

Cassia sat down on the stool beside the bed. The letter was very light in her hand. It had been written twenty-four years ago by a clerk who was now one of the most powerful men in the empire, and it was worth more than the house and the vineyard and everything in them.

"The land near the forum," she said. "The claim. The case in the courts for fifteen years."

"The land is worth a fortune, and the claim is good, and the clerk wants it. He has wanted it for years. He thought he could buy it, and my husband would not sell, and then my husband died, and I would not sell, and then my son would not sell." The old woman closed her eyes. "We are a stubborn family, domina. It is the only thing we have that is worth anything."

"You should have burned this letter," Cassia said. "This letter is a death warrant. If Primus knows you have it, he will do anything to get it. He has already burned my house. He will kill you, and he will kill your son, and he will kill the boy on the Via Ostiensis, and he will do it from a desk in the palace without ever leaving his chair."

"I know."

"Then why have you kept it?"

"Because it is the only thing I have left that is worth dying for," Sulpicia Varia said. "I have spent twenty-four years keeping this house and this name and this family together. I have done things that I will answer for. And when they came for us, I had nothing to fight with except a letter and a dying woman's word." She looked at Cassia. "I am giving it to you. Not to my son. Not to the lawyer. To you. Because you are the only person I have ever met who understands what a thing is worth."

Cassia looked at the letter in her hand.

"What do you want in exchange?"

"Nothing."

"You always want something."

"Not this time." The old woman's eyes were wet. "This time I want to do one thing that is not for the family, and not for my son, and not for the house. I want to give you the weapon, and I want to see what you do with it." She turned her head away. "Now go. My granddaughter is being married tonight, and I am tired, and I would like to die in peace."

Cassia stood. At the door she said, "I have not forgiven you."

"I know."

"I will not forgive you."

"I know that too." Sulpicia Varia's voice was almost gone. "Go and save my son, domina. And when it is over, if you ever have a daughter, do not do what I did. Do not make her a thing that can be traded. Make her a woman who can trade."

---

The door of the bridegroom's house closed at last, and the crowd stood outside it for a while, singing and shouting and waiting for the bridegroom to come out and throw nuts to them, which he did, at last, laughing, with his hair tousled and his tunic already loosened, and the crowd scrambled for the nuts and the coins and the sweets, and then, slowly, began to disperse.

Cassia stayed until the street was almost empty. Then she walked down the hill alone, with Bathyllus behind her at a distance, and the city was quiet and warm and smelled of flowers and dust and the river.

At the corner of the Clivus Victoriae a man was waiting for her.

He was a small man in a plain tunic with the look of a palace clerk: neat hands, neat hair, a face that had been trained to be forgotten. He stepped out of a doorway as she passed and said, quietly, "The lady is to know that the letter is required."

Cassia stopped. Bathyllus came up behind her, fast, and the clerk did not move.

"Which letter?" Cassia said.

"The letter from the year of the consulship of Domitian and Silanus. The one the old woman has kept." The clerk's voice was polite and entirely without threat, and that was the most frightening thing about it. "My master is prepared to pay a great deal for it. He is also prepared to be patient. He is not prepared to be disobeyed."

"Who is your master?"

The clerk smiled. "The lady knows."

He went away down the hill, and Bathyllus started after him, and Cassia said, "No. Let him go."

"Domina—"

"He is a messenger. There is no point in killing a messenger." She stood in the street with the warm wind on her face and the letter in her sleeve. "But he is right about one thing. The letter is required. By him, by Pactus, by the consul, by the whole city." She began to walk again. "The only question is who gets it first."

She went back up the hill by the garden lane and in through the door that the steward had left unbolted for her, and the house was still full of noise, and she sat in the garden with the letter in her sleeve and waited for the wedding to end.

The wedding night went on until dawn, and the noise of it carried up the hill and into the streets, and Cassia sat in the garden of the Sulpicii house with Corvinus and did not go home.

The guests were gone. The torches had burned down. The slaves were clearing the atrium, and somewhere in the house the bride and groom were behind a closed door, and the whole city was asleep except for the two of them.

"Show me the letter," he said.

She gave it to him. He read it by the light of a single lamp, and his face did not change, and when he had finished he folded it and gave it back.

"Primus," he said. "I have known him for twenty years. He was at my father's funeral. He sent a wreath." He laughed, without any humour at all. "He has been in my house a hundred times. He has sat at my table. He has advised me on the Asian command."

"He has been managing you for twenty years."

"Yes."

"He is the man who is destroying you."

"Yes."

"And Pactus is his instrument, and Sittius is his money, and the fire was his message." Cassia looked at the letter. "And your mother has been holding this over his head for twenty-four years, and he has been paying her, and now he has decided that the Sulpicii are more trouble than they are worth."

Corvinus looked at the garden. The sky over the Palatine was beginning to go grey. Somewhere in the house a woman laughed, and a door closed.

"What do we do?" he said.

"We fight," Cassia said. "Not in the Senate. Not in the courts. In the palace. We take this letter to a man who can use it — not the prefect, not the consul, not any of the men Primus has bought. To the emperor's own secretary, or to the empress, or to a man in the guard who hates him." She folded the letter and put it in her sleeve. "We do not go to the trial with a family scandal. We go to the trial with the head of the man who has been robbing the emperor for twenty years, and we put it on the table in front of the whole Senate, and we watch them eat each other."

"And if it does not work?"

"Then we lose everything," Cassia said. "And we will have lost it honestly."

"Honestly." He turned the word over. "I have been a senator for twenty years. I do not think I know what it means anymore. Every speech I have made has been a calculation. Every vote has been a favour or a debt. Every dinner has been a negotiation. I have not said a true thing in public since I was a young man, and I am not sure I said one then." He looked at her. "You are the only person I have ever told the truth to. I told it to you in a vineyard when I was twenty, and I told it to you in a room above a kitchen when I was forty-five, and both times it cost me everything."

"It cost you nothing," Cassia said. "It cost you the girl. That is not the same."

"It is exactly the same."

She looked at him. The lamp was burning low, and the garden was grey, and somewhere in the house the bride and groom were asleep behind a closed door.

"There is something I have never asked you," she said.

"Ask."

"Why did you not come back for me? Not in the first year. In the second, or the third, or the tenth. You came back to Rome. You saw the villa sold. You knew my name. You could have asked. You could have sent a man to Puteoli. There were a hundred men in this city who could have found me for the price of a dinner." Her voice was steady. "Why did you not?"

He was silent for a long time. The birds were louder now, and the sky was going from grey to gold.

"Because I believed I had killed you," he said. "Not with a sword. With my silence. I believed that I had stood in a room with my father and said nothing while he told me what he was going to do, and that you had died because of it, and that the child had died because of it, and that a man who has done a thing like that does not go looking for the woman he has killed." He looked at her. "I was twenty years old, and then I was twenty-five, and then I was thirty, and by then the silence had become a kind of house that I lived in, and I did not know how to leave it."

"You could have tried."

"Yes."

"You could have asked one question."

"Yes."

"One question, Lucius. That is all it would have taken." Cassia's eyes were bright. "I spent four years in that house at Puteoli. Four years. And every night I lay awake and thought about the fact that you did not come, and I made myself hate you, because hating you was the only thing I had that was mine. And now you tell me that you were grieving. That you were silent. That you were twenty." She stood up. "I do not know what to do with that. I have been hating you for so long that I do not know what I am without it."

"You are still you," he said. "You are the woman who built the house."

"I am the woman who built the house because of you," Cassia said. "Because of you and your father and your mother and the whole cold machine of that family. I built it to be safe. I built it so that no one could ever sell me again. And now I am standing in your garden with a letter in my sleeve that could destroy a man in the palace, and I am not safe, and I have not been safe for a year, and I find —" She stopped.

"What?"

"I find that I do not mind," she said. "I find that I would rather be unsafe with you than safe in the house alone."

He stood up. They looked at each other across the lamp, and the sky was gold now, and the first cart went past in the street below, and the day of the trial was seventeen days away.

"Then we will be unsafe together," he said.

"That is the worst speech you have ever made."

"I know. I am out of practice."

She laughed, and then she was crying, and he put his arms around her, and they stood in the garden of the house on the Palatine while the sun came up over the city and the masks in the atrium looked out of their cupboards at nothing at all.
